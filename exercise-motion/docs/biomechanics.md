# How the muscle numbers are computed

`src/biomech.ts` turns an animated rep into how hard each of the app's 23 muscle groups works at each frame, and `src/muscles.ts` holds how strong each group is. This page says what the model does, where each rule and number comes from, how its output compares with measured muscle activity, and where it is still crude.

## The model

1. **Known forces.** Gravity on each body segment (Winter's segment masses and centres of mass), each segment's mass times its linear acceleration, and the loads a spec lists: a weight at an anchor, a cable pulling toward its pulley, or a push in a fixed direction. A bar held in both hands and hung from a cable is one rigid body: the cable pulls its eye, what each hand carries is solved with the muscles, and a cable shorter than its rest length is slack. Accelerations are low-passed near 1 Hz, with no lag. Rotational inertia is left out, which costs about 2 N·m against hip moments of 100 to 300 N·m at lifting tempo.
2. **Contact forces.** A floor, a footplate or a fixed handle pushes back with whatever balance needs, so a spec lists what the body touches (`support.contacts`), never how much each contact carries. Each contact point pushes inside a friction cone about its surface's normal and stops carrying as it lifts off. Whole-body balance of force and moment fixes what it can. Where balance leaves a choice (how hard the feet press sideways, where under a foot the pressure sits, how two hands share a bar) the lifter takes the one that costs the muscles least. A seat, bench or pad that carries the pelvis takes whatever is left over.
3. **Joint torques.** Each joint's torque is the moment of every force beyond it (inverse dynamics), read along that joint's anatomical axes.
4. **Muscle effort.** Each muscle group is a set of torque generators: its peak torque at each joint motion it produces, scaled by a strength curve against joint angle and by a force-velocity factor. At the shoulder the curve runs against how far the arm is raised, and the lats lose leverage as the arm goes behind the body. A muscle that is being stretched carries up to 1.4 times its isometric force for the same activation, so lowering a weight reads lower than lifting it. A head that crosses two joints (hamstrings, rectus femoris, gastrocnemius, the long heads of biceps and triceps, the pecs across the shoulder girdle) pulls on both at once. Activations between 0 and 1 are chosen to meet every joint's torque with the least total squared activation, together with the contact forces, as one bounded least-squares problem solved exactly. Its answer is unique and moves continuously with the pose, so neighbouring frames cannot disagree about which muscle works.
5. **Stops.** A straight elbow or knee rests on bone and ligament, so a load that would bend it backward takes no muscle within 3° of straight, fading out by 12°. A held weight can rest against a limb (`support.leans`), which pushes it away square to the limb and is pushed back as hard.
6. **Co-contraction.** Antagonists give back a tenth of the net torque at each joint. The level is set from the torque, not from a sign test, so it fades to nothing as a torque passes through zero.
7. **Stretch and tendons.** Each group's length is a linear function of the joint angles it crosses, read from 0 at standing length to 1 at the end of the joint's passive range. Passive tension starts at 60% stretch. Tendon load is active plus passive force, with 4.9% strain at peak isometric force.

Dynamometer norms come from untrained people, so the lower body is scaled up to a trained lifter by the measured ratio. The upper body is set by what a trained 80 kg man lifts where the untrained norms could not bench press, do a pull-up or curl like one. The force-velocity curve keeps Anderson's shape with its top speed doubled, which is what the speed at which trained lifters move a given share of their maximum implies. Strength curves are extended flat beyond the angles Anderson tested, because the fitted cosine falls to a third at a deep squat's knee angle and nobody is that weak there.

## How it compares with measured activity

`src/measured.ts` holds electromyography from the literature for each exercise that has been studied, and `./scripts/measured.ts` prints the model beside it. Measured activity is a share of a maximal isometric effort's electrical signal. It is not a share of strength, it passes 100% in dynamic lifts, and studies differ two- to four-fold on the same muscle in the same lift. So each muscle is placed in a tier (leads, clearly works, ticks over, quiet) and the model is held to the order between tiers and to how much less lowering costs than lifting, not to levels. Two muscles in the same tier are never ranked against each other, so the script also flags a muscle every study has above 35% that the model reads under 15%, which is a wider gap than studies have among themselves.

A group whose studies measured one head with a different job from the rest (adductor longus, the long head of the triceps) is shown and not scored.

Across the five exercises the model puts 82 of 86 pairs of muscles from different tiers in the measured order.

| Exercise | Order agrees | Where the model differs from the studies |
|---|---|---|
| Barbell back squat, 60 kg | 16 of 17 pairs | Obliques read 5% against 11 to 22% measured. Adductors read 39%, which nothing checks: only adductor longus has been measured (13 to 23%), not the adductor magnus that extends the hip. |
| 45° leg press, 100 kg | 4 of 5 | Glutes read 8% beside quads at 32%, where studies put them at about three quarters of the quads. See below. |
| Push-up | 42 of 44 | Lats read 2% against 11 to 14%, and abs sit level with the front delts. |
| Dumbbell Romanian deadlift, 12 kg a hand | 11 of 11 | Lats read 14% against 53% in the one study that measured them, on a 70 kg barbell. |
| Wide grip lat pulldown, 50 kg | 9 of 9 | Chest reads 15% and rear delts 10% against 64% and 48% in one study. Lower back reads 1% against 20%. |

Lowering reads at 0.6 to 0.75 of lifting for the prime movers, against 0.4 to 0.87 measured. A muscle the model holds still (the spine's) reads the same both ways, where the erectors measure 0.55 to 0.66 in a Romanian deadlift.

The leg press is the clearest miss. The feet's push on the plate is solved for least muscle effort, and the cheapest push runs 8 cm from the hip and 27 cm from the knee wherever the feet sit: moving the heels 16 cm up the plate changes neither joint's torque. The feet do it with friction, mostly by pressing about 85 N inward under each forefoot, which the plate carries from one foot to the other. Forcing the push square to the plate gives 49 N·m at the hip against 78 at the knee and puts the glutes at nine tenths of the quads, close to the studies. Charging for sideways force did not fix it at any weight that left the push-up alone. What is left to look at: glutes and adductors are equally strong in the model at a deeply bent hip, so they split whatever the hip gets; the hip and ankle both sit at the end of their range at the bottom; and people may not steer a push on a guided sled the way least effort says.

The pulldown's elbow is worth a note. With the bar at the upper chest, a vertical cable and the slight lean the instructions ask for, the elbows cannot reach the cable's line (the forearm and hand are longer than the upper arm), so the forearms tilt forward and the cable loads the elbow extensors at the bottom: 19 N·m, triceps 39%. Biceps peak at 43% in the middle of the pull and go quiet at the chest. The catalog lists biceps as a prime mover; the one study that measured them at mid-pull found 15% (Lehman et al., 2004), and the one that measured the triceps' long head found 60% (Signorile et al., 2002), though that head also pulls the arm down. How low the bar finishes decides this: 4 cm lower on the chest takes the elbow to 31 N·m and the triceps to 58%.

## Where it is still crude

- **Torque generators, not muscles.** Nothing wraps around bone, and a moment arm changes with joint angle only where a strength curve says so.
- **The shoulder is read on three axes fixed to the chest.** Its strength follows how far the arm is raised, but not which way it is turned: with the arm out to the side, turning it outward lands on the muscles that lift a hanging arm forward. Nothing above 120° of elevation or behind the body has been measured muscle by muscle, so those ends of the curves are extrapolated.
- **One lifter.** 1.78 m, 80 kg, trained. Activations are shares of his strength.
- **Abdominals read high.** A plank and a leaned-back pulldown put the abs at 40%, about twice the measured activity. The lumbar torque itself checks out against a simple beam, so the gap is in how the trunk's flexor strength was measured or in what else carries that torque.
- **Friction is a flat half of the normal push** for every shoe, palm and bar.
- **Poses decide the answer.** A limb held a hand's breadth off the line of its load moves the work to another muscle. `validateLoad` flags a muscle that out-works the ones the catalog lists, one that switches on more than once each way, and one that works hardest while the lifter rests at lockout.
