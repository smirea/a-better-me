## Setting up
1. Pick up a kettlebell or dumbbell and hold it right next to your chest with bent elbows, as your upper arms are slightly in front of your sides (i.e., in the goblet position).
    1. You can use either a kettlebell or a dumbbell, but some people find holding a kettlebell in the goblet position to feel more stable and comfortable, especially with a fairly heavy weight.
    2. Based on your preference and the load you use, you can transfer your kettlebell or dumbbell into the goblet position through a couple of different approaches.
    3. An effective approach is initially to pick up the weight by the handle through a deadlift motion and set it on an elevated surface (like a bench or plyometric box) with the handle facing up if you use a kettlebell or the handle directed vertically if using a dumbbell (as only one end of the dumbbell is on the surface). Then squat down until the weight is right in front of your chest, firmly grasp it in the goblet position, and squat up with the weight. If you use a kettlebell, grasp it by its horns (i.e., side handles). If you use a dumbbell, cup your hands around the top end of the weight with the base of your palms by the handle, and your fingers wrapped around the rest of that end of the weight.
    4. With a lighter kettlebell, you can tilt it onto its side with the weight base directed toward you, squat down to grasp its horns, stand upright with it, and curl it into the goblet position.
    5. With a lighter dumbbell, you can also pick it up with one hand and curl it up across your body to be in front of your chest. Then cup your free hand around the top end of the dumbbell with the base of its palm by the handle and fingers wrapped around the top end of the weight before repositioning your other hand to cup the other side of the weight in the same manner.
2. While holding the weight to your chest and looking straight ahead, stand upright with your feet near shoulder-width apart from each other as your toes are pointing somewhat out to the sides (rather than pointing straight ahead).
    1. While people may commonly find that setting up reasonably close to this stance allows them to squat effectively, the specific stance width and degree of toe flare that is ideal will vary among different individuals based on their hip structure and mobility.
    2. Ultimately, you want to position your feet in a manner that results in you feeling comfortable and stable when squatting, which typically requires trying out different stances over time and assessing how you feel with them.
    3. Some people may feel most comfortable squatting with their feet close to hip width apart as their toes are pointing straight ahead, while others will feel best with their feet outside of shoulder width with their toes rotated out to the sides at 45-degree angles.
    4. In the starting position, your neck should be near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright, and you will keep your neck in this position throughout your set.
    5. Continue holding the weight close to your chest in the goblet position throughout your set.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward as you tense these muscles like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward, leaving your abdominals relaxed, or simply sucking your belly button in.
    2. Strive to create tension throughout the entirety of your core to form a “360-degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
    3. Continue bracing your core throughout your set to help you maintain a rigid trunk position.
2. Initiate the descent by simultaneously breaking at your knees and hips. Progressively bend at your knees and hips to squat down in a controlled manner while keeping your knees aligned with your feet.
    1. As you descend, it is natural for your torso to lean forward mildly at your hips to maintain balance, but aim to keep your torso mostly upright, with your back flat and chest up.
    2. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the ground as your weight is evenly distributed throughout your feet (rather than being all on your heels or toes) during your set.
    3. As you bend your knees, it is normal for them to track forward over your feet (especially the deeper your squat) to help you maintain your balance.
    4. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot, as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet.
3. Continue to squat down through the available range of motion your mobility allows without rounding your back, lifting your heels off the ground, or letting your knees cave inward.
    1. People can often descend at least until their thighs are fairly close to being parallel to the ground, but what constitutes a full range of motion for a squat will vary among different individuals.
    2. If your back starts rounding, your heels lift off the ground, or your knees cave inward at the bottom of your squat, you may be squatting too deep relative to your current level of mobility and body control.
4. Reverse the movement by progressively standing up as you drive through the ground while extending (straightening) your knees and hips until you have returned to a fully upright position.
    1. Continue keeping your chest up, back flat, feet fully on the ground, knees stacked over your feet, and the weight held close to your chest as you squat up.
5. Alternate between steps 2-4 until you have completed your set.
    1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
    2. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before initiating the descent for the next rep.
## Description
While statically training your spinal extensors, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.
