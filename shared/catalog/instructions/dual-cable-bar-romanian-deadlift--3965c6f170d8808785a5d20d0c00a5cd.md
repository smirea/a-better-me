## Setting up
1. Adjust each pulley/arm of a functional trainer or motorized dual cable machine (e.g., Tonal) to its lowest height or whichever height is needed for you to hip hinge through your full range of motion.
    1. The ideal setting should be low enough for you to experience a meaningful stretching sensation in your hamstrings at the bottom of your Romanian deadlift. Simultaneously, it should not be so low that you have to round your lower back when initially picking the bar up.
    2. When using a Tonal, the lowest height setting or the second-to-lowest height setting is typically appropriate. Lifters with flexibility limitations or short arms relative to their leg length may be more likely to be able to train through their full range of motion with the second-to-lowest arm height.
    3. When using a functional trainer, you should use the lowest pulley setting and will likely have to set up a step as well during step 3.
    4. Note that the point where you start feeling resistance after connecting the bar will be a bit higher than the pulleys/arms rather than being fully level with them.
2. If your machine has arms that can be swiveled inward or outward to modify the width between the arms, adjust them to where the distance between the arm ends matches the length of your bar attachment.
3. If the lowest height of your machine’s pulleys/arms is too high for you to be able to perform a full range of motion Romanian deadlift, position a sufficiently sturdy step centered between the pulleys/arms for you to stand on.
    1. If it is rated to withstand the combined weight of your body and the machine weight you will use, an aerobic step elevated on however many risers you need to train through your full range of motion can be a quality option.
    2. Stackable deadlift blocks can also work well, and a thick bumper plate may alternatively be used, but it is not ideal due to its surface not being fully flat and its height limitations. Depending on your hip width, you may position two bumper plates directly next to each other if the soles of your feet do not fit fully onto one plate in a hip-width stance, since you do not want part of your feet hanging off the edge of what you will use as a step.
    3. How tall the elevated surface should be will vary among different individuals, so trial and error will be needed to determine if you selected a sufficiently high elevation. If the machine stops providing resistance before you reach the end range to which your hamstring can comfortably stretch during the exercise, a taller object will be warranted.
4. Connect each end of a long bar attachment to the cables and select your desired resistance.
5. In an approximately hip-width stance, stand centered with the middle of your feet under the bar.
    1. Since the bar can move a bit without receiving any tension from the machine, you can initially pick up the bar slightly off the ground to position your feet under it. During this maneuver, you should keep the bar low enough that the machine is not providing any resistance when setting up.
    2. Setting up with your toes pointing straight ahead is a good starting position, but you can point your toes slightly out to the sides if that feels more comfortable to you.
6. With your arms pointing straight down toward the ground, sit your hips backward while bending your knees until your hands are level with the bar. Keep your lower back flat and chest up rather than rounding your back.
    1. More motion should come from your hips than your knees as you descend, and your knees should not track far forward. However, you can let your knees shift mildly forward rather than maintaining completely vertical shins.
7. With straight elbows, firmly grasp the bar with a shoulder-width overhand grip, such that your palms face backward.
    1. As an alternative to the double overhand grip, you can use a mixed grip (with one palm facing forward and the other facing backward) or lifting straps with the double overhand grip if you wish.
    2. Your arms should be directed straight down such that they form approximately 90-degree angles with the bar.
    3. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
    4. If you position the bar closer to the middle of your palms (rather than at the base of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hands while you hold it, which pulls on your hand skin uncomfortably and increases the risk of tearing a callus.
8. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed, or simply sucking your belly button in.
    2. Continue bracing your core from this point on to maintain a rigid back position while using your lower body muscles to lift the weight.
9. While keeping your lower back flat and chest up, drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar before thrusting your hips forward and squeezing your glutes to stand upright with the bar.
## Exercise technique
1. While allowing your knees to bend mildly, sit your buttocks backward to hinge at your hips in a controlled manner as you keep your lower back flat, chest up, and core braced.
    1. Continue keeping your elbows straight with the bar just in front of your legs. Avoid letting the bar drift forward away from you, which may be more likely to occur if you think about “bending over” rather than “sitting back.”
    2. While you hinge at your hips, your torso will transition to an angle that is closer to horizontal as you lower the bar.
    3. Most of the motion should come from your hips, and your shins should remain vertical throughout your set, but do not keep your knees fully straight. You do not need to focus on bending your knees, since the appropriately mild degree of knee bend can naturally occur when you sit your buttocks backward without intentionally keeping your knees locked.
    4. As a cue, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks while your hands are carrying something.
    5. Throughout the set, keep your feet fully planted on the ground and your neck near a neutral position, as when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.
2. Continue hinging at your hips through the full range of motion in which you can comfortably move.
    1. Lifters with normal flexibility can typically lower the bar at least below their knees, but how deeply you can hinge without rounding your lower back or excessively bending your knees depends on your hamstring flexibility.
    2. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch and can no longer sit back any farther, you have reached your personal end range. Descending past this point would require you to begin rounding your back or bending your knees in a squat-like manner.
    3. While you do not need to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, you should clearly be in control of the entire descent.
3. Reverse the movement by thrusting your hips forward to lift your torso upright into the starting position.
    1. The lifting phase should simply be a reversal of what you just performed, so keep your lower back flat, chest up, core braced, feet fully on the ground, and the bar close to your legs.
    2. When you have finished lifting the bar, you should be able to draw a straight line from your shoulders through your hips to the middle of your feet when viewed from the side.
    3. Avoid leaning back at the top through excessive lower back arching that would pull your shoulders a little behind your hips. Squeezing your glutes at the end of the lifting phase can help you finish with your hips properly stacked under your shoulders, preventing excessive arching.
4. Alternate between steps 1-3 until you have completed your set and are ready to set the bar down onto the ground.
    1. You will be able to brace most effectively when you breathe between reps in the fully upright position, rather than during a rep. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before initiating the descent for the next rep.
    2. After finishing the last rep, set the bar down in the same manner in which you initially picked it up (e.g., keeping your lower back flat rather than simply rounding over at your lower back).
## Description
While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
