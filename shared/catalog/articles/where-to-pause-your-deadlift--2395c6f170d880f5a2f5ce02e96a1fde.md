Pause variations can be performed with the pause at any point in the lift’s range of motion, so where the pause is utilized can be individualized to address someone’s specific weakness or technical deficiencies. You can certainly try pausing at different points in the rep; pausing near the start of the lifting phase, when the working muscles are at their longest length, can generally be an effective approach. During the pause phase, muscles act isometrically (statically), without changing their length, in contrast to concentric shortening during the lifting phase or eccentric lengthening during the lowering phase. Compared with performing isometric exercise at shorter muscle lengths, training isometrically at longer lengths may contribute to greater muscle hypertrophy and improved strength across a wider portion of the lift. Isometric training at long muscle lengths can transfer to improved performance at short lengths, whereas isometric training at short lengths is comparatively less effective at improving strength at long muscle lengths.

Along with having greater direct transfer to strength throughout the range of motion, pausing near the start of the lifting phase, you can often improve positioning during subsequent parts of the lifting phase. If you begin lifting in a mechanically advantageous position, it may be easier to complete the rep than if you start with suboptimal positioning. Failing a deadlift near the top of the lift often results from suboptimal positioning during the first quarter of the lifting phase. In many instances, refining technique and improving strength during the first quarter of the lifting phase can allow someone to approach the end of the lift with more advantageous positioning, making it easier to complete successfully.

Increasing strength near the start of the lifting phase can also make it easier to complete the initial portion of the rep, which may build greater speed and reduce difficulty in subsequent parts of the lift. If you have greater momentum entering into a later portion of the rep, you can be less likely to fail than compared to if the bar was moving quite slowly into that point from having to grind to begin the lifting phase. To effectively transfer improved starting strength to increased bar speed, make sure you train the exercise without the pause as well, because training only the pause variation can neglect to optimize your technique when you're not pausing.
