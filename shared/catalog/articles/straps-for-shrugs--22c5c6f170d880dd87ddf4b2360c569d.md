Especially for individuals experienced with training shrug variations, fatigue commonly develops more rapidly in the gripping muscles of the forearm and hand than in the back muscles that move the shoulder girdle. If you find that your grip strength functions as the limiting factor as opposed to your back muscle strength when performing this exercise, you can utilize chalk or straps in an effort to amplify the stimulus each set provides your back muscles. As a simple approach, if you are unsure whether grip strength is limiting your performance, perform a set of as many quality reps as you can with a particular weight without using straps, and compare your rep total to a set using straps. To perform an appropriate comparison, ensure that you use the same technique across conditions, with the same range of motion and tempo. Additionally, you would want to perform each condition when you are similarly recovered. Practically, resting at least three minutes between the two sets should allow most fatigue to dissipate by the time you perform the second set, therefore enabling a useful comparison in terms of how performance of this exercise may be affected by your grip strength. However, if you really wish to optimize the validity of your self-assessment, you can compare your rep performance between these conditions on a second day, where you reverse the order of the conditions. If you performed the set without straps on the first day, perform the set with straps on a different day before the set without straps to control for any fatigue from the first set that may influence the second. When comparing how many reps you complete during each condition, you will be well informed as to how grip strength may be affecting performance of this exercise for you as an individual.

While assessing a shrug variation (that was performed unilaterally in a standing position for 3 reps with free weight resistance loaded to a 5-RM), Ekstrom et al. (2003) found EMG activity to be very high in the upper trapezius, moderate in the middle trapezius, and low in the lower trapezius. This very high upper trapezius activity during a shrug is congruent with the upper trapezius elevating the shoulder girdle by drawing the lateral aspect of the clavicle superomedially.

Moseley et al. (1992) also found the levator scapulae to exhibit very high peak EMG activity during a shrug variation (that was performed bilaterally for 10 reps in a seated position with “light weight” dumbbells using loads self-selected by participants), which is congruent with the ability of this muscle to elevate the shoulder girdle through scapular elevation. Moseley et al. (1992) additionally assessed EMG activity of all three trapezius regions during that shrug variation, along with 15 other exercises. For each muscle, they reported EMG findings from exercises they considered “optimal” based on the duration and magnitude of EMG activity, but they did not identify the shrug as “optimal” for any trapezius region. Correspondingly, Moseley et al. (1992) did not report specific EMG values of any trapezius region while performing shrugs, indicating that no trapezius region exhibited at least moderately high EMG activity for what was considered a meaningful duration of each shrug rep. The absence of high EMG activity in the upper trapezius may have been influenced by the participants performing a set of shrugs at a relative intensity insufficient to optimize agonist muscle peak activity, in contrast to Ekstrom et al. (2003), who explicitly assessed a set of shrugs with only 2 reps in reserve. If the shrug was performed with a load and/or rep-scheme that brought the set closer to volitional failure, Moseley et al. (1992) may have measured higher upper trapezius EMG activity.

While the anatomy of the upper trapezius and levator scapulae warrants their functional classification as shoulder girdle elevators, the limited research that has assessed changes in the size of these muscles after a training intervention does not indicate that shrugs are effective for inducing hypertrophy of these muscles. Conley et al. (1997) used MRI to measure the cross sectional area of the neck musculature at nine different sites (involving 13 total muscles, including the trapezius and levator scapulae) before and after recreationally active participants completed a 12-week resistance training intervention. Each week, the intervention involved four full body training sessions, with each exercise performed for 3-5 sets of 3-10 reps. The exercises included squats, push presses, bench presses, midthigh pulls (i.e., above the knee rack pulls), Romanian deadlifts, crunches, bent over rows, and shrugs. Following the intervention, the cross sectional area did not change at the sites corresponding to the upper trapezius and levator scapulae. Especially since the participants reportedly were not well trained lifters (who would most likely have lower rates of muscle growth than recreationally active individuals), one would expect the upper trapezius and levator scapulae to grow if they functioned as prime movers while shrugging. While not as directly as shrugs, midthigh pulls and Romanian deadlifts would also be expected to train muscles that function as shoulder girdle elevators in an isometric fashion.

When our anatomical understanding of muscles indicates that they are well suited to perform a function meaningfully resisted by an exercise, we cannot necessarily conclude from a single training study that that exercise is incapable of effectively training those muscles. This is especially the case when EMG research demonstrates high muscle activity during that exercise. While anecdotes are not at the level of empirical evidence derived from well controlled experiments, they still can function as a form of evidence while being recognized for their clear limitations. In isolation, countless lifters use shrug variations with the intent to train their upper trapezius and report feeling this muscle meaningfully working, while shrugging does not provide irrefutable evidence that shrugs indeed train the upper trapezius very effectively, but this phenomenon is not meaningless either. Ultimately, the findings from Conley et al. (1997) provide notable evidence against the claim that shrugs effectively train the upper trapezius and levator scapulae, but this single study does not preclude classifying these muscles as prime movers during shrug variations.
