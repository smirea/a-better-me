Forward and backward lunges are much more similar than different, as they both involve similar movement patterns that resist knee extension to train the quads, while resisting hip extension to train gluteus maximus and adductor magnus. While you can consciously strive to reach the same knee and hip positions during each variation, people may naturally tend to perform these two exercises with somewhat different knee and hip motions. Comfort et al. (2015) and Goulette et al. (2021) compared bodyweight forward and backward lunges and found that participants achieved a slightly greater angle of peak knee flexion and peak ankle dorsiflexion (resulting in a greater forward lean of the shin) during the forward lunge. The differences were significant for Goulette et al. (2021) but not for Comfort et al. (2015). While not significantly different, Comfort et al. (2015) also found reverse lunges to involve slightly greater peak hip flexion, and Goulette et al. (2021) found reverse lunges to involve significantly greater forward trunk lean despite participants being instructed to maintain an upright trunk position. When taking a step backward, people may naturally adopt a forward trunk lean to maintain balance during a reverse lunge, which may contribute to a slightly greater hip flexion angle during the descent, even without consciously striving to do so. This difference in average trunk position may help explain why Song and Woo (2021) found that the lumbar erector spinae exhibited significantly higher EMG activity during bodyweight backward lunges than during bodyweight forward lunges.

The available evidence also demonstrates that loading parameters may differ between the forward and backward lunge. Comfort et al. (2015) calculated that the resistive torque acting on the knee and hip was greater during forward lunges. Because resistive torque acting on the knee and hip is what your lower body muscles must oppose while lunging, this suggests that forward lunges may tend to be more challenging than backward lunges when performed for the same number of reps with the same resistance. If a similar relationship applies to weighted lunges, heavier loads may be required during reverse lunges to achieve the same level of resistive torque as during forward lunges. Goulette et al. (2021) additionally reported that the patellofemoral joint (of the knee) experienced significantly greater forces, stress, and loading rates during forward lunges, which also resulted in higher quad force. Along with the difference in resistive torque that Comfort et al. (2015) identified, these differences in knee loading may arise in part from greater momentum being present when the lead leg begins being loaded during the forward lunge compared to the backward lunge. With a backward lunge, you can start loading your lead leg with your entire sole flat on the ground the moment you lift your other foot up off the ground. In contrast, your center of mass has forward momentum by the time the heel of your lead leg contacts the ground during a forward lunge, which may increase the demands imposed on your lead leg. Of all the differences identified, the discrepancy in patellofemoral joint loading rate was the greatest, given that the loading rate of the forward lunge was 2.2 times that of the backward lunge. A higher loading rate and magnitude of forces applied to the knee do not necessarily indicate that forward lunges are problematic for the knee, but these factors may be relevant for lifters with pre-existing knee issues. While there is no reason to fear forward lunges, certain individuals may find them provoke knee discomfort. In contrast, backward lunges may feel better, particularly with a slower descent, and are more readily performed than forward lunges. Because they are quite similar to one another, if one lunge variation simply feels better to your body than the other, that can be a completely legitimate justification to train that variation.
