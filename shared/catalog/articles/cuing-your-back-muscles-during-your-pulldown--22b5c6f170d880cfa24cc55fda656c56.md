Sensations associated with training a muscle effectively (e.g., sensations of high tension, a burn, and/or a pump in a muscle) during a particular exercise are also not a precise means of determining how well an exercise trains a muscle. However, if you prioritize training your back muscles and do not feel that your back muscles are working as prominently as you believe they should during your lat pulldown, you can cue to pull through your back. Specifically, you can consciously focus on lowering your elbows toward your sides as a cue while performing the exercise, rather than on pulling the cable attachment you are gripping. To pull the cable downward, your elbows and shoulders will operate in a coordinated manner. Your back muscles act on your shoulders to pull the upper arms downward, and your arm muscles (i.e., biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis) act on your elbows to pull your forearms toward your upper arms. The elbows are pulled down toward the sides through shoulder adduction and/or extension performed by the back muscles, such as the lat and teres major, so consciously focusing on this aspect of the movement rather than simply thinking about pulling the cable downward may facilitate engagement of these back muscles.

Compared with receiving only basic lat pulldown technique instructions, Snyder and Leech (2009) found that untrained women demonstrated significantly greater EMG activity in the lat during a lat pulldown after receiving cuing intended to emphasize back muscle engagement. The researchers also found that the mean EMG activity of the biceps was similar across cuing conditions, whereas the mean EMG activity of the teres major was slightly higher with cuing; however, the difference relative to the no-cuing condition was not statistically significant. It is worth noting that Snyder and Leech (2009) specifically studied very low intensity lat pulldowns that were performed for 3-rep sets with loads corresponding to 30% of the participants’ max, and greater EMG activity does not necessarily mean greater muscle hypertrophy will occur after long-term training. Consequently, using certain cues may influence muscle activity during resistance training, but it is unclear how much effect cuing has on the effectiveness of muscle training during challenging sets. It remains unclear whether differences in muscle activity induced by cuing would be meaningful enough to influence long-term muscle growth. Regardless of cueing, the back and arm muscles work together during pulling movements, but different cues may alter the emphasis on how well certain muscles are trained.
