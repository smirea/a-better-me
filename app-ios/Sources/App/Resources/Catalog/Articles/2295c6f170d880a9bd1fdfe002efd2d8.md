Relative to when both feet are symmetrically in contact with the ground, the hip abductor muscles (e.g., gluteus medius, gluteus minimus, and the upper region of the gluteus maximus) serve a notably greater role in stabilizing the pelvis whenever the body bears weight predominantly through only one foot. When distributing load evenly through both feet, no resistive torque acts on the hips in the frontal (side-to-side) plane unless the load is asymmetric (e.g., holding a weight on only one side). If you perform movements such as a squat, the hip abductor muscles still resist the hip adduction torque generated by contraction of the hip adductors (e.g., the adductor magnus, which serves as a primary hip extensor). However, in this case, they only need to counteract the torque produced by your own muscles. During exercises such as lunges and step-up variations, the hip abductor muscles also help prevent the hip adductors from adducting the thigh, which would result in knee cave, but they serve an additional key function whenever most of your weight is borne through one leg.

In a single-leg stance position, gravity acting on your bodyweight (and any additional load) produces a meaningful magnitude of resistive torque at the stance hip toward adduction. During an open-chain hip action, when the foot is free to move, the thigh moves toward the body’s midline during hip adduction and away from the midline during hip abduction. With a closed-chain hip action, such as when the foot remains in contact with the ground, hip adduction would result in the top of the pelvis on the side of the free limb dropping. In contrast, the top of the pelvis on the side of the free limb hikes upward if closed-chain hip abduction is performed dynamically. During exercises such as single-leg squats, split squats, lunges, and step-up variations, the hip abductor muscles function isometrically, producing torque that maintains a level pelvis. If they fail to generate sufficient torque, pelvic drop will occur. With all other factors held constant, the degree to which the hip abductor muscles are challenged during a set increases as a progressively larger proportion of total weight is shifted onto one leg. Correspondingly, hip abductor demands are generally greater during single-leg squat variations (e.g., pistol or skater squats) than during lunges or split squats, which are themselves more demanding for the hip abductors than symmetrical squats. This relationship is supported by EMG studies showing greater gluteus medius activity during single-leg squats than during forward lunges or forward step-ups, which are often performed with assistance from the non-working leg. Rear-foot elevated split squats have also been shown to elicit greater gluteus medius EMG activity than back squats.
