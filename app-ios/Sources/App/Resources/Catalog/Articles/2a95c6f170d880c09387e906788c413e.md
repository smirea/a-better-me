Dumbbell biceps curls are often performed with the lifting phase of each rep beginning with a neutral grip and ending with an underhand grip, but it is also common to maintain an underhand grip throughout the rep. To transition from a neutral grip to an underhand grip, the forearm supinates, and the biceps function as one of the two primary supinators in the body (along with the aptly named supinator muscle). The fact that the biceps function both as an elbow flexor and a forearm supinator may prompt someone to believe that combining both actions into one exercise can train the biceps more effectively than if elbow flexion were the only motion occurring during the lifting phase of a curl. However, when you hold the middle of a dumbbell’s handle, the evenly distributed load fails to resist forearm supination, so the supination function is essentially not trained any more than if you performed the same movement without a weight. Because the two techniques most likely provide the same stimulus to the biceps, it is sensible to use whichever approach you personally prefer.

With a symmetrically loaded dumbbell weight, you can resist forearm supination during a biceps curl if you hold the handle where your index finger and thumb are pressed up against one end of the dumbbell. This results in an asymmetric distribution of force that simultaneously loads the biceps' supination and elbow flexion functions. Potentially, this technique may provide a different stimulus to certain regions of the biceps compared to if you curled in a way that only trained its elbow flexion function. However, this matter has not been directly studied, so any possible differences these curl techniques may have upon biceps development are completely speculative.
