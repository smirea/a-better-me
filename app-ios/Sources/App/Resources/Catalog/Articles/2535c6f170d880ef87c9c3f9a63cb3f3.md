The available evidence clearly supports the conclusion that leg pressing and squatting can effectively increase the size of the quads and gluteus maximus. This is unsurprising, given that a combination of hip and knee extension moves the load during both exercises. To date, no study has compared how a squat intervention compares with a leg press intervention in inducing increases in the size of specific muscles. However, Rossi et al. (2018) assigned untrained participants to one of the three groups performing a 10-week training intervention involving only back squats, only leg presses, or an equal combination of squats and leg presses.

After the intervention, no significant differences existed among the groups with respect to their increases in whole body fat-free mass. Because the entire body was assessed as a single measure, this finding does not necessarily indicate whether the groups experienced similar degrees of growth in any particular muscles. Before and after the intervention, the participants were also tested for their one-rep maxes in the back squat and leg press. The average back squat max increased by approximately 8%, 32%, and 20%, respectively, in the leg press group, squat group, and combination group. On the other hand, the average leg press max increased similarly for each group, with the leg press group and squat group both experiencing 34% increases, while the combination group experienced a 31% increase. It should be unsurprising that squat strength improvements were the greatest in the group training the highest volume of squats and the least in the group not training squats at all. Back squat performance can be strongly influenced by the magnitude of knee and hip extension torque a person can generate, but efficiently expressing that torque output during squatting requires practice of the motor skill of squatting. Especially in untrained individuals, improvements in a maximal squat after training squats can largely result from neural adaptations as individuals learn to squat in a coordinated, efficient manner. Exclusively leg pressing does not allow someone to practice the back squat, and it also does not meaningfully train the spinal extensor muscles that maintain a rigid trunk position during back squatting. Compared with back squatting, leg pressing is less technically demanding; therefore, practicing leg pressing has a smaller effect on leg press performance than practicing squatting has on squat performance.

The finding that the squat only group improved leg press strength to the same degree as the leg press only group indicates that the trunk extension demands present while back squatting did not impair the stimulus provided to lower body muscles. During leg pressing, the combination of knee and hip extension strength will likely be the limiting factor in performance. While lower body strength may also limit back squat performance, lifters may also be limited by isometric (static) trunk extension strength if they are unable to prevent their lumbar or thoracic spine from excessively flexing into a rounded position. If trunk extension strength is the limiting factor during back squatting, the lower body muscles may not be trained as effectively as when lower body strength is the limiting factor. However, this did not seem to be an issue for the squat only group, as demonstrated by its improvement in leg press strength equalling that of the leg press only group. While it may not be a focus of many lifters, it is also worth noting that the back squat group experienced a significantly greater increase in vertical jump performance than the leg press group, which is a finding in agreement with another study comparing these two exercises. Whether such an outcome would occur if the participants were also performing jumps as part of their training programs remains to be seen, but the joint angles and movement pattern of jumping more closely resemble that of back squatting than leg pressing.

In isolation, neither exercise is inherently superior to the other; however, the back squat and leg press are not completely interchangeable. If someone wishes to improve isometric trunk extension strength along with lower body strength and muscle size, training the back squat by itself would be more sensible than training only the leg press. However, training programs typically include a variety of exercises, so the additional spinal erector training that back squats provide may not be as notable in a program that already includes deadlifts and other hip hinge variations that meaningfully engage these muscles. With a certain volume of hip hinge exercises at a high relative intensity in one session, lifters may actually find that they are approaching the current limit to which their lower back can recover, but they may still benefit from further training the quads and gluteus maximus. In this instance, a leg press may be a more suitable exercise to incorporate into the session than the back squat, which may contribute to excessive lower back loading for someone whose lumbar spinal erectors are already fatigued from deadlifting.

Squats and leg presses are ultimately different tools available in your toolbox, and the value that either one provides will be dependent on what you specifically wish to accomplish from using it as part of your border training program.
