Lunge variations are commonly considered to be unilateral exercises, which have been defined by Fountaine (2018) to be exercises where “each limb works independently of the other to create the desired movement.” Unilateral exercises differ from bilateral exercises, where “both limbs are used in unison to contract the muscles, which create forces, and subsequently move a given load.” Exercises can clearly be bilateral when both limbs are symmetrically loaded as they simultaneously move in the same manner (such as during a barbell back squat) or unilateral when one limb is loaded as the other limb remains suspended in space (such as during a pistol squat). However, both legs function simultaneously against resistance in an asymmetrical manner to move your body (and any weights you may add) during lunge variations.
The front leg may be loaded to a greater extent than the rear leg, but the rear leg can still be meaningfully loaded during lunging, as demonstrated by Hefzy et al. (1997), who found the rear leg receiving about one quarter of the total load during a forward lunge. Hofmann et al. (2017) provided further evidence supporting the notable involvement of the rear leg when assessing forward lunges performed with a vertical trunk and vertical shin, a vertical trunk and inclined shin, or an inclined trunk and inclined shin. The researchers found that knee loading (measured as knee extensor impulse) was similar between the front and rear legs when lunging with the trunk and front shin both inclined forward, but the rear knee experienced meaningfully greater loading when using either technique with a vertical trunk position. In light of these data, Hofmann et al. (2017) considered lunges to be bilateral exercises, along with split squats, which Schultz et al. (2014) also reported imparted greater loading to the rear knee than to the front knee while simultaneously transferring more weight to the front leg. The specific technique used to perform a lunge can affect the distribution of load between the two sides. Besides modifying the trunk angle, people may intentionally be able to shift almost all of their weight into the lead leg while using the rear leg lightly for balance assistance, or they may be able to focus on pushing off forcibly through the rear leg to perform much of the ascent through work from the rear leg. The ability to lunge with variable techniques can further complicate the classification of lunges as unilateral or bilateral. What someone considers a unilateral or bilateral exercise can also be notably influenced by how the terms are operationally defined, and there can be legitimate reasons to classify lunges as either unilateral or bilateral. Given this lack of clarity, this database seeks a middle ground by classifying lunge variations simply as asymmetrical exercises, since both legs are loaded asymmetrically.
