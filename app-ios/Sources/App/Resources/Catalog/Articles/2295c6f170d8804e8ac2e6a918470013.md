*Key takeaway: Squats can be excellent for developing the gluteus maximus, but they are unlikely to train the gluteus medius or gluteus minimus meaningfully.*

Squats have the reputation of being a high-quality glute exercise, and that certainly is the case for the gluteus maximus when squatting sufficiently deep. Hip extension strength is vital for squat performance, and the prominent contribution of the gluteus maximus to hip extension explains why multiple studies have found that this muscle grows meaningfully with back squat training interventions. However, the gluteus maximus is not your only gluteal muscle. The gluteus medius and gluteus minimus constitute a notable proportion of the musculature on the side of your hip, and they together reflect a third of total glute muscle volume based on data from Handsfield et al. (2014).

The gluteus medius and gluteus minimus primarily function as hip abductors (bringing the thigh out to the side) when you are in an upright position or in a low angle of hip flexion, like during the initial part of a squat’s descent. In these positions, contraction of the two gluteal muscles can help prevent the knees from caving inward, which results from hip adduction and internal rotation. While these glutes act in a stabilizing role to prevent other muscles from adducting the hip, squats do not provide external resistance that pulls the hip toward adduction unless a band is looped around your thighs. The resistance from squatting is directed downward, which pulls the hip toward a flexed position, and only a portion of the gluteus medius can assist in extending the hips at certain joint angles. Once the hip is flexed by about 90 degrees of greater (like at the bottom of a half squat), the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus lose most of their leverage to function as hip abductors, while they develop the greatest leverage to act as hip internal rotators. Forceful contraction of these muscles can consequently make it more difficult to prevent the knees from caving inward, so highly activating them may not be particularly beneficial at the bottom of a squat.

Based on biomechanical modeling research on the functions of the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, it is reasonable to anticipate that squats would not effectively train these muscles. That is indeed what Plotkin et al. (2023) found after participants trained deep back squats for nine weeks, which prompted the gluteus maximus to grow significantly, but the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus essentially remained the same size on average. In light of these findings, you can be confident in the ability of sufficiently deep squats to induce notable glute growth since the gluteus maximus is the largest part of the glutes. However, it is worth recognizing that other exercises directly training hip abduction will likely be required if you want to train your gluteus medius and gluteus minimus effectively.
