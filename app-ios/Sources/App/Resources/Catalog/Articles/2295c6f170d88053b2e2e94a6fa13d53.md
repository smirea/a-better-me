When prioritizing high-quality technique that emphasizes a very controlled descent, level hip bones, and the working leg’s knee stacked over the foot, a fairly low step can be sufficiently challenging for most people. As you become more proficient with this exercise over time, you can incrementally increase the height of your step to increase the intensity of the exercise, but refrain from selecting a height that prompts your technique to degrade. There are a few common signs that may appear at the end of the step-down’s lowering phase, indicating that a particular step may be too high for someone’s current ability. These signs include the hip bone of the free leg dropping down or the knee of the working leg caving inward shortly before the heel reaches the ground. Additionally, people may begin descending slowly and in a controlled manner, then abruptly accelerate at the end of the descent due to an inability to control the most difficult end range. This technique fault is commonly accompanied by forcefully contacting the ground, followed by pushing off the ground with the free leg to make the ascent easier, rather than lifting the body exclusively with the elevated leg.
