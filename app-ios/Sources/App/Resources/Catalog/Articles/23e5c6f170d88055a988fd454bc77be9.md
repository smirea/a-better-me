The available evidence clearly indicates that horizontal pressing exercises can induce significant triceps growth, consistent with biomechanical and EMG data predictions. Across studies that measured changes in triceps and pecs major size, it is also clear that bench pressing typically results in greater pecs major growth than triceps growth on average. At least seven different studies have measured changes in the size of the pec major and the whole triceps after a bench press intervention, and each one found the pec major to grow by a greater percentage than the triceps. When pooling all data, the pec major showed approximately twice the magnitude of average growth relative to the triceps after bench press training interventions. Most studies measuring changes in triceps size have examined the triceps as a single muscle group rather than the three individual muscles. However, the specific response of the long head of the triceps almost certainly contributes to why the triceps as a whole does not grow as much from bench pressing as the pec major.

All three heads of the triceps function as primary elbow extensors, and elbow extension is the only function of the medial and lateral heads, which originate from the humerus and exclusively cross the elbow joint. In contrast, the long head originates from the scapula (shoulder blade) and crosses the shoulder joint. Although its shoulder function is less well studied than that of other muscles, the available evidence indicates that it contributes to shoulder extension and adduction, owing to its origin on the scapula. During the lifting phase of most bench press variations, the shoulder moves through a combination of flexion in the sagittal plane and horizontal flexion in the transverse plane. When the long head of the triceps contracts, it cannot selectively function at the elbow without simultaneously imparting a pulling force on the shoulder joint. Consequently, when bench pressing, the desirable elbow extension torque produced by the long head is coupled with undesirable shoulder extension torque that directly opposes the front delt and upper pec major. On the other hand, contraction of the medial and lateral heads of the triceps is exclusively beneficial to bench press performance. It can correspondingly be more efficient for the nervous system to recruit these two heads to a preferentially greater degree than the long head during an exercise that resists both shoulder flexion and elbow extension. When performing a challenging set of bench pressing, the long head of the triceps may still be effectively trained if it is highly activated due to its contraction producing greater extension torque at the elbow than the shoulder.

Brandão et al. (2020) provided insight into whether this would occur by measuring changes in the size of each head of the triceps and the pec major after 43 untrained participants completed a 10-week training intervention with two sessions per week. One group exclusively performed a flat wide grip barbell bench press, another group exclusively performed a barbell skullcrusher, while two other groups performed a combination of the two exercises in different orders. After the intervention, the bench press-only group experienced an insignificant 2% average increase in long head size, whereas each of the three other groups experienced significant increases in long head size ranging from 14-18%. The medial head also increased in size by 14-16% for the three groups, including the skullcrusher, but it only grew by an average of 7% in the bench press-only group, which was not a statistically significant change. However, the lateral head grew similarly well by about 7% in the three groups, including the bench press, but it failed to grow at all in the skull crusher-only group. For comparison, the pec major increased in size by 9% in the bench press-only group, which exceeded the 4.8% increase in total triceps muscle size.

Wanttaja (2020) also measured changes in the size of the long head and lateral head of the triceps after 17 men with at least one year of upper body lifting experience completed a 10-week flat Smith machine bench press intervention. Using specialized equipment, one group exclusively trained the concentric (lifting) phase of the bench press, while the other exclusively trained the eccentric (lowering) phase. After the intervention, the long head increased in size by 2% and 5%, respectively, in the concentric and eccentric training groups. The lateral head additionally increased in size by 4% and 14%, respectively, in the concentric and eccentric training groups. Outside a laboratory, you will be training both the concentric and eccentric phases of a bench press, and this conventional style of training has been reported by other studies to produce similar magnitudes of muscle growth as eccentric only training. Wakahara et al. (2013) also found that a 12-week flat dumbbell bench press training intervention increased total triceps muscle volume without significantly increasing the thickness of the long head.

Although they trained different bench press variations, the results from the three aforementioned studies indicate that the long head of the triceps is unlikely to be trained as effectively as the other triceps heads during bench pressing. Other biarticular muscles have been found not to grow significantly from multi-joint exercises that oppose one of their functions at a joint while training another function at a different joint.

For instance, the rectus femoris is the one biarticular quad muscle that functions as a hip flexor in addition to a knee extensor, whereas the three other quad muscles function only as knee extensors. Squats, which resist both knee extension and hip extension, effectively develop the three quad muscles that exclusively function as knee extensors. However, most studies that have measured rectus femoris size before and after a back squat intervention have found no increase in size; these studies include all studies of participants with resistance training experience. Just as the elbow extension torque from the long head of the triceps is beneficial to bench press performance, so too is the knee extension torque from the rectus femoris beneficial to squat performance. However, just as the shoulder extension torque from the long head of the triceps is deleterious to bench press performance, so too is the hip flexion torque from the rectus femoris deleterious to squat performance. Correspondingly, engagement of the monoarticular heads of the triceps is more efficient than the long head when bench pressing, just like engagement of the monoarticular quads is more efficient than the rectus femoris. Both of these biarticular muscles can be developed more effectively with a single joint exercise, such as a machine leg extension for the rectus femoris and a lying skullcrusher for the long head of the triceps.

In addition to Brandão et al. (2020), two other studies have measured changes in the size of the long head of the triceps after participants performed an intervention consisting exclusively of a single joint elbow extension exercise. Both of those studies assessed one group of participants training cable triceps pushdowns and the other group training overhead cable triceps extensions. The two different groups experienced significant growth in the long head of the triceps in both studies, providing further evidence that the long head is highly likely to be trained most effectively by single joint elbow extension exercises. During this type of exercise, the shoulder extension torque that contraction of the long head generates is inconsequential to performance, in contrast to bench press variations.

While bench pressing can contribute to meaningful triceps development, it simply cannot replace single joint elbow extension exercises if you wish to optimize total triceps development. The poor stimulus of bench pressing may be particularly noteworthy for the long head of the triceps, which is the largest of the three triceps heads, accounting for 44% of total triceps volume. Consequently, you cannot optimize your total arm muscularity without including a triceps “isolation” exercise that can robustly train the long head to a considerably greater degree than what bench pressing provides.
