With many squat variations, such as bodyweight squats and barbell back squats, you would fall backward if you tried to maintain a completely vertical torso, so you must lean your torso forward by flexing your hips to maintain balance. When the load is situated in front of your body, such as during a front squat or a goblet squat, you can adopt a relatively more upright torso position, but some degree of forward trunk lean will still be normally required to maintain your balance. On the other hand, Spanish squats allow you to maintain a completely vertical torso, shifting your center of mass closer to your hips and farther from your knees. This correspondingly results in Spanish squats involving a greater lever arm of resistance acting on the knees and a lesser lever arm acting on the hips compared to a free squat where your torso naturally inclines to maintain your balance. Consequently, Spanish squats can be significantly more quad-dominant than free squats, which may train hip extensor muscles such as the gluteus maximus more effectively than Spanish squats. In addition to biasing the quads to a greater degree than the hip extensor muscles, the longer lever arm acting on the knee during a Spanish squat makes this exercise notably more challenging than a standard bodyweight squat on a per-rep basis. If you opt to perform a weighted variation, you will similarly find that not nearly as much additional weight is needed to match the difficulty of a weighted free squat.
