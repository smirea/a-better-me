Relative to conventional deadlifts or block pulls with a straight barbell, trap bar deadlifts can allow you greater liberty in how you perform them with respect to biasing more of a hip hinge or squat movement pattern. A conventional deadlift keeps the load in front of you and constrains forward knee travel, while a trap bar deadlift positions the load closer to your center of mass and permits pushing your knees farther forward than you would during a conventional deadlift. Trap bar deadlifting with greater forward knee travel makes the movement more squat-like, whereas maintaining a more vertical shin position results in a more hip-hinge-dominant movement.

Squat and deadlift variations can be analyzed as existing along a “squat-hinge continuum” based on the demands they impart to the hip extensor muscles (e.g., gluteus maximus, hamstrings, adductor magnus) relative to the quads, which extend the knees. Compared with trap bar deadlifting with the shins vertical, the lever arm of the resistance acting on the knees is greater when the knees are positioned farther forward, resulting in a greater ratio of loading imparted to the quads relative to the hip extensor muscles. In contrast, the lever arm of the resistance acting on the hips is greater when deadlifting with vertical shins, which prompts you to sit your hips farther back and experience a lower ratio of loading imparted to the quads relative to the hip extensor muscles. While people tend to perform trap bar deadlifts with their knees situated farther forward than when they conventional deadlift, you certainly have the ability to trap bar deadlift with the same knee position you would use for conventional deadlifts. Correspondingly, you can trap bar deadlift with whichever technique you prefer in congruence with your training goals.

Based on research assessing participants' self-selected technique for trap bar deadlifting (likely the technique in which they were most efficient at pulling), trap bar deadlifts have been found to be more hip-dominant than knee-dominant. Swinton et al. (2011) specifically assessed powerlifters performing low handle trap bar deadlifts and conventional deadlifts. Both exercises imparted meaningfully greater loading to the hip extensors and lumbar spinal extensors than the quads, but conventional deadlifts were relatively more hip dominant. When lifting 80% of the participants’ conventional deadlift one-rep maxes, the ratio of peak resistive torque acting on the hip to the peak resistive torque acting on the knee was 3.7 for conventional deadlifts and 1.8 for trap bar deadlifts. This difference was primarily attributable to the trap bar deadlift, which produced a peak resistive torque at the knee that was 190% of that in the conventional deadlift. A smaller gap was present for hip extensor loading, as the peak resistive torque acting on the hip for the trap bar deadlift was 92% that of the conventional deadlift. These findings indicate that the degree to which trap bar deadlifts train the quads more than conventional deadlifts most likely exceeds the degree to which conventional deadlifts train the hip extensor muscles more than trap bar deadlifts. In terms of which hip extensor muscles may be less involved during trap bar deadlifts, the biarticular hamstrings in particular will likely receive a lesser stimulus. Camara et al. (2016) and Andersen et al. (2018) both found the hamstring’s biceps femoris to exhibit lower EMG activity during the low handle trap bar deadlift compared to the conventional deadlift, which is congruent with what would be anticipated in light of the mechanical differences between these two exercises. Since the trap bar deadlift imparts greater loading to the knee, the knee flexion torque that results from hamstring contraction will have more of a deleterious effect on performance than during the conventional deadlift. While the hip extension torque that the hamstrings produce is beneficial, their knee flexion torque will oppose the quads as they extend the knee, so the hamstrings are not the most efficient hip extensor muscles for the nervous system to recruit during an exercise that imparts meaningful resistance to the quads. To a lesser extent, this is the same principle that results in the hamstrings experiencing minimal hypertrophy after interventions of squatting, which imparts even greater loading of the quads relative to the hip extensor muscles. Accompanying the lower hamstring EMG activity, Camara et al. (2016) found the quad’s vastus lateralis (which was not assessed by Andersen et al.) to exhibit greater activity with the trap bar deadlift compared to the conventional deadlift. In contrast to the difference in hamstring EMG activity, Andersen et al. (2018) also found the gluteus maximus (which was not assessed by Camara et al.) to exhibit similar EMG activity during the two deadlift variations, which makes sense given that the gluteus maximus is able to generate hip extension without simultaneously generating knee flexion torque.
