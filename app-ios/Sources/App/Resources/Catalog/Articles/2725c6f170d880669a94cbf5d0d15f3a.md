Two intervertebral joints (namely L4-L5 and L5-S1) are normally situated below the height of the iliac crests, which form the top of your hip bones. If you set up with the top of your bones contacting the top of the pad, the motion of these two intervertebral joints will be restricted. Some of the muscles (such as the lowest portions of the multifidus and the interspinales lumborum) that function to extend these joints will likely not be trained as effectively as they otherwise could be if the pad were positioned lower. However, in some situations, it may still be beneficial to set the hip pad so its top contacts the tops of your hip bones.

Placing the pad at this high position helps the user perform the exercise as a pure spinal extension while restricting hip motion. When the hips are not fully supported, moving purely at the lumbar and thoracic spine rather than moving the spine simultaneously with the hips is a coordinated motor skill that people may have a difficult time executing initially. Especially when performing a reasonably challenging set, someone may unconsciously be meaningfully using the hip muscles to perform the exercise due to previously learned motor patterns. Essentially locking the hips in place with a high pad placement can teach someone what nearly isolated spinal motion feels like and make it easier to replicate this motor task when lowering the pad in the future. If you personally find it difficult to perform your back extension purely as a spinal movement when setting the top slightly below the tops of your hip bones, training the exercise with a higher pad placement for some time may be warranted.
