The terms “Romanian deadlift” and “stiff-leg deadlift” are often used interchangeably to describe the same exercise by scholarly articles and lifters in general. The nomenclature of the Romanian deadlift has been attributed to this exercise being performed by Nicu Vlad, a Romanian weightlifter who won gold, silver, and bronze medals respectively in the 1984, 1988, and 1996 Olympic Games in addition to earning medals in seven World Weightlifting Championships (with three of them being gold). American weightlifting coach Jim Schmitz reportedly saw Vlad performing the barbell hip hinge exercise and subsequently called it the Romanian deadlift, since Vlad did not have a particular name for the variation he was performing.

Although they are often used interchangeably, some people use the terms “Romanian deadlift” and “stiff-leg deadlift” to denote different variations. They consider the stiff-leg deadlift to be what this database describes as the “barbell stiff-leg deadlift from floor,” in which each rep begins with the plates fully on the floor, as in a conventional deadlift. This variation differs from the Romanian deadlift, which starts from an upright position and uses a range of motion individualized to one’s flexibility, as the plates often do not contact the floor. If someone has sufficient hamstring flexibility for the plates to contact the floor during a Romanian deadlift, a light tap to the ground is used, but the full weight of the bar is not transferred to the ground, as in a stiff-leg deadlift performed from the floor. In contrast to the Romanian deadlift, which is typically performed with a controlled lowering phase, this conception of the stiff-leg deadlift from the floor often involves a quicker lowering phase with a tempo akin to conventional deadlifts: a controlled drop. Both variations can involve a mild degree of knee flexion at the bottom of the hinge, but the stiff-leg deadlift from the floor may require adopting a more flexed lumbar position to pull from the floor with the knee in position while maintaining high hips. This differs from the Romanian deadlift, which is performed only within the range of motion in which the lumbar spine can be maintained in close to neutral or flat back position.

As demonstrated by sources such as Hegyi et al. (2018) along with Piper and Waller (2001), another interpretation of the stiff-leg deadlift also exists as a truly straight leg deadlift. Under this conception of the exercise, the knees are kept extended to the point of complete straightness, but not fully locked (which could otherwise allow knee hyperextension in individuals with sufficient mobility). Such a technique would result in a movement that is more akin to “bending over” rather than “sitting back,” and the bar would correspondingly be likely to drift further in front of the legs compared to a Romanian deadlift, which utilizes a mild knee bend that allows you to “sit back.” In light of the different ways in which these names may be interpreted, hopefully, this note provides some clarification if you have ever been confused by how different individuals may view Romanian deadlifts and stiff-leg deadlifts exercises as the same exercise or distinct variations.
