_Key takeaway: Research has found seated hamstring curls to stimulate significantly greater hamstring growth than lying hamstring curls, which are like to train the hamstrings similarly to standing hamstring curls._

All four of the hamstring muscles function as knee flexors and will consequently be trained by any hamstring curl variation. However, the hip position you adopt for a variation can affect the degree of hamstring growth that exercise is likely to stimulate. The hip position is relevant because three of the four hamstring muscles originate from the pelvis (on the ischial tuberosity, which is also known as the “sit bone”) and cross the back of the hip joint. This allows them to function as hip extensors, shortening as the hip extends and lengthening as the hip flexes. Since they cross two joints, the semitendinosus, semimembranous, and biceps femoris long head are known as the biarticular (crossing two joints) hamstrings. In contrast, the biceps femoris short head originates from the femur (thigh bone) and exclusively crosses the knee joint, so it is unaffected by the hip position.

A seated hamstring curl places the hip into a meaningfully flexed hip position and allows the biarticular hamstrings to be trained at longer lengths than a lying hamstring curl, which situates the hip in only a mildly flexed position. Standing hamstring curl machines also commonly place the hip in a mildly flexed position, resulting in the hamstring being trained in a manner similar to that of lying hamstring curls. However, certain standing models permit you to lean forward meaningfully, which can place the hip into a higher degree of hip flexion than lying hamstring curl machines.

Like with other biarticular muscles (such as the gastrocnemii, rectus femoris, and long head of the triceps), the available body evidence indicates that the biarticular hamstrings are likely to experience greater hypertrophy when trained at long lengths compared to shorter lengths. Maeo et al. (2020) assigned 20 participants without resistance training experience to train single leg hamstring curls for 12 weeks. For each participant, one leg trained seated hamstring curls with the hip flexed by approximately 90 degrees, while the other leg trained lying hamstring curls with the hip flexed by approximately 30 degrees. Each exercise was performed through the same 90-degree knee range of motion for 5 sets of 10 reps twice per week. Assigning one leg of each participant to a different training group can help control for many individual factors (e.g., genetics, diet, lifestyle) that could confound results when each person is assigned to only one group. Consequently, this study design is a particularly useful means of comparing the effects of two hamstring curl variations. Following the intervention, the biceps femoris short head grew similarly across the two variations, whereas each of the three biarticular hamstrings showed significantly greater growth with the seated hamstring curls.

This is quite meaningful to total hamstring growth because the biarticular hamstring constitutes the overwhelming majority (90% according to some data) of total hamstring muscle volume. Correspondingly, the seated group experienced a 14% increase in total hamstring muscle volume, which exceeded the 9% increase observed in the lying group. While not directly tested, standing hamstring curls would most likely induce hamstring growth, as with lying hamstring curls, if they place the hip in a mildly flexed position. When comparing different hamstring exercises, other studies (one, two) have also found that exercises loading the biarticular hamstring at long lengths (i.e., in a greater angle of hip flexion) resulted in greater total hamstring growth than exercises training them at shorter lengths (i.e., in a lower angle of hip flexion). Consequently, the outcomes reported by Maeo et al. (2020) align with the broader body of evidence available on this topic.

The aforementioned findings indicate that seated hamstring curls are likely the more effective variation for developing your hamstrings, but this does not mean that the other variations are ineffective for training your hamstrings. Sometimes, your gym may have a lying or standing hamstring curl machine that you feel suits your body well, whereas a seated machine simply feels awkward, regardless of how you adjust it. Certain machines do not accommodate all body types equally, and some are designed better than others. If you have the option between a low-quality seated machine and a high-quality lying or standing machine, you should not necessarily ignore the hamstring curl machine that feels like it suits you better just because it trains the hamstrings at short muscle lengths. Furthermore, it is unclear how much effect the hamstring curl variation has on muscle growth when included in a program that also includes other exercises that train the hamstrings in a lengthened position. For instance, if you perform Romanian deadlifts and seated hamstring curls, we lack evidence to determine whether seated hamstring curls still elicit greater long-term hamstring growth than lying hamstring curls. Additionally, incorporating variation into your program can help prevent training from becoming stale for many people. If you have been training seated hamstring curls for a long time and feel that it has become monotonous, there is nothing wrong with switching to a lying or standing variation for a period as a means to enjoy training more. While seated hamstring curls can be an excellent variation to develop the hamstrings, lying or standing hamstring curls may still serve a useful function in your overall training program.
