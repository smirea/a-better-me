During the lifting phase of a row, your traps and rhomboids will function to retract (pull backward and together) your scapulae (shoulder blades). Because scapular motion is naturally coupled with motion of the shoulder’s glenohumeral joint, your traps and rhomboids will be trained while rowing, regardless of whether you focus on any particular scapular motion. However, you may be able to amplify the stimulus that the rows provide to these muscles through two approaches; the first of which was included in the technique described above. Allowing your scapulae to fully protract (move forward and apart from each other) at the end of the lowering phase of a row will subject the traps and rhomboids to tension in a more lengthened position compared to if you only returned your scapulae to their resting position (in between full protraction and retraction). Currently, no study has yet investigated how hypertrophy of the traps or rhomboids may be influenced by the peak muscle length reached while resistance training. However, research on other muscle groups indicates that training at longer peak lengths, up to a certain point, may promote greater hypertrophy than training at shorter peak lengths. The effect may vary among different muscle groups, and training at very long peak muscle lengths does not necessarily induce greater hypertrophy than training at moderately long peak muscle lengths, as data from Larsen et al. (2025) suggests. However, with the exception of one study by Goto et al. (2019), the available evidence indicates that training at longer peak muscle lengths is likely to promote greater, or at least equal, hypertrophy relative to training at shorter peak lengths. Even if the effect of training at longer muscle lengths is uncertain for the traps and rhomboids, it is sensible to use a technique that may provide additional benefits if it does not impose any cost.

It is worth noting that scapular protraction is naturally coupled with flexion of the thoracic spine, while scapular retraction is naturally coupled with extension of the thoracic spine. With a row variation that has external stabilization provided to the trunk, such as a chest-supported machine row, trunk flexion can be limited, but the trunk can be free to flex during free weight row variations, such as a bent over barbell row. Consequently, full scapular protraction can readily be reached with minimal trunk flexion during a chest-supported row, but it can be very difficult for most people to protract their scapulae fully during a bent over barbell row without also exhibiting noticeable trunk flexion. As a result, intentionally moving into full scapular protraction at the end of a bent over barbell row’s lowering phase changes its technique elsewhere in the body to a greater degree than with a chest-supported row. Performing a bent over barbell row with notable trunk flexion as a consequence of reaching full scapular protraction is not necessarily problematic, but it deviates enough from the standard technique for this exercise to be its own variation in contrast to how full scapular protraction can be incorporated into a chest-supported row without meaningfully affecting spinal motion.

Along with emphasizing the achievement of full scapular protraction at the end of each rep or this row, you can likely further amplify the stimulus provided to your traps and rhomboids by immediately following up a set of rows with pure scapular retraction reps. When a row can no longer be performed due to fatigue, the strength of other back muscles, rather than the trapezius and rhomboids, may be the limiting factor. If your scapular retractor muscles are not the limiting factor during a multi-joint row, you can perform isolated scapular retraction reps directly after a set of rows to ensure that these muscles are further stimulated. To do so, you would keep your elbows extended after your last rep of standard rows and alternate between fully retracting and fully protracting your scapulae. Performing multiple reps of these pure scapular retraction after a set of rows to fatigue suggests that the rows may have initially trained other back muscles more effectively than your traps and rhomboids, but the pure scapular retraction can readily rectify that discrepancy in a time-efficient manner.
