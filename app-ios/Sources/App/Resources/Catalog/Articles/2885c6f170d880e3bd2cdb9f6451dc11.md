Any cable chop variation will train the abdominal obliques because they are the strongest trunk rotators in the human body. However, the low-to-high variation potentially may not train the obliques quite as effectively as high-to-low or horizontal cable chops. In addition to primary trunk rotation and side bending, the obliques secondarily function as trunk flexors. During a low-to-high cable chop, the downward line of pull requires you to generate trunk extension torque in addition to trunk rotation torque to move your trunk diagonally away from the pulley. This means that the trunk flexion torque generated by contraction of the obliques may be counterproductive to performance, in contrast to the high-to-low chop (which trains trunk rotation and trunk flexion together) or the horizontal chop (which exclusively trains trunk rotation). Engaging the spinal extensor muscles can counteract the undesirable trunk flexion torque produced by the obliques during a low-to-high chop; however, it is unclear whether the nervous system still somewhat limits oblique engagement relative to a variation that does not train trunk extension with trunk rotation.
