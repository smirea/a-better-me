Compared to free weight squats like back squats, front squats, and goblet squats, belt squats will involve a greater ratio of knee loading to hip loading and consequently be more quad-dominant. In a belt squat, the resistance pulls downward from the belt near the hips, resulting in a lever arm acting on the knees that is notably larger than that acting on the hips. This imposes higher demands meaningfully on the quads as the function to extend the knee than the gluteus maximus and adductor magnus, as they function to extend the hip. Free weight squats involve relatively higher resistive lever arms acting on the hip, while still having notable resistive lever arms acting on the knee. Provided that they are performed through a sufficiently long range of motion, free weight squats can effectively train the quads, gluteus maximus, and adductor magnus simultaneously.

When assessing the moment arms, the gluteus maximus and adductor magnus are unlikely to be trained as effectively by belt squats as by free weight squats; however, no study has yet measured muscle growth after a belt-squat training intervention. Consequently, we cannot confidently determine how much difference may exist. When looking at other forms of evidence, it is worth noting that EMG activity of the gluteus maximus has been measured to be lower during a belt squat compared to a back squat, but quad EMG activity has been found to be similar. While belt squats most likely have a higher ratio of quad engagement to gluteus maximus engagement, that does not necessarily mean that belt squats are likely to train the quads any more effectively than back squats. Belt squats may provide a similar stimulus to the quads as free weights squats while just training the hip musculature to a lesser degree.

If your goal is to train the greatest amount of muscle mass, free weight squats are likely preferable to belt squats, but if you want to target the quads while minimizing fatigue elsewhere in the body, belt squats can be quite useful. More evident than the likely reduced hip muscle utilization is the lower spinal extensor muscle demand with belt squats compared with free weight squats. Unsurprisingly, EMG activity of the lumbar extensor muscles has been measured to be significantly lower when belt squatting than back squatting. If you feel that you are approaching the limit from which your lower back can recover, belt squats can help you train your quads like free weight squats without imposing the same recovery cost to your lower back.
