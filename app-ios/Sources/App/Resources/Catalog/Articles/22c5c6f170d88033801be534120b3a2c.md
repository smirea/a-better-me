You can use one of three arm positions for wrist roller wrist extension, each described on its own exercise page as a variation in this database. In addition to holding your arms out in front of you with straight elbows (which flexes the shoulders by about 90 degrees), you can keep your upper arms by your sides with your elbows bent by about 90 degrees, or you can keep your arms just in front of you, directed toward the ground with straight elbows. Keeping your arms outstretched parallel to the floor requires your shoulder flexor muscles (e.g., front delt, upper pec major) to act isometrically (statically) to prevent your arms from lowering. Similarly, keeping your elbows bent requires your elbow flexor muscles (e.g., biceps, brachialis, brachioradialis) to act isometrically to prevent your elbows from straightening during wrist roller wrist extension. In contrast to the other two positions, minimal resistance will be applied to your shoulder or elbow flexor muscles when keeping your arms directed down toward the ground, due to the weight's position relative to your elbows and shoulders.
When performing one of the two variations with the weight held farther in front of you, you train more muscles than when you keep the weight close to you, which has potential advantages and disadvantages. If your shoulder or elbow flexor muscles must act isometrically to maintain your arm position, they will also receive a stimulus to increase in size and strength, along with the wrist flexor muscles that lift the weight. However, we lack data to conclude with any degree of confidence how effective isometric mid-range exercise is for developing the shoulder or elbow flexor muscles compared with dynamic full range of motion exercise training these muscle groups. A study from Rasch and Morehouse (1957) found that a six-week intervention of dynamic curls yielded greater increases in upper arm circumference than an intervention of isometric mid-range curls. While arm circumference may increase from increased elbow flexor muscle thickness, circumference measurements are not necessarily the same as muscle size, so these findings should be taken with a grain (or shaker) of salt, particularly in light of the study being published more than a decade before a man landed on the moon.
Isometric training targeting the shoulder or elbow flexor muscles from holding the wrist roller out in front of you may come at the expense of wrist extensor training. If your sets are limited by the ability of your shoulder or elbow flexor muscles to maintain arm position rather than by your wrist extensor muscles' ability to dynamically lift the weight, your wrist extensor muscles will receive a suboptimal stimulus compared with the case where their strength is the limiting factor. On the other hand, if your wrist extensor muscles still serve as the limiting factor in set performance when keeping your elbows bent or arms outstretched, the additional isometric stimulus provided to the elbow or shoulder flexor muscles should not be accompanied by an impaired stimulus to the wrist flexor muscles.
Of the different variations, the likelihood of your wrist extensor muscles not functioning as the limiting factor (and therefore receiving the least stimulus) will likely be greatest when keeping your arms parallel to the floor with straight elbows. In this position, the weight has a dramatically longer lever arm acting on the shoulder than the wrist, and the lever arm acting on the shoulder is much longer than the lever arm (via the length of your upper arm) acting on the elbow when performing the bent elbow variation. Consequently, the shoulder flexor muscles will have to generate considerably greater torque to maintain the outstretched arm position than the elbow flexor muscles will have to generate to maintain the bent elbow position. However, data from Bober et al. (2002) indicate that average maximal shoulder flexion torque at 90 degrees of shoulder flexion is mildly less than average maximal elbow flexion torque at 90 degrees of elbow flexion. As a result, the probability that the shoulder flexor muscles are the limiting factor when using the wrist roller with the outstretched arm position meaningfully exceeds the probability that the elbow flexor muscles limit set performance when using the wrist roller with bent elbows. Nonetheless, strength ratios across muscle groups can vary substantially among individuals.
Due to their larger cross-sectional area (a key determinant of force production) and longer internal lever arms, the shoulder flexor muscles can generate much more torque than the wrist extensor muscles. However, the advantage in sheer torque production that the shoulder flexor muscles have can be offset by the markedly greater resistive lever arm they face compared with the wrist extensor muscles. While shoulder flexion strength may limit set performance when some people train wrist roller wrist extension with outstretched arms, others with proportionally greater shoulder flexion strength and lower wrist extension strength may have different outcomes. As a straightforward way to gauge whether your shoulder or elbow muscles are limiting your wrist roller exercise sets while your arms are parallel to the ground or your elbows bent, you can attempt to perform additional reps with your arms directed down toward the ground immediately after completing your initial set. If you can indeed perform more reps with the weight closer to you after completing as many reps as you could with either of the other two variations, your forearm muscles may not be the limiting factor, or receive the optimal stimulus from the other variations with the weight held farther in front of your body. Overall, the key takeaway is that the arm position you use for wrist roller exercises can influence which muscle groups are trained most effectively. When your goal is to maximize your forearm muscle development, it will be wise to perform a variation that you find to be limited by the strength of these muscles rather than by the strength of other muscle groups in the body.
