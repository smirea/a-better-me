A push-up and a bench press can be performed with essentially the same shoulder and elbow motion, thereby training the pressing muscles in a very similar manner. During either type of exercise with hands spaced apart by the same distance, the pec major and front delt can perform the same shoulder functions, while the triceps can perform the same elbow function. Two studies have compared changes in muscle size after participants completed a push-up intervention or a flat barbell bench intervention, and both found that the two exercises induced similar magnitudes of pec major growth. One of these studies measured triceps growth and found it was similar between the two groups. The lack of any difference in muscle growth aligns with other studies’ findings that push-ups and bench presses yield similar degrees of EMG activity in the pec major and triceps when resistance is equated between the exercises, but some research has found greater front delt EMG activity to occur when bench pressing.

Studies have equated resistance in various ways, such as by adding elastic bands or a weighted vest to push-ups, up to a rep max that the bench press also uses. Alternatively, research has attempted to equate resistance by loading the bench press with the weight measured by scales under someone’s hands during push-ups (as the average of the top and bottom positions). However, this approach results in each bench press rep having a higher intensity than each push-up rep, with a particularly profound difference for females, so it does not provide the ideal comparison for EMG research. For instance, the available evidence indicates that individuals support about 70-75% of their bodyweight through their hands at the bottom of a standard push-up. When loading a barbell with this proportion of body weight, people, on average, can complete significantly more push-up reps than bench press reps to fatigue. Consequently, EMG research using this approach may be comparing a high intensity bench press to a moderate intensity push-up. This could lead to higher EMG values during the bench press than if a study used a bench press load that allowed participants to perform the same number of reps to fatigue as on a push-up.

Although less studied than the upper body muscles, the rectus abdominis of the core has been shown to exhibit significantly greater EMG activity during push-ups than during bench presses. Other abdominal muscles are likely to be more active during a push-up, as they function to resist lumbar extension and thereby prevent the hips from sagging downward. When resistance is properly equated, a push-up can likely train the upper body’s pressing muscles similarly to a bench press while also training the core muscles similarly to a tall plank, an advantage the bench press lacks. Kohiruimaki et al. (2019) examined how well push-up variations can develop the abdominals by assigning eight previously untrained men to an 8-week intervention in which they performed suspended push-ups in three weekly sessions, with three sets each. When performing this push-up variation, participants held handles attached to suspension straps with their feet elevated to the height of their hands. In addition to the thickness of the triceps increasing by 16%, the thickness of the rectus abdominis and external abdominal oblique, respectively, increased by 27% and 14% after the intervention. This is an impressive magnitude of abdominal muscle growth for an exercise training the abs in a stabilizing manner rather than as prime movers, and it provides notable evidence supporting the ability of suspended push-ups to train the abdominal muscles effectively. However, we cannot necessarily generalize these findings to other push-up variations, as suspended push-ups have been shown to elicit significantly greater EMG activity in the abdominal muscles than more stable traditional push-ups.

Another notable difference between the two exercises is that push-ups allow your scapulae (shoulder blades) to move freely throughout each rep. During a push-up, you can fluidly retract your scapulae during the descent and protract your scapulae during the ascent since you do not have your scapulae pressed into a bench. While you can try to do so when bench pressing, the bench contacting your scapulae can make it more difficult to move them fluidly and with sound technique. To ensure your scapulae are retracted at the bottom of a bench press rep (when scapular retraction can particularly enhance shoulder stability and performance), standard bench press technique involves setting up in a position of scapular retraction and maintaining it throughout the set.

In a push-up position, gravity exerts a force that will cause your scapulae to retract if unopposed, so the scapular protractor muscles are directly resisted as you push up and transition from a retracted scapulae position to near a neutral position at the top. They similarly act eccentrically to control the dynamic scapular retraction that occurs during the lowering phase. Even when attempting to keep their scapulae fully retracted, some scapular protraction and retraction motion likely occurs during bench press, whereas push-ups likely train the scapular protractor muscles to a greater extent. If this occurs, the serratus anterior and pec minor, which are the two primary scapular protractor muscles, should be more effectively trained by push-ups than bench presses. However, only one study has measured changes in primary scapular protractor muscle size after a horizontal press training intervention. That study found the cross-sectional area of the pec minor to increase by 11.5% after 26 untrained males completed a 10-week intervention of flat wide grip Smith machine bench pressing. While this finding provides evidence that bench pressing can meaningfully develop the pec minor (which experienced the same magnitude of relative growth as the triceps), it did not include a push-up training group for comparison. Consequently, the potential for push-ups to train the serratus anterior and pec minor more effectively than bench pressing is speculative in nature.
