Compared to flat variations, incline chest fly and bench press variations most likely train the upper pec major’s clavicular head to a preferentially greater degree than the sternocostal head that constitutes the middle and lower pec major. However, flat variations can still train the upper pec effectively, and it is currently unclear whether incline variations meaningfully train the upper pec more effectively than flat variations in an absolute sense. At first glance, these statements may appear contradictory, but there is an important distinction between training a muscle to a greater degree than another and training a muscle more effectively overall.

Some studies have found the upper pec to exhibit significantly greater EMG activity during flat pressing compared to incline pressing, while other studies using EMG and related proxies have reported similar upper pec activity during flat and incline variations. In contrast, the lower pec has been more consistently shown to exhibit lower activity during incline variations relative to flat variations. As a result, incline variations may bias the upper pec to a relatively greater degree primarily by reducing lower pec engagement rather than by increasing upper pec engagement. If so, the upper pec may experience similar development from incline and flat variations, while the lower pec may experience less development during incline variations. That said, it remains possible that incline variations induce greater upper pec hypertrophy than flat variations, given the mixed findings.

To date, only one study, conducted by Chaves et al. (2020), has directly measured changes in the size of different regions of the pectoralis major following a training intervention using flat or incline chest exercises. In that study, untrained participants were assigned to perform the flat Smith machine bench press, the incline Smith machine bench press, or a combination of both. After eight weeks of training with one weekly session consisting of 4–6 sets of the assigned exercise, the incline group increased upper pec thickness by 62% and middle and lower pec thickness by 55–58%. The flat and mixed groups both increased upper pec thickness by 32%, which was significantly lower than the incline group. Middle pec thickness increased by 46% and 24% in the flat and mixed groups, respectively, while lower pec thickness increased by 43–42%. Differences in middle and lower pec growth were not statistically significant, but average changes favored the incline group.
Based on these results, incline training appeared superior to flat variations for chest muscle growth, as the incline group experienced substantially greater upper pec hypertrophy without sacrificing middle or lower pec growth. However, these findings warrant skepticism. The magnitude of hypertrophy observed after only eight sessions and approximately 40 total sets was unusually large, raising concerns about potential measurement error. Additionally, it is unclear why the group performing both flat and incline pressing experienced the same upper pec growth as the flat-only group, or why middle pec growth was markedly lower in the mixed group.

Despite these concerns, the findings from Chaves et al. (2020) provide some evidence supporting incline variations for upper chest development. Their observation that flat pressing still produced meaningful upper pec growth aligns with findings from Ogasawara et al. (2013) and Davies et al. (2021), who reported similar increases in upper and lower pec size following flat bench press training. Pinto et al. (2025) likewise found that machine pec fly training resulted in similar hypertrophy of the clavicular and sternocostal heads. These results are consistent with EMG evidence and the anatomy of the pectoralis major, which allows all regions to contribute to shoulder horizontal flexion.
If upper chest development is a priority, including incline press and/or incline fly variations is reasonable, as there is sound rationale for their potential to bias the upper pec more than horizontal variations. However, horizontal variations clearly train the entire pectoral major effectively, and meaningful upper pectoral development can still occur without incline exercises. It is also worth noting that the clavicular head comprises roughly one-fifth of total pectoralis major muscle volume, with the sternocostal head accounting for the remainder. As a result, increasing upper pec size by a given percentage will have a smaller effect on total chest mass than an equivalent percentage increase in the sternocostal head.
