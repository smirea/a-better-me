Pin squats can be performed with the pins set to any point in the lift’s range of motion, so where the pins are positioned can be individualized to address someone’s specific weakness or technical deficiencies. With that said, setting the pins close to where the bar would be at the bottom of your back squat can generally be an effective approach if you are using pin squats as a means to improve back squat performance or are generally looking to enhance your lower body strength through a long range of motion.

Along with directly improving your strength at the joint angles present when starting the lifting phase, setting the pins low enough to train through close to your back squat’s full range of motion can often improve your positioning during subsequent parts of the lifting phase. If you begin lifting in a mechanically advantageous position, it may be easier to complete the rep than if you start with suboptimal positioning. For instance, if your hips shoot up prematurely when squatting out of the hole, you can be pitched forward excessively and have a greater difficulty with the rest of your squat. Even if you consistently fail midway through the lifting phase, starting your ascent with optimal positioning can increase the load you can handle before reaching your limit at that point.

Increasing strength near the start of the lifting phase can also make it easier to complete the initial portion of the rep, which may build greater speed and reduce difficulty in subsequent parts of the lift. If you have greater momentum entering into a later portion of the rep, you can be less likely to fail compared to if the bar was moving quite slowly into that point from having to grind to begin the lifting phase. To have improved starting strength effectively transfer to increased bar speed, you just want to make sure that you train standard back squats at some point as well, because exclusively training the pin variation can neglect to optimize the technique you use without the pins.
