Pull-ups can be performed with a variety of hand grips, including different grip widths and whether an overhand, underhand (resulting in the exercise being called a chin-up), neutral, or hybrid (i.e., a combination between neutral and underhand or overhand) grip is utilized. Additionally, the shoulder motion occurring during a pull-up can be performed predominantly in the sagittal (front to back) plane, frontal (side to side) plane, or in a combination between the sagittal and frontal planes. When symmetrically moving the shoulders strictly in the sagittal plane, as would occur when lifting your arms straight out in front of you, the upper arms remain parallel to each other and do not deviate away from the midline of your body. Lifting your arms straight out to your sides, such as with a snow angel motion, would be an example of moving your shoulders strictly in the frontal plane. When raising your arms in the frontal plane, the upper arms move away from the midline of your body until they are parallel to the ground, and then they move closer to the midline of your body with further elevation. During the lifting phase of a pull-up, the shoulders may extend in the sagittal plane, adduct in the frontal plane, or exhibit a combination of extension and adduction in a hybrid plane between the sagittal and frontal planes. Rather than moving the shoulders exclusively through a single plane, pull-up variations are commonly performed with a technique that allows the shoulders to move between these two planes, but one plane of shoulder motion often contributes more than the other.

Muscles acting on the shoulder joint have different leverage to generate extension torque in the sagittal plane versus adduction torque in the frontal plane. During any active movement, the nervous system is most likely to recruit the muscles best suited to produce the joint actions required for efficient performance. Consequently, determining which muscles have meaningful leverage to extend or adduct the shoulder can inform how effectively these muscles will be trained by a pull-up variation that utilizes a specific shoulder motion. A muscle’s leverage to contribute to a particular joint action can be quantified as its moment arm, which is the perpendicular distance between a joint’s axis of rotation and the muscle’s line of pull. When multiplied by how much force its contraction produces, the moment arm determines how much torque a muscle can generate for a particular joint action.

With any particular grip for a pull-up, you can intentionally rotate your shoulders in a manner that allows you to move your shoulder relatively more in the sagittal or frontal plane. However, most people find that a particular path of shoulder motion is most comfortable and stable for their shoulders, elbows, and wrists when using a specific grip. For instance, people typically perform a wide overhand grip pull-up with shoulder motion predominantly occurring in the frontal plane, whereas they typically perform a close grip chin-up (underhand) with shoulder motion predominantly occurring in the sagittal plane. Performing either variation in the alternative plane of motion would require greater than normal rotational range of motion in the shoulders and forearms, and attempting to do so could feel very awkward and uncomfortable. People typically feel most comfortable when positioning themselves so that, at the portion of the pull-up when their upper arms are parallel to the ground, their elbows and wrists are vertically aligned, or their elbows lie slightly lateral (outside) to their wrists.

In general, widening a grip facilitates relatively more frontal plane shoulder motion, while narrowing a grip facilitates relatively more sagittal plane shoulder motion. An overhand, underhand, or neutral grip may be used with any grip width, but the combination of these grip variables will influence comfort and which plane of shoulder motion feels most natural. When pulling predominantly in the sagittal plane with a relatively close grip, an underhand or neutral grip is typically more comfortable than an overhand grip. Similarly, when pulling predominantly in the frontal plane with a relatively wide grip, an overhand or neutral grip is typically more comfortable and allows greater frontal plane motion than an underhand grip. With a moderate grip width, people may commonly find that pulling mostly in the sagittal plane feels natural with an underhand grip, but pulling relatively more in the frontal plane feels natural with an overhand grip. Fundamentally, shoulder motion is what matters to the back muscles, and grip is relevant only insofar as it influences which shoulder motion is performed.

The lat and teres major in the back function both as primary adductors and extensors and are therefore highly likely to be trained effectively by pull-up variations regardless of the grip or plane of shoulder motion. However, the preponderance of available evidence indicates that the rear delt lacks meaningful leverage to contribute to shoulder adduction, but it clearly has favorable leverage to function as a shoulder extensor. The rear delt is correspondingly more likely to be trained effectively with a closer grip that emphasizes shoulder extension, which is most comfortable when using an underhand or neutral grip. Alternatively, the middle and lower regions of the chest’s pec major that constitute the sternocostal head have large moment arms to function as shoulder adductors. However, only the lower portion of the pec major exhibits a shoulder extension moment arm, and this moment arm is quite small and only present in flexed shoulder positions, like at the start of a pull-up. Consequently, wider grip pull-ups (that will be most comfortable with an overhand or neutral grip) involving greater frontal plane shoulder motion are likely to train the pec major more effectively than closer grip pulldowns. Which regions of the lat are preferentially biased by a pull-up variation may also be influenced by the extent of shoulder motion in the frontal or sagittal planes. The middle and lower regions have relatively greater leverage to function as shoulder adductors, while the upper region of the lat has relatively greater leverage to function as a shoulder extensor. Plausibly, these biomechanical differences may facilitate the middle and lower regions of the lats being preferentially trained by wider grip pull-ups, while the upper region potentially may be preferentially trained by closer grip pulling variations. That said, the extent to which any preferential biasing may occur across different regions of the lat when comparing different vertical pulling variations remains speculative and has not been tested by any longitudinal training interventions. While certain regions may have better leverage than others to contribute to a particular technique, all regions of the lat may potentially be recruited near their maximal capacity during a challenging set of a vertical pulling exercise, so it is unclear if regional biasing would actually occur.
