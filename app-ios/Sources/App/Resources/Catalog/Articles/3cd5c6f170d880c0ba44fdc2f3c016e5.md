Plate-loaded squat machines with shoulder pads come in a variety of subtypes, including standing hack squat, pendulum squat, V-squat, leverage squat, and power squat pro machines. Standing hack squat machines are characterized by their carriages (AKA sleds) linearly moving along rails that are typically about 45 degrees from vertical. In contrast to the other squat machines, a standing hack squat lacks lever arms that arc about a pivot point, which means that a standing hack squat will provide a constant level of resistance torque throughout a rep’s range of motion. Resistive torque from the other machines will vary during a rep depending on how close their lever arms are to being parallel to the ground, which is when the torque from gravitational force can be maximized.

Like standing hack squats, pendulum squats and V-squats also have long back pads that can extend along the length of the entire back. The designs of these three machines let users avoid any forward torso lean when using them, which minimizes resistive torque acting on the spinal extensor muscles. A pendulum squat has a long lever arm connecting the carriage (with the shoulder and back pads) to the machine’s pivot point, resulting in the carriage moving in a prominent pendulum motion during the exercise. When resting your back against the back pad of a pendulum squat, you face toward the base of the machine with the machine’s pivot point in front of you. In contrast, when resting your back against the back pad of a V-squat, you face away from the base of the machine with the machine’s pivot point behind you. This design divergence contributes to pendulum squats and V-squats having different paths of motion.

Leverage squat and power squat pro machines most visually differ from the other three aforementioned machine types by lacking back pads, which gives you the freedom to vary the degree to which you lean your torso forward at your hips or keep it upright. The ability to  lean the torso forward relative to the line of resistance provided by the machine can let you load your spinal extensor muscles to a greater degree with either of these machines compared to the machines that have long back pads. While leverage squats exclusively have shoulder pads, power squat pros additionally have an upper back pad positioned between the shoulder pads, which can distribute the machine’s loading through a wider surface area. Like V-squats and pendulum squats, leverage squats also have angled footplates, but power squat pros have completely flat footplates. Pro power squats also differ from any of the other squat machines by normally having multiple pairs of loading sleeves that shift the resistance profile based on where you load the weight plates, which can let you adjust how difficult the bottom of the rep is compared to its start.

When differentiating these squat machine types, it is worth noting that manufacturers do not follow a universal nomenclature. In particular, “power squat pro” is not a standardized name for this fairly uncommon design, but manufacturers most often incorporate some variant of “power squat pro” into their model names for this style of machine.

While manufacturers may not consistently use any names that differentiate them, V-squat machines can also be subdivided into one of two subtypes that can affect their usage. Some V-squats have a pair of short shoulder pads that are completely perpendicular to the back pad, designed to be used with a user’s back against the back pad. Other V-squats (which some manufacturers call V-front squat or front squat machines) have a second pair of shoulder pads extending from those pads further away from the back pad, and these pads are typically angled with corners for the shoulders to rest snugly under while facing toward the back pad. The machines with these extended shoulder pads are intentionally designed for users to train standard back-supported V-squats along with reverse V-squats (squatting like you would with a leverage squat) and good mornings while facing toward the back pad. Some people still perform reverse V-squats with machines that only have the short shoulder pads, but not everyone can do so comfortably while feeling stable. When squatting while facing toward the back pad with one of these machines, the neck may have cock back into an extended position with the face pressed against the pad to keep the shoulder pads sufficiently on the shoulders. This is not inherently problematic, but some people find the head positioning to feel awkward and uncomfortable, in contrast to when using a machine with the additional shoulder pads that are designed with this variation in mind.
