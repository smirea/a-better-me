The considerable back arch used during a sternum chin-up renders the exercise a hybrid of a vertical pull and a horizontal row. The initial portion of the lifting phase more closely resembles a vertical pull, while the end of the lifting phase more closely resembles a row. While standard chin-ups can be performed with a slight back arch, they are clearly vertical pulling movements. When considering potential differences in how effectively each exercise may train particular muscle groups, comparing rows to vertical pulls in general can provide useful insight. Naturally, the difference between a pure row and a pure vertical pull should be greater than the difference between a sternum chin-up and a standard chin-up, since the sternum chin-up is a hybrid movement. Nonetheless, the direction of these differences will likely mirror the differences observed between pure vertical pulls and pure rows performed in the same shoulder plane of motion, though to a lesser degree.

Because both variations resist shoulder extension and elbow flexion, both can train the primary shoulder extensors (lat, teres major, and rear delt) and elbow flexors (biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis). However, the standard chin-up involves a greater elbow range of motion than the sternum chin-up and therefore may have greater potential to train the elbow flexor muscles. Vertical pulling exercises have not yet been directly compared with rowing exercises in a single longitudinal study that measures muscle growth; however, other research has shown that lat pulldowns can produce elbow flexor hypertrophy comparable to curls, whereas dumbbell rows have produced less elbow flexor growth than curls. Whether these findings generalize across vertical pull and row variations remains unclear, but it is plausible that vertical pulls, on average, train the elbow flexors more robustly than rows.

In contrast, row variations are likely to train the traps and rhomboids more effectively than vertical pull variations due to differences in the direction of the resistive force. During a row, the horizontally directed resistance (relative to the shoulder girdle) creates a protraction force that is opposed by the traps and rhomboids as they retract the shoulder girdle during the lifting phase. During a vertical pull, the vertically directed resistance creates a force toward shoulder girdle elevation, which is opposed primarily by the lower trapezius, lats, and pectoralis minor, which contribute to shoulder girdle depression. Because only part of the trapezius contributes to depression, whereas all regions of the trapezius contribute to retraction, rows are likely better suited than vertical pulls for training the trapezius and rhomboids. Correspondingly, sternum chin-ups likely train the traps and rhomboids more effectively than standard chin-ups, while standard chin-ups may train the elbow flexor muscles more effectively.
