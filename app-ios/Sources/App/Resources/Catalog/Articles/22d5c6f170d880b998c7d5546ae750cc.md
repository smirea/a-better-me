*Key takeaway: Setting up the safety pins can help promote safety in the weight room.*

It is by no means uncommon for people to perform exercises with a bar on their backs without using safety pins, but using them when appropriate is strongly recommended. Positioning safety pins for exercises such as barbell squat variations is the equivalent of wearing a seat belt when driving. In either situation, you may not intend to use them, but both safety mechanisms can reduce the risk of injury in the event of an accident. While you can try to refrain from performing a set of barbell squats with a load or number of reps that is beyond your capacity, sometimes lifters can overestimate what is left in the tank and unintentionally fail a rep. Furthermore, a non-zero risk of injury exists during any reasonably challenging physical activity, even when using high quality technique and an intelligently designed program that utilizes appropriate volumes, intensities, and rates of progression. For instance, the likelihood may be very low on a per-rep basis, but it is certainly possible to sustain a sudden muscle strain injury during a challenging set of squats. If such an acute injury occurs, set the bar down onto the safety pins when they are positioned appropriately. However, if you did not previously set up the safety pins, you face a risk of sustaining further injury or damaging equipment if an unforeseen injury acutely occurs while your torso is in an inclined position with a bar on your back.

Many experienced lifters have learned how to bail out of a squat that they can no longer complete by dumping the bar off their back and rapidly moving forward away from the dropping bar. This approach may be a viable alternative to using safety pins for individuals proficient in the bailing technique who are using bumper plates on a floor that can tolerate dropping a loaded barbell from a notable height. However, safely bailing out of a squat requires greater coordination than simply using the safety pins, and it can be difficult for lifters who squat with a substantial forward torso lean. Although not suitable for every environment, an individual’s ability to bail out of a squat may be impaired if an injury is the reason the person failed the rep, so using safety pins may be preferable in many circumstances.

As demonstrated by competitive powerlifters, weightlifters, and bodybuilders, who have lower rates of reported injuries than athletes in many other sports, strenuous resistance training is not inherently dangerous, and there is no need to fear lifting weights. Nonetheless, the potential for acute injury during training cannot be eliminated, and it is prudent to take simple precautionary measures to promote your safety in the weight room.
