Relative to most biceps curl variations, this exercise allows you to train your biceps in a more lengthened position by positioning your upper arm behind your torso, which situates your shoulder in an extended position. Both the long head and the short head of the biceps originate from the scapula and have shoulder flexion moment arms, resulting in greater lengthening in an extended shoulder position compared to a neutral or flexed shoulder position. However, the brachialis and brachioradialis, which are the two other primary elbow flexor muscles (respectively present in the lower half of the upper arm and the forearm), originate from the humerus and are not lengthened or shortened by modifying the shoulder position.

The available evidence indicates that training a variety of muscles at longer peak lengths, up to a certain point, is likely to promote greater hypertrophy than training at shorter peak lengths. Consequently, it is rational to hypothesize that curling with an extended shoulder position may train the biceps somewhat more effectively than curling with a neutral or flexed shoulder position. Attarieh et al. (2025) provided direct insight into this matter by assigning 15 untrained men to a 10-week single-arm underhand-grip cable curl intervention, performed twice per week for 3-5 sets of 8-12 reps to failure. For each participant, one arm performed a preacher curl facing toward the cable station with 50 degrees of shoulder flexion, while the other arm performed a Bayesian curl through the same elbow range of motion, facing away from the cable station with 50 degrees of shoulder extension. Assigning one arm of each participant to a different curl variation helps control for many individual factors (e.g., genetics, diet, lifestyle, prior training history) that can confound results when each person is assigned to only one exercise. Following the intervention, the thickness of both the biceps and brachialis similarly increased for the two curl groups. The absence of any difference for the brachialis is unsurprising given that it does not cross the shoulder joint. However, similar biceps growth across the two exercises suggests that the biceps may respond differently to lengthened training than other biarticular (i.e., crossing two joints) muscles. Other biarticular muscles like three of the hamstrings, the calf’s gastrocnemii, the quad’s rectus femoris, and the long head of the triceps have been reported to experience significantly greater growth when trained with a variation that loads them at longer lengths compared to a variation that loads them at shorter lengths. While the difference was not statistically significant, it is worth noting that the average increase in biceps size still slightly favored the Bayesian curl. The preacher curl group increased mean thickness at the proximal (closest to the shoulder), middle, and distal (closest to the elbow) sites of the biceps, respectively, by 6%, 7%, and 7%, while the Bayesian curl group increased thickness by 9% at all three sites. On the other hand, the brachialis increased thickness by an average of 8% in the Bayesian curl group and by 10% in the preacher curl group. These small differences may be due to chance and measurement error, but additional evidence indicates that curling from an extended shoulder position may train the biceps more effectively than curling from a flexed shoulder position.

Through a study presented as a conference abstract that did not undergo peer review, Kobayashi et al. (2024) described an experiment quite similar to the one conducted by Attarieh et al. (2025). Kobayashi et al. (2024) assigned 21 untrained participants to a 12-week intervention in which they trained single-arm dumbbell curls twice per week for 5 sets of 10 reps each session. For each participant, one arm performed a preacher curl with 50 degrees of shoulder flexion, while the other arm performed an incline curl with 50 degrees of shoulder extension. After the intervention, the authors reported that the incline and preacher curl groups, respectively, increased biceps volume by 18% and 14.9%, which was a statistically significant difference between groups. However, the preacher curl group showed a 21% increase in brachialis size, exceeding the 17% increase observed in the incline curl group. Similarly, the brachioradialis increased its volume by a greater degree for the preacher curl group (13%) than the incline curl group (10%). Whenever a study is presented only as an abstract, not submitted for peer review in a journal, the validity of its results should be appraised with a healthy dose of skepticism. Nonetheless, Kobayashi et al. (2024) included renowned researchers in this field who have produced high quality peer reviewed publications, so their findings are worth mentioning despite the limitations from this form of evidence.

Kassiano et al. (2025) also compared the effects of training dumbbell preacher curls at approximately 45 degrees of shoulder flexion with dumbbell incline curls at approximately 30 degrees of shoulder extension. They assigned 63 untrained women to either group, which performed 3 sets of 8-12 reps until failure twice per week for eight weeks. In contrast to Attarieh et al. (2025) and Kobayashi et al. (2024), Kassiano et al. (2025) measured elbow flexor thickness of the biceps and brachialis combined at three different upper arm sites, so we cannot cleanly differentiate whether these two muscles may have individually responded. Following the intervention, thickness of the proximal site increased by 11.2% for the incline curl group, exceeding the 8.3% increase for the preacher curl group. While the two groups experienced similar increases in middle site muscle thickness, the preacher curl group's increase in thickness of 10.3% at the distal site was greater than the incline curl group’s 6.4% increase at this distal site. While speculative in nature, these regional differences may be influenced by the biceps reflecting a greater proportion of total elbow flexor muscle thickness at the proximal site than the distal site, which is where the brachialis may be thicker.

If the biceps grew more from incline curls, it would be sensible that the total elbow flexor muscle thickness would favor the incline curl group at the proximal site, most reflective of biceps size relative to brachialis size. Similarly, it would be sensible that the total elbow flexor muscle thickness would favor the preacher curl group at the distal site, most reflective of brachialis size relative to biceps size, if the brachialis grew more from preacher curls. More likely than the shoulder position itself, the resistance profile of free-weight preacher curls may contribute to the brachialis being trained somewhat more effectively when the shoulder is flexed. When the resistance is provided by gravity acting on a free weight, resistive torque on the elbow will be greatest when the forearm is parallel to the ground and least when the forearm is perpendicular to the ground. The elbow flexor muscles are most lengthened at the starting position of any curl when the elbow is fully straight. At this point during an incline dumbbell curl, the arm is perpendicular to the ground, so resistive torque does not act on the elbow in this position. However, the flexed shoulder position present during a dumbbell preacher curl prompts the arm not to be perpendicular to the ground in the starting position, so resistive torque acts on the elbow flexor muscles when they reach their longest lengths during each rep. Even though the brachialis reaches the same lengths during each variation, the ability to be meaningfully loaded at its longest length during a preacher curl potentially may amplify the stimulus provided to this muscle when using free weights. This may also explain why Kobayashi et al. (2024) found that the brachioradialis experienced greater growth during the dumbbell preacher curl.

While the resistance profile may have an effect, its role remains speculative at this time, and we cannot draw strong conclusions about the differential effects of training curls from a flexed versus an extended shoulder position. There is reason to think that curls with an extended shoulder position may train the biceps more effectively than curls with a flexed shoulder position, and that curls with tension at the start of each rep (e.g., preacher curls) may be ideal for training the brachialis and brachioradialis. However, the evidence is insufficient to support either possibility with a high degree of confidence. Nonetheless, you can be confident that curls can effectively train your primary elbow flexor muscles, regardless of whether they involve an extended or flexed shoulder position and whether they use high resistance at the start of each rep. These variables may have some effect, but biceps curl variations are far more similar than different, and you have many viable options from which to select to develop your arms.
