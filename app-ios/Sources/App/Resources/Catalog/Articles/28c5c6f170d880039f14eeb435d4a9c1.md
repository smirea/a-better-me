When performing this exercise, you can move your shoulders in essentially the same manner as you would during a dumbbell lateral raise, and these exercises train the same muscle groups. However, the resistance profiles of these variations differ meaningfully. With a dumbbell lateral raise, the shoulder does not experience any resistive torque in a neutral frontal plane position (when your arm is at your side), and the resistive torque steadily increases as the shoulder abducts up to 90 degrees. This occurs because the resistance is provided by a vertically directed gravitational force acting on the dumbbell, and the perpendicular distance between the shoulder joint and the dumbbell in the frontal plane progressively increases as the arm approaches parallelism with the ground.

With a machine lateral raise, resistive torque acts on the shoulder throughout the rep. The specific resistance profile can vary across machines, but many machines provide a flat resistance profile, such that resistive torque is essentially constant throughout the range of motion once the machine’s weight plates separate at the start of a rep. Other machines can use a cam mechanism that contributes to greater resistance torque being present toward the start or end of the exercise’s range of motion. At the start of each rep, where the shoulder abductor muscles are at their longest lengths, these muscles will correspondingly experience meaningful tension during a typical machine lateral raise and minimal tension during a dumbbell lateral raise.
