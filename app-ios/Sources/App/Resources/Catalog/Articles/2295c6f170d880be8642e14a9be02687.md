With a collective volume exceeding that of the hamstrings, the six hip adductor muscles constitute a meaningful proportion of your total lower body musculature. Except for seated machine hip adduction, resisted hip adduction exercises are not commonly performed by most lifters. However, most lifters will still train their hip adductor muscles if they perform exercises for their glutes, hamstrings, and or abs because the hip adductor muscles contribute to much more than hip adduction.

The adductor magnus constitutes over half of the total volume of the six hip adductor muscles combined, and this muscle is a powerful hip extensor, especially at high degrees of hip flexion, such as at the bottom of a squat, where it has greater hip extension leverage than any other muscle in the body. At high degrees of hip flexion, three other hip adductor muscles, namely the adductor longus, adductor brevis, and gracilis, also function as hip extensors and can assist the larger adductor magnus. While the adductor longus and adductor brevis can be trained by deep squats, lunges, and leg press variations, the gracilis is unique in that it also has excellent leverage to function as a knee flexor. Like the hamstrings, the gracilis is correspondingly unlikely to be trained effectively as a hip extensor during compound exercises that notably load the quads, since its contraction opposes the quads at the knee, but it can be trained effectively by leg curls and, to a lesser degree, hip hinges such as Romanian deadlifts and good mornings. When the hip is in a neutral position, such as when standing upright, or at very low angles of hip flexion, the adductor brevis and especially the adductor longus also function as hip flexors, while the pectineus functions as a hip flexor in any hip position. They can therefore be trained by exercises that involve hip flexion from near a neutral hip position, such as the V-up, also known as the jackknife, or the dragon flag, which are often performed with the primary intent of training the abs, since the abdominal muscles and hip flexors function together during these exercises.

Given their multiple functions, you can train your hip adductor muscles quite well overall without using resisted hip adduction exercises. However, you will most likely experience greater development of these muscles if you include at least one direct hip adduction exercise in your program. Seated machine hip adduction is an ideal option because it allows you to train these muscles at very long lengths in a stable manner, limited only by the strength of your hip adductors. In contrast to squats, hip hinge variations, and hip flexion exercises such as V-ups, no other muscles are likely to fatigue before the hip adductor muscles during seated machine hip adduction, so these muscles will be the limiting factor for each set.

Beyond likely contributing to the development of the aforementioned muscles, resisted hip adduction may be required to train the adductor minimus effectively, since it does not meaningfully contribute to hip extension or flexion, unlike the other hip adductors. As its name suggests, the adductor minimus is a small muscle, but training a muscle that is otherwise largely neglected by other exercises is an additional benefit of including direct hip adduction exercises.
