If the total load is equated, holding a weight on one side of the body or on both sides will not directly affect how the gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus function as hip extensors to lift the torso and control descent during the lowering phase of a hip hinge exercise. That is because hip extension occurs in the sagittal plane of motion, which runs front to back and divides the body into right and left halves. However, holding a single weight on the side opposite your working leg can increase the demands placed on the hip abductor muscles on the side of the working leg.

When holding weights on both sides, the weight opposite the working leg imparts a torque toward hip adduction of the working leg, while the weight on the same side imparts a torque toward hip abduction. The weight opposite the working leg is farther from the working hip joint, so it has a greater lever arm to produce hip adduction torque than the other weight has to produce hip abduction torque. This results in a net resistive hip adduction torque acting on the working leg, but the weight on the same side partially counteracts that torque. When holding a weight only on the side opposite the working leg, no such counterbalance exists, requiring the hip abductors of the working leg to generate greater hip abduction torque to resist hip adduction and stabilize the pelvis.
As a result, holding one 20 lb (9 kg) weight on the side opposite the working leg may require greater hip abduction torque than holding two 20 lb (9 kg) weights symmetrically. In practice, the prime movers that extend the hip in the sagittal plane to lift the body may be challenged to a similar degree whether you hold a single 40 lb (18 kg) weight or two 20 lb (9 kg) weights. Consequently, if you have sufficient hip extension strength to use two 20 lb (9 kg) weights, you may be able to challenge the hip abductor muscles of the working leg more effectively by holding a single 40 lb (18 kg) weight on the opposite side. This offset loading approach can enable greater hip abductor training without reducing the stimulus to the hip extensors, provided that the total load is matched. However, if offset loading limits the total weight you can use because hip abduction strength becomes the limiting factor, the stimulus to the hip extensor muscles may be reduced.

Mo et al. (2023) provided further insight by assessing EMG activity during single-leg Romanian deadlifts performed with a weight held on the same side or the opposite side of the working leg. The upper region of the gluteus maximus and the gluteus medius both showed significantly greater EMG activity when the weight was held opposite the working leg. Because these muscles function as hip abductors, this finding aligns with the greater hip abductor demands created by offset loading. Similarly, Neumann and Cook (1985) found that carrying a load in an offset position elicited the greatest gluteus medius EMG activity when compared with other loading configurations.
