_Key takeaway: Not everyone has the mobility to pull from the floor with ideal technique, but slightly elevating the bar can accommodate mobility limitations while effectively training most of the same muscles._

Plenty of lifters can comfortably train conventional deadlifts from the floor, but the arbitrary starting height may not be ideal for everyone. While deadlifting from the floor, your body proportions determine the magnitude of hip and knee flexion required to grasp the bar. When a barbell is loaded with at least one pair of full-diameter plates (such as 45lb (20kg) plates or bumper plates), the center of the barbell will be elevated slightly, under 9in (23cm) above the ground when the plates rest directly on the ground. Based on differences in body proportions, such as arm length relative to height, the joint angles required to deadlift from the floor can vary from person to person, and some lifters will have to adopt a noticeably more bent-over position than others to reach the bar. For some people, this can result in a deadlift from the floor requiring joint angles similar to those of a deficit deadlift, whereas for others it may require joint angles similar to those of a block pull or rack pull. Certain individuals may lack the hip flexion range required to deadlift from the floor without adopting a highly flexed lumbar spine to assume the starting position. Lumbar spine flexion occurs along a spectrum, and deadlifting with a controlled degree of lumbar spine flexion (which can be present even with a visibly flat back, since the lumbar spine is naturally arched in an upright resting position) is normal and not inherently injurious. However, repeatedly lifting heavy loads with excessive lumbar spine flexion may increase the risk of certain lower back injuries and may tax the lower back during recovery, potentially interfering with the performance of other exercises. When moderately elevating the bar for a block pull or rack pull, you can modify the exercise to align with your mobility and individual body proportions. With a somewhat higher starting height, you can train a very similar movement pattern to deadlifting from the floor without requiring as much hip flexion range of motion to reach the bar while keeping your lower back near a visibly flat position.

Reduced range of motion exercises like block pulls and rack pulls are often perceived as “overloading” lifts with the purpose of being able to utilize heavier loads. With a high elevation that situates the bar at or above knee level, most people can lift meaningfully heavier loads than they can from the floor, but pulling from mid-shin often has a small effect on how much weight someone can lift. When regularly training mid-shin pulls, people can often pull slightly more weight than from the ground, but some may not be able to lift a heavier load if they rely on momentum from leg drive to overcome a mid-shin sticking point. In terms of muscle contributions, the primary difference between a mid-shin elevated deadlift variation and a deadlift from the floor is that the elevated pull involves lower quad involvement. When deadlifting from the floor, the quads are most strongly utilized during the initial portion of the pull to help lift the bar off the floor, so a mid-shin pull omitting this portion is unlikely to train the quads as effectively. However, deadlifting from the floor is still unlikely to be the ideal exercise for quad development, and mid-shin pulls are likely to train your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and back muscles to a similarly effective degree as deadlifts from the floor. Consequently, if you utilize other exercises to train your quads (like squat, lunge, leg press, or leg extension variations), the reduced quad contribution from a mid-shin pull compared to a deadlift from the floor should not be problematic.
