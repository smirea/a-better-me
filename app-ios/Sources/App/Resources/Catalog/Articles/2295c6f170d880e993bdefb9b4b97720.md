If the total load is equated, holding a weight on only one side of your body or holding weights on both sides will not directly affect how the quads function as knee extensors or how the adductor magnus and gluteus maximus function as hip extensors. That is because these are actions that occur in the sagittal plane of motion, which runs from front to back and divides the body into right and left halves. However, holding only one weight on the side opposite your lead leg can increase the demands on your hip abductor muscles on the lead-leg side during split squat, lunge, and step-up variations. When holding weights on both sides, the weight opposite to your lead leg imparts a torque toward hip adduction of your lead leg, while the weight on the same side of your lead leg imparts a torque toward hip abduction of the lead leg. The weight opposite to your lead leg is situated farther away from your lead leg’s hip than the weight on the same side, so the weight opposite to your lead leg has a greater lever arm to produce hip adduction torque than the other weight’s lever arm to produce hip abduction torque. This results in net resistive hip-adduction torque acting on the lead leg, but the weight on the lead leg counteracts a portion of that torque. However, when you hold a weight only on the side opposite to your lead leg, no counterbalance force exists, requiring the hip abductors on the side of your lead leg to generate greater hip abduction torque to resist hip adduction and stabilize your pelvis.

This may result in holding one 20lb (9kg) weight on the side opposite to your lead leg, requiring your lead leg’s muscles to generate greater hip abduction torque than if you symmetrically held a 20lb (9kg) weight in each hand. In reality, though, the prime movers of the exercise that extend your knees and hips in the sagittal plane to lift your body could be similarly challenged whether you hold a 40lb (18kg) weight on one side or a 20lb (9kg) weight on each side. Consequently, if you have the knee extension and hip extension strength to use two 20lb (9kg) weights for your exercise, you may be able to challenge the hip abductor muscles of your lead leg meaningfully more when holding a single 40lb (18kg) weight on the side opposite to your lead leg. Using this offset-loading approach could allow you to train your hip abductor muscles more effectively without training your quads or hip extensor muscles any less effectively, even with the same total load. However, if the use of the offset loading limits how much total weight you can utilize due to hip abduction strength being the limiting factor, the stimulus provided to your quads and hip extensor muscles may be blunted.
When investigating EMG activity of the gluteus medius as participants carried a load in different positions, Neumann and Cook (1985) found that this primary hip abductor's activity aligned with expectations based on the aforementioned biomechanics. Holding a load on the same side of the studied gluteus medius yielded less EMG activity than when no load was held, while holding a weight on the opposite side yielded the greatest activity of any condition. Holding a weight symmetrically to the chest or on the back also induced greater gluteus medius EMG activity than the unloaded condition, but lesser activity than holding the weight on the opposite side.

As with the hip abductor muscles, the same principles discussed apply: the muscles that produce trunk side bending on the side of the lead leg will be trained more effectively when holding one weight on the opposite side. This means that muscles like the obliques, quadratus lumborum, and various intrinsic back muscles will have to work harder to prevent your trunk from bending to the side of the weight. When holding two equal weights symmetrically, the right and left weights completely counteract each other in the trunk’s frontal plane. However, the trunk side bending muscles will have to function isometrically to maintain an upright trunk when you hold a weight on only one side of your body.
