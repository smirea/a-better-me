Lat pulldowns can be performed with a variety of hand grips, including different grip widths and overhand, underhand, neutral, or hybrid (a combination of neutral and underhand or overhand) grips. Additionally, the shoulder motion occurring during a lat pulldown can be performed predominantly in the sagittal (front to back) plane, frontal (side to side) plane, or in a combination of the sagittal and frontal planes. When symmetrically moving the shoulders strictly in the sagittal plane, as would occur when lifting your arms straight out in front of you, the upper arms remain parallel to each other and do not deviate away from the mid-line of your body. Lifting your arms straight out to your sides, such as with a snow angle motion, would be an example of moving your shoulders strictly in the frontal plane. When raising your arms in the frontal plane, the upper arms move away from the mid-line of your body until they are parallel to the ground, and then they move closer to the mid-line of your body with further elevation. During the downward pulling phase of a lat pulldown, the shoulders may extend in the sagittal plane, adduct in the frontal plane, or exhibit a combination of extension and adduction in a hybrid plane between the sagittal and frontal planes. Rather than moving the shoulders exclusively through one plane, lat pulldowns are commonly performed with a technique that allows the shoulders to move in between these two planes, but one plane of shoulder motion often makes a greater contribution than the other.

Muscles acting on the shoulder joint have different leverage to generate extension torque in the sagittal plane versus adduction torque in the frontal plane. During any active movement, the nervous system is most likely to recruit the muscles best suited to produce the joint actions required for efficient movement. Consequently, determining which muscles have meaningful leverage to extend or adduct the shoulder can inform how well these muscles will be trained by a pulldown targeting a specific shoulder motion. A muscle’s leverage to contribute to a particular joint action can be quantified as its moment arm, which is the perpendicular distance between a joint’s axis of rotation and the muscle’s line of pull. When multiplied by how much force its contraction produces, the moment arm determines how much torque a muscle can generate for a particular joint action.

With any particular grip for a lat pulldown, you can intentionally rotate your shoulders in a manner that allows you to move your shoulder relatively more in the sagittal or frontal plane. However, most people find that a particular path of shoulder motion feels most comfortable and stable for their shoulders, elbows, and wrists when using a specific grip. For instance, people typically perform a wide overhand grip lat pulldown with shoulder motion predominantly in the frontal plane, whereas they typically perform a close underhand grip lat pulldown with shoulder motion predominantly in the sagittal plane. Performing either variation in the alternative plane of motion would require greater-than-normal rotational range of motion in the shoulders and forearms, and attempting to do so could feel very awkward and uncomfortable. People typically feel most comfortable when positioning themselves so that, at the portion of the lat pulldown when their upper arms are parallel to the ground, their elbows and wrists are vertically aligned or their elbows are slightly lateral (outside) of their wrists.

In general, widening a grip facilitates relatively more frontal plane shoulder motion, while narrowing a grip facilitates relatively more sagittal plane shoulder motion. An overhand, underhand, or neutral grip may be used with any grip width, but the combination of these grip variables will influence comfort and which plane of shoulder motion feels most natural. When performing a pulldown predominantly in the sagittal plane with a relatively close grip, an underhand or neutral grip is typically more comfortable than an overhand grip, but an overhand grip can still be used. Similarly, when performing a pulldown predominantly in the frontal plane with a rather wide grip, an overhand or neutral grip will typically be more comfortable and enable more frontal plane motion than an underhand grip. With a moderate width grip, people may commonly find that pulling mostly in the sagittal plane feels natural with an underhand grip, but pulling relatively more in the frontal plane feels natural with an overhand grip. Fundamentally, shoulder motion is what matters to the back muscles, and grip is relevant only insofar as it influences which shoulder motion is performed.

The lat and teres major in the back function as both primary adductors and extensors and are therefore highly likely to be trained effectively by lat pulldowns, regardless of grip or shoulder plane. However, the preponderance of available evidence indicates that the rear delt lacks meaningful leverage to contribute to shoulder adduction, but it clearly has favorable leverage to function as a shoulder extensor and, correspondingly, is likely to be trained better with a closer grip lat pulldown emphasizing shoulder extension. Alternatively, the middle and lower regions of the chest’s pectoralis major that constitute the sternoclavicular head have large moment arms to function as shoulder adductors. However, only the lower portion of the pec major exhibits a shoulder extension moment arm, and this moment arm is quite small and only present in flexed shoulder positions, like at the top of a lat pulldown. Consequently, wider grip pulldowns involving greater frontal plane shoulder motion are likely to train the pec major more effectively than closer grip pulldowns. Which regions of the lat are preferentially biased by a pulldown may also be influenced by the extent of shoulder motion in the frontal or sagittal planes. The middle and lower regions have relatively greater leverage to function as shoulder adductors, while the upper region of the lat has relatively greater leverage to function as a shoulder extensor. Plausibly, these biomechanical differences may facilitate the middle and lower regions of the lat being preferentially trained by wider grip pulldowns, while the upper region may be preferentially trained by closer grip pulldowns. That said, the extent to which any preferential biasing may occur across different regions of the lat when comparing different pulling techniques remains speculative and has not been tested in any longitudinal training interventions. While certain regions may have greater leverage than others for a particular technique, all lat regions may be recruited near their maximal capacity during a challenging set of lat pulldowns, so it is unclear whether regional biasing would occur.
