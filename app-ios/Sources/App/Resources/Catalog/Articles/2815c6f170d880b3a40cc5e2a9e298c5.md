Compared to strict pull-ups, kipping pull-ups have been measured to involve significantly lower EMG activity of the lats and biceps when five of each variation were performed by individuals with at least one year of CrossFit training experience. On the other hand, kipping pull-ups were found to produce significantly greater EMG activity of the rectus abdominis, gluteus maximus, and rectus femoris. These results should be unsurprising, since the mechanics of the kipping motion indicate that it should be largely driven by the core and hip musculature, and the momentum generated by these muscles can reduce the demands imparted onto the upper body’s pulling muscles to perform each rep.

It is worth noting that a notable difference in the intensity of a 5-rep set will exist when comparing strict pull-ups to kipping pull-ups in participants who have developed competent technique for both variations. We lack the data to know the magnitude to which the discrepancy existed for the participants of that study, but any person who is skilled at performing kipping pull-ups should be able to complete more kipping pull-ups meaningfully until they become fatigued, compared to strict pull-ups. This would result in a 5-rep set of kipping pull-ups being notably more submaximal than a 5-rep set of strict pull-ups, which could affect the reported EMG values. In practice, people typically perform higher-rep sets of kipping pull-ups than strict pull-ups, so comparing muscle EMG activity between the two variations at the same number of reps is not equivalent to comparing EMG activity during the last 5 reps of sets performed for as many reps as possible.
