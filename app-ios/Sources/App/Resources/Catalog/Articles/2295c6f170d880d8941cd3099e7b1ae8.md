### **How safety squat bar squats may differ from straight-barbell back squats**

*Key takeaway: Compared to back squatting with a straight barbell, safety squat bar squats can minimize shoulder, elbow, and wrist stress while training the lower body similarly and the upper back somewhat more.*

When comparing safety squat bar (commonly known as SSB) squats to back squats with a traditional straight barbell, the most visible difference is arm position. Straight bar squats require a meaningful amount of shoulder external rotation to hold the bar, whereas the safety squat bar allows the shoulders to remain closer to a neutral position, similar to when standing with your arms at your sides. Due to mobility limitations or prior injuries, some people experience pain or discomfort in their shoulders, elbows, or wrists when squatting with a straight bar, but feel comfortable using a safety squat bar. In some cases, discomfort with straight bar squats can be reduced by adjusting hand placement or by gradually improving shoulder mobility while managing training volume and intensity. However, if straight bar squats consistently aggravate the upper body joints, using a safety squat bar may be a sensible alternative. While a straight barbell is an extremely versatile training tool, it is not the ideal option for every individual when squatting.

Beyond reducing stress on the shoulders, elbows, and wrists, the safety squat bar positions the load farther forward than a straight bar back squat, though not as far forward as a front squat. Load placement affects leverage and can influence the technique people naturally adopt. Although individual studies differ somewhat in EMG findings, the available research consistently shows that safety squat bar squats involve a more upright average trunk position than straight bar back squats. With identical bar placement, a more upright trunk increases the resistance moment arm at the knee while reducing it at the hip and lumbar spine. However, positioning the load farther forward has the opposite effect, reducing the knee moment arm while increasing the hip and lumbar spine moment arms. Because these factors counterbalance one another, safety squat bar squats tend to be fairly similar to straight bar back squats in overall load distribution across the knee, hip, and lower back muscles, though straight bar back squats appear to involve slightly greater hip loading.

When comparing safety squat bar squats to straight barbell back squats, bar placement during the back squat is also relevant. High bar squats more closely resemble the load position of a safety squat bar than low bar squats. With a forward inclined torso, a higher bar position places the load farther in front of the hips and lower back, making safety squat bar squats more similar to high bar squats than to low bar squats. Kristiansen et al. (2021) examined high bar back squats, low bar back squats, and safety squat bar squats and found that safety squat bar squats exhibited a slightly higher knee to hip loading ratio than low bar squats, whereas they were similar to high bar squats. Although these differences exist, they do not necessarily mean that safety squat bar squats train the quadriceps meaningfully more or the glutes meaningfully less than low bar squats. Determining whether meaningful differences in muscle hypertrophy exist would require longitudinal training studies, but it is reasonable to expect broadly similar lower body muscle growth across these squat variations.

Nonetheless, the thoracic extensor muscles, which function to prevent the chest from caving through thoracic flexion while squatting, are likely trained more effectively during safety squat bar squats compared to straight bar back squats. Positioning the weight farther forward increases the lever arm of resistance acting on the thoracic spine to a relatively greater degree than on the lumbar spine, and maintaining a mildly upright trunk position is unlikely to offset this effect for the thoracic spine in the same way it may for the lumbar spine. While no studies have yet directly investigated this specific question, there is widespread agreement among lifters that safety squat bar squats impose a greater upper back demand than straight bar back squats, which aligns with expectations based on load placement. Consequently, safety squat bar squats may be a more potent means of developing thoracic extension strength than straight bar squats.

Forward positioning of the load can also make safety squat bar squats more difficult than straight bar squats when lifting the same load for the same number of reps. To date, every study that has measured one rep max or three rep max performance with both a safety squat bar and a straight barbell has found that straight bar back squats allow heavier loads to be lifted. When pooling findings from four studies, straight bar back squat one rep max or three rep max performance has been approximately 5 to 10% higher, on average, than with the safety squat bar. Kristiansen et al. (2021) reported only a 2.6% difference for the high bar back squat but a 7% difference for the low bar back squat, which is logical given that high bar positioning more closely resembles the safety squat bar. The magnitude of this difference is almost certainly influenced by training familiarity. If you have limited experience with a safety squat bar but are proficient with straight bar squatting, the gap will likely be larger than if you regularly train with a safety squat bar. Thoracic extension strength may also influence the difference. If your thoracic extension strength is high relative to your overall squat strength, the disparity between safety squat bar and straight bar back squats may be smaller than average.

Ultimately, safety squat bar squats and straight bar back squats are far more similar than different. Unless you are competing in powerlifting, where straight bar back squats are required, squatting exclusively with a safety squat bar can be a viable option if you prefer it. Conversely, if your shoulders and elbows tolerate straight bar back squats well, there is little reason to worry about missing meaningful strength or hypertrophy adaptations by sticking with the straight bar.
