When performing a bridge or hip thrust variation, you can modify your knee angle based on how far out in front of you you position your feet. Because the hamstrings function both as hip extensors and knee flexors, the knee position can affect the degree to which the hamstrings can be trained by a resisted hip extension exercise like a bridge. The hamstrings operate at short lengths when the hip is near an extended position with the knee in a rather flexed position, like during the end of the lifting phase of a glute bridge or hip thrust. When shortened to this extent, biarticular (crossing two joints) muscles, such as the hamstrings, are in a relatively disadvantaged position that can limit force production and reduce their contribution to an exercise.

While hip extension is the primary joint action, bridging with a rather flexed knee position or hip thrusting also requires the knee to extend during the lifting phase. Contraction of the biarticular hamstrings facilitates hip extension, but their activation also generates knee flexion torque that is not conducive to bridging with a meaningfully flexed knee. Consequently, the gluteus maximus may be preferentially recruited over the hamstrings to function as a hip extensor during bridge variations or hip thrusts performed with a notable degree of knee flexion.

Norikazu and Masaaki (2018) investigated how EMG activity of three biarticular hamstrings and the gluteus maximus may be affected by performing unilateral and bilateral bridges with the knee flexed to 30, 60, 90, or 120 degrees. The gluteus maximus exhibited similar EMG activity regardless of the knee position, but EMG activity for each of the biarticular hamstrings was considerably affected by the knee angle. The biceps femoris long head, semimembranosus, and semitendinosus all exhibited their highest EMG activity when bridging with 30 degrees of knee flexion, and their activity progressively decreased with each increment of greater knee flexion. A longitudinal training study comparing hypertrophy induced by hamstring bridges (with minimal knee flexion) and glute bridges (with substantial knee flexion) has not yet been conducted. However, Plotkin et al. (2023) assessed changes in the cross-sectional area of the hamstrings, quad, and gluteus maximus after untrained participants completed a nine-week intervention of barbell hip thrusts (which use a rather flexed knee angle like glute bridges but have the upper back elevated) or barbell back squats. While both exercises resulted in meaningful gluteus maximus hypertrophy, the cross-sectional area of the hamstrings did not significantly increase after hip thrusts or back squats. Squats resulted in greater quad growth than hip thrusts, but the quads still exhibited greater average growth than the hamstrings after the hip thrust intervention. While hip thrusts are not identical to glute bridges, these findings, alongside the aforementioned EMG research and mechanistic reasoning, suggest that bridge variations performed with a meaningfully flexed knee position are unlikely to train the hamstrings effectively. Alternatively, bridges performed with an extended or minimally flexed knee (i.e., hamstring bridges) are more promising variations for training the biarticular hamstrings.
