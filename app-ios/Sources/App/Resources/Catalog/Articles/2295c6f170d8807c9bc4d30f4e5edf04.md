While maintaining approximately 90 degrees of hip flexion throughout the exercise, L-sit pull-ups naturally engage the hip flexor muscles to a greater extent than standard pull-ups. Even without external load added to the legs, L-sit pull-ups position the legs in a way that maximizes their lever arm, which increases the challenge to the hip flexor muscles as they act isometrically to prevent the legs from lowering. The hip flexors work in tandem with the abdominal muscles, which are more strongly engaged during L-sit pull-ups than during standard pull-ups.

Hip flexion can occur either by the thigh moving while the pelvis remains stationary or by the pelvis rotating forward (anteriorly) while the thigh remains relatively stationary. Anterior pelvic rotation is coupled with lumbar spine extension, resulting in a sway-back posture with an exaggerated lower-back arch. When the hip flexor muscles contract, they exert force on both the pelvis and the thigh, and which segment moves depends on how well other muscles stabilize the pelvis or thigh. Contraction of the abdominal muscles can rotate the pelvis backward (posteriorly), allowing the abdominals to stabilize the pelvis and provide a stable base for the hip flexors to lift the legs. As hip flexor force increases, the abdominal muscles must contract more forcefully to lift the thighs rather than simply tilting the pelvis forward.

The three hamstring muscles that originate from the pelvis are also influenced by pelvic position. They are shortened by posterior pelvic rotation and lengthened by anterior pelvic rotation. These hamstrings are additionally lengthened by hip flexion and knee extension, which means they may not be able to elongate sufficiently to achieve an L-sit position unless the pelvis simultaneously rotates backward. As a result, posterior pelvic rotation can naturally occur when maintaining the L-sit position. Just as anterior pelvic rotation is coupled with lumbar spine extension, posterior pelvic rotation is coupled with lumbar spine flexion.

Part of the latissimus dorsi originates from the back of the pelvis. The lats are shortened with lumbar spine extension and lengthened with lumbar spine flexion. Because the L-sit position involves posterior pelvic rotation and lumbar spine flexion, the lats operate at longer muscle lengths during L-sit pull-ups than during standard pull-ups, which are typically performed with a more extended lumbar spine. Muscles have an optimal length for active force production, and when they are lengthened beyond that range, their ability to generate force can decline. In an overhead position, the lats are already lengthened by the shoulder position, and additional lengthening from lumbar flexion may further reduce their force-producing capacity.

In addition to placing greater demands on the hip flexor and abdominal muscles, L-sit pull-ups can therefore be more challenging for the lats compared to standard pull-ups. Due to these combined factors, some people may be able to perform L-sit holds and standard pull-ups separately but lack the strength to perform L-sit pull-ups.
