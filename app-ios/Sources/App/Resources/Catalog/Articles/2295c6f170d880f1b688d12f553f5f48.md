No study has yet assessed whether hip abduction exercise interventions induce hypertrophy of any particular muscles, so we must rely on other forms of evidence to determine which muscles are most likely to be effectively developed. Naturally, determining which muscles can contribute meaningfully to hip abduction in a seated position is a logical starting point. With only one exception, every muscle that has been studied to increase in size significantly after a resistance training intervention has been found to have meaningful moment arms (which quantify leverage) for the joint actions notably loaded by the exercises in the training interventions. This applies to the calves, quads, hip adductors, gluteus maximus, sartorius, triceps, elbow flexors, lateral deltoid, neck extensors, pec major, trapezius, sternocleidomastoid, and masseter. When accounting for muscles and specific muscle heads that have been studied independently rather than being collectively assessed as a group, more than 30 different muscle groups, muscles, and muscle heads have been found to increase in size significantly after a resistance training intervention. Except for the neck’s scalene muscles (which grew after a neck extension exercise intervention) every single one has evidence of having a meaningful moment arm to contribute to a joint action that was loaded by the exercise intervention prompting the hypertrophy.

The available evidence indicates that the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus have very large hip abduction moment arms in a neutral sagittal hip position (like when you are standing upright), and they undoubtedly function as primary hip abductors in such a position. However, biomechanical modeling research demonstrates that these moment arms steadily decrease as the hip flexes, becoming essentially non-existent near 90 degrees of hip flexion, at which point they have large hip internal rotation moment arms. When the hip abducts from 90 degrees of flexion, motion occurs in the transverse plane (which divides the body into upper and lower halves) rather than the frontal plane (which divides the body into front and back halves), which is where motion occurs when the hip abducts at 0 degrees of hip flexion. As a comparison to this change in a muscle’s leverage with varying joint positions and different planes of motion, we can think about how the lateral deltoid functions at the shoulder. When abducting your shoulder with your arm starting at your side (such as during a lateral raise), your lateral deltoid has excellent leverage to perform this action as you move in the frontal plane. However, if you raise your arms up in front of you to be parallel with the ground and then spread your arms apart to horizontally abduct (AKA horizontally extend) your shoulders, your lateral deltoid has lower leverage to perform this action in the transverse plane. For this reason, you would be better served performing lateral raises rather than machine reverse flies (AKA rear delt flies) if you wish to train your lateral deltoid most effectively. Similarly, the absence of any meaningful hip abduction moment in a rather flexed hip position indicates that hip abduction exercises performed while seated upright are unlikely to train the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus effectively, but other forms of evidence suggest otherwise.

When examining muscle EMG, muscles that function as prime movers during exercise typically exhibit high EMG activity when the exercise is performed at sufficiently high intensity. If a muscle lacks meaningful leverage to contribute to a particular exercise, it would be expected not to exhibit high EMG activity. For instance, the quad’s vastus lateralis does not have any hip joint moment arms, and it exhibits low EMG activity during seated abduction exercise. Similarly, the hamstring’s semitendinosus has a very small hip adduction moment arm, and it exhibits moderately low EMG activity during seated hip adduction exercise in contrast to the adductor longus, which has a very large hip adduction moment arm and exhibits high EMG activity during seated hip adduction exercise.

In contrast to this trend, Brandt et al. (2013) reported the gluteus medius to exhibit high EMG activity during high-intensity seated machine hip abduction exercise (involving 80 degrees of hip flexion) and very high EMG activity during standing banded hip abduction exercise, which was performed with a low angle of hip flexion. Paz et al. (2022) also found the gluteus medius to exhibit moderate EMG activity during a 10-rep set with 10-rep max loads of seated machine hip abduction exercise (involving 90 degrees of hip flexion) and somewhat greater EMG activity that did not significantly differ during side-lying hip abduction with the hip fully extended. Muscles sometimes exhibit meaningful EMG activity during exercises in which they do not function as agonists if their contraction can serve a stabilizing role. However, the external stability that is present when performing the seated machine hip abduction exercise reduces the likelihood that the gluteus medius would be notably engaged in this manner. While not specifically assessing the seated machine variation, Fujisawa et al. (2014) also measured EMG activity of the gluteus medius, upper gluteus maximus, lower gluteus maximus, and tensor fasciae latae during resisted isometric hip abduction performed in 0, 20, 40, 60, or 80 degrees of hip flexion. They found the gluteus medius to exhibit similar EMG activity regardless of position, the tensor fasciae latae to exhibit the greatest activity in 0-20 degrees of hip flexion, and the gluteus maximus to exhibit the greatest activity in 80 degrees of hip flexion. While the lower gluteus maximus exhibited fairly low activity even in the most flexed position, the upper gluteus maximus was markedly affected by the hip angle, as its mean activity exceeded that of any other muscle at 80 degrees of hip flexion. Like biomechanical modeling research that is used to estimate muscle moment arms, EMG research has its limitations, but the aforementioned EMG data is noteworthy nonetheless, as it suggests that the gluteus medius can still be trained by seated hip abduction exercise.

If the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus do not meaningfully contribute to hip abduction in a seated position, hip abduction torque should be significantly less when tested in 90 degrees of hip flexion compared to a neutral hip position, unless other muscles are able to substitute as abductors. When transitioning from 0-90 degrees of hip flexion, the obturator internus and superior gemellus develop meaningful hip abductor moment arms, and the piriformis has even greater leverage to function as an abductor. These deep hip muscles are most likely to be heavily engaged during seated hip abduction exercise, but they are dwarfed by the gluteal muscles. The combined physiological cross-sectional area (a key determinant of how much force a muscle can generate) of the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus is over five times greater than the combined physiological cross-sectional area of the piriformis, obturator internus, and superior gemellus. Besides those three small muscles, no other muscles have been reported to experience notable increases in hip abduction moment arms as the hip flexes, but older biomechanical models potentially may not have accurately estimated the contribution the upper gluteus maximus may have. Although they did not investigate how hip flexion angle may influence abduction moment arms, Blemker and Delp (2005) demonstrated that prior research methods likely failed to capture the hip-abduction contribution of the upper gluteus maximus. Quantitative data is lacking on how much leverage the upper gluteus maximus has to function as a hip abductor in a flexed hip position, but it is plausible that it is able to compensate for reduced abduction contributions from the gluteus medius and minimus in a seated position. Its very large cross-sectional area certainly permits it to have a high force-producing capacity, and its potentially amplified role as an abductor in a flexed hip position could explain the previously reported high EMG activity recorded by Fujisawa et al. (2014).

Hoch et al. (2025) provided further insight into this topic by testing maximal hip abduction torque with the hip in 30 degrees of flexion, neutral, or 30 degrees of hyperextension. To determine the contributions of different muscles, they utilized nerve blocks that selectively paralyzed the gluteus maximus, tensor fasciae latae, or the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus together. With the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus paralyzed, hip abduction torque was reduced by 62-63% in each position. Paralysis of the gluteus maximus decreased hip abduction torque by 44-46% in neutral or hyperextension, while torque declined by 56% in 30 degrees of flexion, indicating a greater relative contribution of the gluteus maximus in a flexed hip position. Most likely due to its small cross-sectional area, paralysis of the tensor fasciae latae reduced abduction torque by only 6-15%, with the greatest reduction occurring in 30 degrees of hip flexion. When examining the reported data, it is worth noting that part of the reduction in hip abduction torque resulting from paralysis of each muscle may be attributable to reduced hip joint stabilization, which may restrict the functional hip abductors from fully contributing. If we simply sum the reductions in hip abduction torque when comparing each of the three paralysis conditions with the pre-nerve block baseline, we obtain a negative value; therefore, the observed torque reductions do not imply that those muscles directly generate those percentages of total torque. Besides directly producing hip abduction torque, contraction of these muscles together can assist in stabilizing the hip in the sagittal and transverse planes, allowing for a more efficient expression of frontal plane torque. As an example, contraction of the gluteus maximus in 30 degrees of hip flexion generates hip external rotation torque, while contraction of the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus generates net internal rotation torque. If the gluteus maximus is inactive, the internal rotation torque from the other gluteal muscles may no longer be sufficiently counteracted to direct as much of the force from their contraction into abduction. Correspondingly, the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus may not be able to reach their level of activation during a hip abduction effort without inadvertently internally rotating the hip with the gluteus maximus completely inactive. While the gluteus maximus may not directly produce half of the total hip abduction torque, the findings from Hoch et al. (2025) nonetheless provide strong evidence that the gluteus maximus makes a notable contribution to abducting the hip.

Questions remain unanswered about how well each of the gluteal muscles will be trained by seated hip abduction exercise, but it is reasonable to predict that the upper gluteus maximus may be able to contribute most of the gluteal muscles. In addition to the aforementioned piriformis, obturator internus, and superior gemellus, the tensor fasciae latae, sartorius, and rectus femoris will likely assist the upper gluteus maximus during seated hip abduction exercise. However, the tensor fasciae latae and sartorius will be working at rather short muscle lengths in the seated position, so their contributions may be lesser compared to if the hip abduction exercise were performed at a lower angle of hip flexion. This may explain why the tensor fasciae latae has been measured to exhibit significantly lower EMG activity during machine seated hip abduction compared to side-lying hip abduction exercise. Because it functions as a knee extensor and hip flexor, the rectus femoris will be working at moderate muscle lengths when seated with flexed knees. Providing evidence of its contribution, Brandt et al. (2013) found the rectus femoris to exhibit moderate EMG activity during seated machine hip abduction. Nonetheless, this muscle will most likely be more effectively trained with hip flexion or single-joint knee extension exercises.
