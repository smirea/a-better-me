This variation combines asymmetrical weight forearm pronation and asymmetrical weight forearm supination exercises into one, which can readily be performed in part because the two exercises share the same starting position. As you transition from the neutral forearm position (with your palm facing in toward your opposite side) to your palm facing down, you are eccentrically training your supinators as your forearm pronates. When moving from the fully pronated position (palm down) to the neutral position, you are performing concentric supination. As you transition from the neutral forearm position to your palm facing up, you are eccentrically training your pronators as your forearm supinates. When moving from the fully supinated position (palm up) to the neutral position, you are performing concentric pronation.
When combining two movements that train different muscle groups together into one exercise, it is worth noting that whichever muscle group is most fatigued by the time you finish the set will likely be the muscle group that receives the greatest stimulus. Depending on which movements are paired together, you can risk one of the movements being considerably underloaded to the extent that it provides a magnitude of stimulus that is simply insufficient to promote increases in strength or muscle size. For instance, if you combine a dumbbell squat and dumbbell biceps curl into one exercise where you perform a squatting movement followed by a curling movement, you would be noticeably limited by your curling strength in all likelihood. People can normally squat markedly more weight than they curl, and the lower body muscles would not experience remotely close to the same stimulus that they could receive from a squat using loads that are not constrained by curling strength.
However, average forearm pronation and supination strength have been found to be fairly similar in the general population. When pooling the results together from nine studies that measured both pronation and supination strength, Kotte et al. (2018) found average pronation strength to be 93% of supination strength for the dominant arm and 98% of supination strength for the non-dominant arm among males. Females exhibited a mildly greater discrepancy, as their average pronation strength was 90% of their supination strength for the dominant arm and 89% of supination strength for the non-dominant arm. Correspondingly, forearm pronation strength will more likely serve as the limiting factor than supination strength during an exercise training both functions, but the difference may be fairly small. Compared with the general population, the discrepancy between supination and pronation strength is likely to be greater among lifters who commonly perform curling, vertical pulling (e.g., lat pulldown, pull-up), or rowing variations, since these exercises can strengthen the biceps. Except for the brachioradialis, which can contribute to both pronation and supination and is effectively trained along with the biceps by exercises that resist elbow flexion, the other muscles that function as forearm pronators or supinators are unlikely to be meaningfully trained by the exercises most commonly performed. Compared to the general population, lifters will consequently likely have proportionally greater forearm supination strength relative to pronation strength. If your pronation strength limits the performance of this combined exercise before you feel your supinators are meaningfully fatigued, you simply can finish the set by performing reps through the half of the movement that resists supination (i.e., alternating between the neutral start position and the palm down position). With that approach, you can ensure that both your supinators and your pronators are effectively trained by the time you finish the exercise.
