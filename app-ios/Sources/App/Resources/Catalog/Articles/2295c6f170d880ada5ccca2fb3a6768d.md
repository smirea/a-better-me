When performed with a sufficiently high relative intensity and appropriate load, machine leg extensions can increase rectus femoris size regardless of seat position; however, seat position can influence the magnitude of muscle growth. Larsen et al. (2025) assessed changes in the thickness of the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis after untrained participants trained single leg machine leg extensions for 10 weeks. For each participant, one leg was trained with an upright torso, positioning the hip into 90 degrees of flexion, while the other leg was trained with a recline torso, positioning the hip into only 40 degrees of flexion. The two interventions resulted in similar growth of the vastus lateralis, which is unsurprising since that muscle does not cross the hip joint. However, the rectus femoris experienced significantly greater growth from the leg extensions using a reclined torso position compared to the upright torso position. Because the rectus femoris functions as a hip flexor, it shortens in progressively greater angles of hip flexion, while it lengthens as the hip extends. When you are able to lean your torso back when seated, your hip is in a lower angle of flexion compared to when you are seated upright or leaned forward, so your rectus femoris can operate at a longer muscle length when you lean back. The available evidence indicates that biarticular muscles (which cross two joints) are likely to experience greater hypertrophy when trained at longer than shorter lengths, and the rectus femoris, hamstrings, calves, and triceps fit this trend.

If you wish to train your rectus femoris at longer lengths during machine leg extensions, you can intentionally position the back pad as far back as you can to allow you to lean further back at your hips when performing the exercise. With this approach, you would still want to align your knee joint with the machine’s pivot point, so you would position your thighs at the same part of the seat as you would if you had the back pad further forward to maintain an upright torso. When using the reclined technique, your upper back would still rest against the back pad, but your lower back would no longer contact the back pad, in contrast to if you positioned the seat further forward. While Larsen et al. (2025) specifically utilized a machine that allowed participants to be meaningfully reclined, many leg extensions are not constructed in a manner that would allow users to set up appropriately, with only 40 degrees of hip flexion. Consequently, the degree to which you practically can modify your hip position during a machine leg extension will likely be smaller than what was studied by Larsen et al. (2025). This may potentially result in using a reclined torso position on your leg extension machine, having a smaller effect on rectus femoris hypertrophy compared to what was reported by Larsen et al. (2025).
