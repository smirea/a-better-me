Forward and backward lunges are similar to split squats, as they target the same muscle groups and can appear identical at the bottom of the lowering phase. The distinction between them is that your feet remain stationary throughout a split squat set, except for the initial step you take into the split stance position and the step you take to reposition your feet near each other to conclude the set. With lunges, you start each rep with your feet in a symmetrical stance, step backward or forward (depending on the variation) with one foot during a rep, and then reverse the step to bring your feet back together at the end of each rep. This difference contributes to a lunge set, in which your balance is most challenged for a greater proportion of the time, since the least stable portion of either exercise occurs when one foot is briefly off the ground during a step, which occurs much less frequently during split squats. However, a lunge set also involves a greater proportion of time in which your balance can be least challenged since you are in a highly stable position whenever you are standing in a symmetrical hip-width stance, which you adopt between each lunge rep but not between each split squat rep. Correspondingly, lunges can exhibit greater fluctuations in balance demands throughout a set, with both more frequent moments of maximal balance difficulty and more frequent moments of minimal balance difficulty. In comparison, split squats typically involve a relatively constant level of moderate instability throughout most of the set, which contributes to their reputation as less challenging from a balance perspective than lunges. Nonetheless, certain individuals may find it more difficult to maintain balance during a split-squat set because they do not receive the brief “balance break” after each rep that lunges provide, when the feet are returned to a highly stable, symmetrical stance.
