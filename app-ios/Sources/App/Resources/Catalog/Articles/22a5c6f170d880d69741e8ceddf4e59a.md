McGill et al. (2009) measured EMG activity of several core and lower body muscles as competitive strongmen performed a variety of movements that appear in strongman competitions, including the farmer’s walk, which was performed for several steps with a 165lb (75kg) weight in each hand. During the farmer’s walk, peak EMG activity was high for the internal abdominal obliques, lats, thoracic erector spinae, lumbar erector spinae, gluteus medius, gluteus maximus, and rectus femoris. The external abdominal obliques and biceps femoris also exhibited moderate peak EMG activity, while the rectus abdominis exhibited low EMG activity. The abdominal muscles and rectus femoris showed peak activity during the walking portion of the carry, whereas the lats, erector spinae, and biceps femoris showed peak activity during the initial lifting phase. The gluteal muscles exhibited peak activity during the initial lifting phase and during the first step of the carry. As indicated by these findings, the farmer’s carry can recruit a large amount of musculature simultaneously, making the exercise metabolically demanding when performed at a high relative intensity, particularly for longer-duration sets. Correspondingly, challenging sets of farmer’s carries may be able to train your cardiovascular system more robustly than most resistance training exercises that localize the workload to a smaller amount of total muscle mass.
