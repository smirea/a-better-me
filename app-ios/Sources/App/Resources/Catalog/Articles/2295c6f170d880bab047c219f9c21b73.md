Cossack squats and lateral lunges (also known as side lunges) are very similar and can target the same muscle groups, much like split squats and forward or backward lunges. However, each rep of the Cossack squat begins with the feet spread apart in a wide stance, whereas each rep of a lateral lunge begins with the feet together in an approximately hip-width stance. To initiate a lateral lunge rep, you step out to the side with one foot and finish the rep by stepping back to bring your feet together. In contrast, you only step out to the side when setting up for a set of Cossack squats and only bring your feet back together at the end of the set.

When stepping into the descent of a lateral lunge, you can build greater momentum, which may increase the demands on the lower body muscles to control the descent compared with a Cossack squat. Similarly, stepping back to bring the feet together at the end of the lifting phase of a lateral lunge may facilitate a more explosive push-off than a Cossack squat, which is typically performed with a slower tempo. This difference has not been directly tested in research, but it could plausibly contribute to lateral lunges having greater transfer to change-of-direction ability in athletes who need to decelerate laterally and accelerate explosively in the opposite direction.

While lateral lunges and Cossack squats can be performed to the same depth, people commonly perform Cossack squats to a greater depth than lateral lunges. This difference in range of motion can be influenced by foot position relative to the straight knee. During lateral lunges, this foot remains flat on the floor. In contrast, Cossack squats can be performed with the foot flat or with the heel on the ground, with the rest of the foot lifted during part of the rep. Although some individuals can reach maximal depth with the entire foot flat on the ground, most can achieve greater depth by allowing increased knee and hip flexion and letting part of the foot on the straight-leg side lift off the floor. Compared with lateral lunges, people may be more likely to use the Cossack squat to improve hip mobility, and it is also common to pause at the bottom of a Cossack squat to increase stretch duration.

If the Cossack squat allows you to reach greater depth than the lateral lunge, it may also train certain lower body muscles more effectively. Kubo et al. (2019) examined changes in lower body muscle size after participants completed either a half squat intervention, reaching 90 degrees of knee flexion at end range, or a full squat intervention, reaching 140 degrees of knee flexion. The researchers found that the full squat group experienced about three times as much gluteus maximus growth and a little over twice as much hip adductor growth (including the primary hip extensor, the adductor magnus) compared with the half squat group, while quadriceps growth was similar between groups. If you can reach a meaningfully greater depth during a Cossack squat, it may similarly train the gluteus maximus and hip adductors more effectively than a lateral lunge. That said, Kubo et al. (2019) specifically studied back squats, so it remains unclear whether the same effect would apply when comparing Cossack squats and lateral lunges. Moreover, when both exercises are performed to the same depth, they are likely to elicit similar magnitudes of muscle growth.
