While the biceps is widely recognized for its elbow flexion function, it similarly serves a primary role as a forearm supinator and will correspondingly be meaningfully engaged during a resisted forearm supination exercise. As its name suggests, the supinator (a deep muscle in the upper forearm) also functions as a primary forearm supinator (which is its only joint function), but its cross-sectional area is less than that of the biceps, so the biceps is able to generate greater force. In an extended elbow position, the biceps and supinator have comparable forearm supination moment arms, but the moment arm for the biceps increases as the elbow flexes, in contrast to the moment arm of the supinator, which is not affected by elbow position. This means that the biceps will contribute the greater proportion of total supination torque in a flexed elbow position.

Research measuring maximal elbow flexion and forearm supination torque actually indicates that the biceps can generate a greater proportion of total forearm supination torque than total elbow flexion torque. In large part, this is due to the existence of the brachialis, which has favorable leverage to function as a primary elbow flexor while having a considerably greater cross-sectional area than the supinator. Correspondingly, the biceps receives more help in flexing the elbow than supinating the forearm, so its relative contribution to generating forearm supination torque can be greater.

Like the biceps, the brachioradialis can also function as an elbow flexor and a forearm supinator, but the brachioradialis has worse peak leverage for supinating the forearm, and it can actually switch to functioning as a pronator when the forearm is in a supinated position. While the brachioradialis can mildly assist in forearm supination from a pronated position, its contribution is modest, and it is meaningfully better suited to function as an elbow flexor during any curling or upper body pulling exercise.
