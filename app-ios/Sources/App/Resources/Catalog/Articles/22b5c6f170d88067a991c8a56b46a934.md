When performing a cable lateral raise, you can move your shoulders in essentially the same manner as you would during a dumbbell lateral raise. Correspondingly, these exercises train the same muscle groups and have been shown to elicit similar EMG activity in the lateral deltoid, but their resistance profiles differ. With a dumbbell lateral raise, the shoulder does not experience any resistive torque in a neutral frontal plane position (when your arm is at your side), and the resistive torque steadily increases as the shoulder abducts up to 90 degrees. This occurs because the resistance is provided by a vertically directed gravitational force acting on the dumbbell, and the perpendicular distance between the shoulder joint and the dumbbell in the frontal plane progressively increases as the arm becomes closer to being parallel with the ground.

With a cable lateral raise, resistive torque acts on the shoulder throughout the entire rep, because it is absent only when the arm is completely parallel to the cable, which never occurs during a cable lateral raise. Resistive torque in the frontal plane peaks at the position where the angle between the cable and arm reaches 90 degrees, typically in the middle range of a cable lateral raise when the cable is at its lowest setting. At the start of each rep, where the shoulder abductor muscles are at their longest lengths, these muscles will correspondingly experience meaningful (albeit submaximal when the cable is positioned to its lowest setting) tension during a cable lateral raise and minimal tension during a dumbbell lateral raise.

While exceptions exist, the available evidence indicates that training a variety of muscles at longer peak lengths, up to a certain point, is likely to promote greater hypertrophy than training at shorter peak lengths, using the same equipment. Consequently, it is rational to hypothesize that selecting a modality that maximizes resistance at the portion of an exercise where the working muscles operate at their longest lengths may induce greater hypertrophy than selecting a modality that minimizes resistance at this point. If this occurs, a cable lateral raise should be able to induce greater growth of the lateral delt than a dumbbell lateral raise. Larsen et al. (2024) investigated this matter by assigning 24 well-trained participants (with an average of seven years of lifting experience) to an eight-week intervention in which one shoulder was trained with a unilateral cable lateral raise and the other with a dumbbell lateral raise. Twice per week, they performed 5 sets of 12-16 reps until failure through the same 90-degree range of motion for each side while refraining from any other shoulder exercises that would meaningfully train the lateral delt. Following the intervention, the thickness of the lateral delt increased similarly between groups, indicating that lateral raises are likely to train this muscle just as well whether cables or dumbbells are utilized. New evidence may potentially emerge in the future suggesting otherwise, but it is quite reasonable to use whichever lateral raise variation you personally prefer in light of the data supporting their similar effectiveness for lateral delt development.
