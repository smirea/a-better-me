As the name implies, hamstring curl variations primarily train the hamstrings, including the semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris long head, and biceps femoris short head. However, exercises that resist knee flexion can effectively train other muscles, including the gracilis (a hip adductor) and the sartorius (which crosses the quadriceps on the front of the thigh). These two less commonly discussed muscles share an insertion on the medial (inner) tibia with the hamstring’s semitendinosus and correspondingly have large knee flexion moment arms that have been measured to even exceed the knee flexion moment arm of the hamstring’s biceps femoris. Although they are thin, the gracilis and, especially, the sartorius (which has the longest muscle fibers in the human body) are very long muscles because they originate from the pelvis and descend to the tibia. Together, they have been measured to have a combined muscle volume of 36% of the total hamstring volume, so developing them can allow hamstring curl variations to contribute to a greater increase in total thigh muscle size than if only the hamstrings were trained with resisted knee flexion exercises.

Given their anatomy, hamstring curls are expected to elicit meaningful hypertrophy of the gracilis and sartorius; however, well-designed training intervention studies provide further robust evidence that this is indeed the case. Maeo et al. (2020) found both the gracilis and sartorius to increase in size after participants completed a 12-week training intervention exclusively involving seated hamstring curls or lying hamstring curls. In a later study, Maeo et al. (2024) also found these two muscles to increase in size after participants completed a 12-week training intervention exclusively involving Nordic hamstring curls or seated accentuated eccentric hamstring curls. When averaging the increases in volume measured after each of the four interventions, the gracilis and semitendinosus responded remarkably well to the resisted knee flexion exercises, with increases of 24% and 22%, respectively. The specific variations that stimulated the greatest or least hypertrophy differed among the muscles, but the sartorius, biceps femoris long head, and biceps femoris short head also grew similarly overall by averages of 11-12% after the interventions. Of the six muscles (when considering the two heads of the biceps femoris to be distinct muscles) measured, the semimembranosus tended to grow the least, as indicated by its volume increasing by an average of 7% when pooling the results of the four different hamstring curl interventions together.
