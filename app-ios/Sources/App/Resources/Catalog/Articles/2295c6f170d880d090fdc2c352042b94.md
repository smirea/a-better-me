*Key takeaway: To train your lower body and back muscles most effectively, use a grip that does not limit grip strength during this deadlift variation.*

The double overhand grip described in the setup for this deadlift variation can feel natural, and it is intuitive for people who are fairly new to the exercise. However, as lifters become increasingly proficient with deadlift variations, grip strength often becomes the limiting factor with the double-overhand approach. With any grip, using a towel to ensure your hands are dry before the set and applying chalk can meaningfully improve your ability to hold the bar. Nonetheless, it can be very challenging to maintain a double-overhand grip once you reach a particular weight, and your lower-body and back muscles will not be trained as effectively by a grip-limited deadlift variation. As an alternative to the double overhand, you can use a mixed grip (also known as an alternate grip), a hook grip, or lifting straps, while otherwise using the same technique described on this exercise page.

With a mixed grip, you use an overhand grip (with the palm facing backward) with one hand and an underhand grip (with the palm facing forward) with the other hand, which makes it more difficult for the bar to roll out of your hands. The mixed grip, widely used by powerlifters and recreational lifters, can feel somewhat awkward at first, but it typically does not take long to adapt and feel comfortable pulling with it. While the probability is low, the primary con of a mixed grip is that the distal (near the elbow) biceps tendon on the side of the underhand grip may be at increased risk of injury during deadlifts compared with symmetrical grips. Empirical data on the matter is unavailable, but there is reason to think that the risk of this injury would be elevated if a deadlift is initiated with a slight elbow bend on the underhand side in a “grip and rip” style. Doing so could result in that elbow rapidly extending under a very heavy load, imparting considerable eccentric loading into a stretched distal biceps tendon. With any grip, you should initiate the pull with fully straightened elbows, but this is especially important with the mixed grip. Besides prioritizing this technical component, sensible programming is also likely to reduce the risk of a biceps tendon further when pulling with a mixed grip. If you are new to using a mixed grip or have taken a fairly long break from it, it may not be prudent to jump right into a maximal pull instead of progressively increasing the intensity over time to give your biceps tendon time to adapt. While recognizing that the risk of biceps tendon injury cannot be completely eliminated when pulling very heavy loads with a mixed grip, the risk can be extremely low for the vast majority of lifters who use high-quality technique and reasonable progressions, so there is no need to fear using a mixed grip. This is especially the case for females, given that 95% of reported distal biceps tendon injuries have occurred in males, with middle-aged males who use tobacco appearing to have the greatest injury risk based on retrospective data.

Some people have also expressed concern about the risk of developing side-to-side muscle asymmetry with a mixed grip, but there is little reason to think that any problematic muscle imbalance is likely to develop. If your training exclusively involves mixed-grip pulls, without any other back exercises such as rows or pulldowns, mixed-grip pulls may contribute to a slight muscle imbalance, but this should not be an issue if you perform additional exercises. While assessing the upper trap and upper lat, Beggs (2011) found these back muscles to exhibit similar EMG activity between sides when participants deadlifted with a mixed-grip, as was true when using a double overhand grip. Beggs found the biceps on the underhand side to exhibit greater EMG activity than the biceps on the overhand side with the mixed grip, but deadlift variations will not meaningfully develop your biceps regardless, so this asymmetrical EMG activity should be inconsequential. If you are still concerned about the potential to develop a minuscule muscle imbalance, you also have the option of switching which side you use for the underhand grip from set to set or from session to session, but such an approach is quite unlikely to make any noticeable difference.

An alternative to the mixed grip is the hook grip, which involves sandwiching your thumbs between the bar and your other fingers instead of placing your thumbs over your other fingers (further away from the bar) as you would do with a mixed grip or standard double overhand grip. The hook grip allows you to use a symmetrical double overhand grip, so it likely imparts a lower risk of distal biceps tendon injury than the mixed grip while allowing you to train both sides in the exact same positions. Especially for people with relatively long fingers, the hook grip can provide a very stable grip once acclimated, making it advantageous for competitive strength athletes. For weightlifters who perform the snatch and clean and jerk (which cannot be performed with a mixed grip), the hook grip is the sole grip of choice when competing, and a proportion of powerlifters also deadlift with the hook grip due to how secure it can be. However, if you have no plans of competing in a weightlifting or powerlifting meet, the key tradeoff of the hook grip will most likely be greater than the benefits it provides compared to using a mixed grip or straps. That trade-off is the pronounced thumb pain caused by crushing your thumbs against the bar. Over time, your thumbs can become desensitized to some degree (likely due to nerve endings being destroyed), but the hook grip can remain intensely uncomfortable for several months of training. For this reason, most recreational lifters do not find the hook grip to be worth using over the mixed grip or straps when performing deadlift variations.

While not permitted in powerlifting competitions, straps can be valuable training tools, as they help ensure grip strength does not limit your pulling performance when used appropriately. When utilizing straps, both of your palms will be facing backward, like with a double overhand grip, which should likely result in a lower risk of biceps tendon injury than a mixed grip. Utilizing straps also limits your hand skin from restricting your pulling volume. When a bar has aggressive knurling that pulls on your hand skin, you can only grip it directly for a limited volume before you risk tearing a callus, so straps can be a useful way to circumvent that issue. In part due to this advantage, strength athletes who compete with mixed or hook grips commonly use straps for a portion of their total training volume. Accompanying the pro of ensuring grip strength does not limit your pulling performance is the con of not training your forearm and hand gripping muscles as effectively when pulling with straps. However, most people do not perform deadlift variations specifically to target these gripping muscles, and you can likely increase forearm size more effectively with isolation exercises such as wrist curls. Additionally, you can still train your static grip strength with horizontal and vertical pulling exercises like rows and pull-ups, which will train your grip strength with lighter loads than many deadlift variations that reduce the likelihood of grip strength limiting the stimulus provided to other muscles. If you wish to train your grip strength further, you can also perform exercises like dead hangs, farmer’s carries, or suitcase carries for additional grip strengthening. When using straps for work sets of a deadlift variation, you can also use a double overhand grip for your warm-up sets before incorporating straps. Once you have reached a weight you cannot firmly hold with a double overhand grip, you can incorporate straps.

Sometimes it is clear that grip strength limits your performance on deadlift variations without straps, which suggests your lower-body and spinal extensor muscles are not being trained as effectively as they could be. However, it is not always evident what precisely limits your performance, which may warrant a quick personal experiment. As a simple approach, if you are unsure whether grip strength is limiting your performance, perform a set of as many quality reps as you can with a particular weight without using straps, then compare your rep total to a set using straps. To perform an appropriate comparison, ensure you use the same technique across conditions, with the same range of motion and tempo. Additionally, you would want to perform each condition when you are similarly recovered. Practically, resting for at least five minutes between the two sets should allow most fatigue to dissipate by the time you perform the second set, thereby enabling a useful comparison of how the performance of this exercise may be affected by your grip strength. However, if you really wish to optimize the validity of your self-assessment, you can compare your rep performance between these conditions on a second day when you reverse the order of the conditions. If you performed the set without straps on the first day, perform the set with straps before the set without straps on a different day to control for any fatigue from the first set that may influence the second. When comparing how many reps you complete during each condition, you will be well-informed as to how grip strength may be affecting the performance of this exercise for you as an individual.
