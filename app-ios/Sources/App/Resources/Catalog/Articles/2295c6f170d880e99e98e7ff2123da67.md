Aside from using a straighter knee position, the technique for the straight knee leg raise is essentially the same as for the bent knee leg raise, so both variations primarily train the same muscle groups. In terms of training effect, the main difference is that the straight knee leg raise is a higher-intensity variation because it shifts the center of mass of the legs farther forward relative to the trunk and hips. This increases the lever arm of resistance acting on the trunk and hips, which requires the abdominal muscles and hip flexor muscles to generate greater trunk flexion and hip flexion torque, respectively. Resistive torque can be increased either by increasing the load or by increasing the lever arm through which that load acts on a joint, so the straight knee leg raise functions similarly to a weighted knee raise without requiring additional external weight. This greater resistance likely explains why McGill et al. (2014) found noticeably higher abdominal EMG activity during straight knee leg raises compared to bent knee leg raises when participants performed three reps of each exercise.

However, this does not mean that the straight knee leg raise is inherently superior to the bent knee leg raise for developing the abdominal muscles, just as squatting 200 lb (91 kg) is not necessarily better than squatting 150 lb (68 kg) for inducing quadriceps hypertrophy. If you have the strength to perform both variations with sound technique, you simply need to perform more reps per set with the bent knee variation to achieve a similar hypertrophic stimulus. When an equal number of sets is performed to a similar level of fatigue (for example, performing as many reps as possible in each condition), comparable muscle growth can occur across a wide range of rep counts. Accordingly, the bent knee variation can be just as effective for abdominal development as the straight knee variation for most people when sets are performed with a similar number of reps in reserve. A potential exception may be individuals with especially strong abdominals who can perform very high numbers of bent knee leg raise reps before substantial fatigue, indicating that the resistance is very low relative to their strength. At some point, resistance may be too low to optimally stimulate muscle growth, even when a set is taken to failure; however, the exact threshold is unclear. In practice, this is unlikely to be a concern unless you can perform more than about 30 controlled reps per set, which will not apply to most people.
