If you want to maximize your ratio of pec major engagement to front delt engagement, a standing machine chest fly may be difficult to beat. Both the pec major and front delt have large moment arms for horizontally flexing the shoulder, which is the movement that occurs when bringing the arms toward midline while they are elevated to where they form 90-degree angles with the torso (like during a pec deck fly or flat dumbbell fly). Shoulder horizontal flexion occurs in the transverse plane, which is the plane of motion that divides the body into upper and lower halves.

As with other chest flies, a standing machine chest fly trains shoulder horizontal flexion, but it more so trains shoulder adduction in the frontal plane, which is the plane of motion that divides the body into front and back halves. If your arms are elevated and form a “T-position” with your torso without your elbows being in front of your shoulders (like during part of a snow angel), shoulder adduction is the movement that brings your arms down and in toward your sides, while shoulder abduction is the motion that sweeps your arms out away from your sides. The sternocostal region of your pec major, constituting your middle and lower chest, has meaningful leverage to function as a shoulder adductor, so it is very well suited to function as the prime mover during a standing machine chest fly.

In contrast, the front delt has notable leverage for functioning as a shoulder abductor in the frontal plane, which is why it can assist the side delt during a lateral raise. While its ability to generate shoulder horizontal flexion torque would be beneficial during a standing machine chest fly, the shoulder abduction torque its contraction simultaneously generates is counterproductive during this exercise. Since a greater degree of shoulder motion occurs in the transverse plane than the frontal plane during a standing machine chest fly (so long as you do not excessively lean forward), it would be particularly inefficient for the front delt to be heavily engaged throughout this variation. The front delt will correspondingly provide minimal assistance to the pec major during a standing machine chest fly, in contrast to most fly chest fly variations that meaningfully train both muscles.
