When performing lunges, you can alternate lead legs every rep within one set, or you can continuously perform each rep with one lead leg as a set before performing another set where you use your other side as the lead leg for all of its reps. Either approach can be used to train your lower body effectively, but one strategy may be preferable for you based on the context.

The main advantage of the alternating approach is that you can train each side of your body with a similar intensity in a time-efficient manner. If you continuously lunge with one leg in the lead position, your rear leg will experience fatigue by the end of the set because it is still contributing to a non-negligible degree. For your rear leg to be close to fully recovered before lunging continuously with it as the lead leg, you will have to rest after the set that just trained your other side in the lead position. If you do not rest between these sets, the leg that you train second in the lead position will be unlikely to match the first set’s reps with the same proximity to failure if your legs are similarly strong. Resting between sets with the continuous approach will let your two legs be similarly fresh for their sets as the lead leg, but the additional rest time can contribute to a longer duration being required to complete the same number of reps per lead leg compared to the alternating approach. With greater time-efficiency, the alternating strategy can help you complete your workout more quickly or have more time to complete additional training within the workout window you have.

The alternating approach can also pose a greater peak cardiovascular demand with its longer set duration than the continuous approach, which may be viewed as a pro or a con based on the individual. If you find that, rather than the strength/strength endurance of your lower body muscles, your cardiovascular fitness limits your lunge performance with the alternating strategy, it may not be stimulating increases in lower body strength or size as effectively as the continuous technique. Whether this may happen will be influenced by an individual’s cardiovascular fitness and the rep range used for lunges. Performing a greater number of reps per lead leg in a set will naturally be more cardiovascularly demanding, so some people may prefer to avoid high-rep sets when lunging with the alternating approach to prevent the cardiovascular demand from limiting performance. Sets of lunges performed close to failure with 20-25 reps per lead leg can be exhausting with the alternating strategy, but performing 8-10 reps per lead leg may feel much more manageable.
