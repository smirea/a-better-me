Lying dumbbell biceps curls and incline dumbbell biceps curls both can train the biceps at longer lengths than seated or standing biceps curls because the biceps reaches a longer length in an extended shoulder position (when the elbow is behind the torso) compared to a neutral shoulder position (when the elbow is by the side). While lying dumbbell biceps curls can be performed with variable shoulder angles, they are commonly performed with a shoulder position fairly similar to an incline curl.

With an incline dumbbell curl, the upper arms can hang straight down perpendicular to the ground, which can position the shoulders in about 45 degrees of extension beyond the neutral position when using a 45-degree incline bench. When performing a lying curl, the shoulders would be meaningfully more extended if the upper arms were also directed straight down perpendicular to the ground, but the conventional technique for this variation involves keeping the upper arms angled diagonally rather than vertically. Lifters with short arms can maintain a more extended shoulder position without the dumbbells contacting the ground compared to lifters with longer arms, but people can generally maintain a position of moderate shoulder hyperextension that is comparable to a 45-degree incline curl. If the same shoulder and elbow angles are used for both variations, the biceps will function at the same lengths during each type of curl.

However, the resistance profile differs between these two exercises. In the starting position, the dumbbell is situated vertically under the elbow with a vertical forearm during an incline curl, but the dumbbell is positioned horizontally in front of the elbow with a diagonally angled forearm during a lying curl. Because gravity exerts its force straight downward, the lever arm of resistance provided by a dumbbell is equivalent to the horizontal distance between the dumbbell and the center of the elbow joint, and this lever arm is greatest when the forearm is parallel to the ground. This means that the lever arm of resistance will be meaningfully greater during the initial range of lying curl compared to an incline curl.

Because the resistive torque acting on a joint is the product of the lever arm of the resistive force acting on a joint and the magnitude of the resistive force (which is determined by how heavy the weight is in this context), resistive torque will correspondingly be greater earlier in the range of a lying curl relative to an incline curl. This results in the biceps experiencing greater resistance in its most lengthened position when performing a lying curl, despite the biceps being able to work at similar lengths during the two different curls.

Thus far, no study has yet assessed muscle hypertrophy after a lying curl training intervention, let alone compared it to a different biceps curl training intervention, so insufficient evidence is available to draw any firm conclusions about whether lying curls may stimulate distinct adaptations relative to other curls. Due to their ability to provide notable resistance to the elbow flexor muscles at the position in which the biceps are meaningfully lengthened, it is theoretically plausible that lying curls may stimulate a mildly greater degree of elbow flexor muscle hypertrophy than other curl variations. However, any potential difference is speculative at this moment.
