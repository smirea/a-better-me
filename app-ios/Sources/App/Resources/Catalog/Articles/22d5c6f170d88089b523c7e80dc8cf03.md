Exercises that resist knee extension exclusively, such as machine leg extensions, or that primarily load knee extension while imparting minimal hip extensor loading (e.g., reverse Nordic curls and sissy squats), can serve a distinct role in a training program. A wide array of exercises can train knee and hip extension simultaneously, including squats, lunges, step-ups, and leg presses. These multi-joint exercises can certainly stimulate meaningful increases in the size and strength of the quads, but they are not ideal for training the rectus femoris, the quad muscle that crosses the hip and functions as a hip flexor and a knee extensor. Notably, the rectus femoris does not just weakly assist in flexing the hip, but rather functions as a primary hip flexor that has greater leverage for producing hip flexion torque than any other muscle in the human body at certain joint angles. Because contraction of the rectus femoris can produce a substantial amount of hip flexion torque, its recruitment during a movement that requires both hip and knee extension is less biomechanically efficient than recruitment of the other muscle bellies of the quadriceps. As the rectus femoris contracts more forcefully during a movement such as the squat, the hip extensors must work harder to resist the hip flexion torque it generates. Striving to maximize efficiency, the nervous system preferentially recruits the three monoarticular (crossing only the knee joint) muscles of the quads to a greater extent than the rectus femoris during compound exercises in which extension occurs simultaneously at the hip and knee joints.

Although some research indicates that the rectus femoris does not increase significantly with multi-joint exercises such as squats, other studies have reported statistically significant increases in this muscle following such training among participants new to lifting. When a relatively small magnitude of growth occurs (as is the case even in studies that achieve statistical significance), it is unsurprising that the change is statistically significant in some studies but not in others. While the rectus femoris will be trained to a degree during these types of multi-joint exercises, the available body of evidence indicates that the monoarticular quad muscles are much more likely to experience a robust hypertrophy response. Correspondingly, your rectus femoris will almost certainly lag behind your other quad muscles if all of your quad training is performed through multi-joint exercises like squats.

However, during a single joint knee extension exercise where no movement occurs at the hip joint, the rectus femoris is just as active as the monoarticular muscles of the quad, if not more so. This is logical, since the hip flexion torque generated by rectus femoris contraction is essentially inconsequential during an exercise that does not simultaneously resist the hip extensor muscles. Congruent with what would be expected, machine seated leg extensions have been demonstrated to produce a considerable degree of hypertrophy of the rectus femoris that is similar to or even more pronounced than that of the three monoarticular quad muscles. While less studied than machine leg extensions, reverse Nordic curls have been shown to produce significant increases in rectus femoris size, and the biomechanically similar sissy squat would likely yield a similar result, although it has not been directly studied.
