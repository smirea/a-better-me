This exercise is referred to as a “back extension” as an alternative name in this database because it is most commonly called that, or a “hyperextension.” However, this variation is performed as a hip hinge, dynamically training hip extension while keeping the lumbar (lower back) and thoracic (middle and upper back) spine rigidly static throughout the set. Accordingly, it would be more precise to call this exercise a “Roman chair hip hinge” or “Roman chair hip extension,” especially to differentiate it from another Roman chair exercise. In contrast to hinging at the hips, you can alternatively keep your hips stationary and intentionally move through dynamic trunk flexion (as your back rounds) during the lowering phase and dynamic trunk extension (as your back straightens) during the lifting phase. This exercise is also commonly referred to as a “back extension” or “hyperextension,” but it produces a distinctly different training effect.

When performing the hip hinge variation on the Roman chair, the training effect should be similar to other hip hinge exercises such as Romanian deadlifts. During these movements, the primary hip extensor muscles (gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and the three hamstrings that cross the hip) function dynamically as the prime movers, while the trunk extensor muscles (such as the erectors spinae) act isometrically to resist trunk flexion and maintain a relatively fixed trunk position. Although studies directly comparing these two groups have not yet been conducted, other lines of evidence suggest that hip hinge exercises provide a greater hypertrophic stimulus to the hip extensor muscles than to the trunk extensor muscles. In contrast, the Roman chair spinal extension variation, which involves dynamic trunk motion with a fixed hip position, is likely more effective for stimulating increases in trunk extensor muscle size while not meaningfully training the hip extensor muscles. With this variation, the trunk extensors can be loaded eccentrically and concentrically at long muscle lengths, making trunk extension strength the primary limiting factor. While the trunk extensor muscles are still active during hip hinge exercises, performance is often limited by hip extension strength, and the trunk extensors may not be loaded at long lengths, which may be suboptimal for hypertrophy. Neither variation is inherently superior. Understanding their differences can help you choose the option that best aligns with your specific training goals.
