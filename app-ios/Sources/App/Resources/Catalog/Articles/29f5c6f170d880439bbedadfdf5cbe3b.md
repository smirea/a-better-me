Compared to most curl variations, preacher curls provide greater stability from the arm support that can essentially isolate elbow motion by restricting motion from other joints, such as the shoulders and hips. For instance, when performing a standing curl with free weights (such as dumbbells, barbells, or EZ bars), someone can swing their elbows forward through shoulder motion or generate momentum from their hips. Seated dumbbell curls do not use lower body momentum, but still do not prevent someone from using shoulder motion during the curl, since the upper arm is not externally supported. Using a strict technique in which only elbow motion occurs during a curl does not necessarily result in greater muscle growth, but it can make it easier to track progress over time and reduce fatigue.

In addition to the enhanced stability support that reinforces strict technique, preacher curls exhibit a different resistance profile than most other curl variations. During any curl using free weights, resistance from gravity acting on the primary elbow flexor muscles (i.e., biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis) peaks when the forearm is parallel to the ground. At this point, the horizontal distance between the weight and elbow is maximized, which results in the greatest lever arm of resistance acting on the elbow. On the other hand, resistance to the elbow flexor muscles is absent when the forearm is perpendicular to the ground. When you keep your upper arms by your sides during a free weight curl, resistance is correspondingly the greatest when the elbow is flexed by 90 degrees and the least when the elbow is fully extended in the starting position of each rep. With a preacher curl, the flexed shoulder position from the arm pad means that the forearm is not directed straight down toward the ground in the starting position when the elbow is fully extended. This results in the elbow flexor muscles experiencing greater resistance during the initial portion of the lifting phase compared to free weight curls performed with the arms situated by the sides, where the shoulders are in a neutral position.

While far from conclusive, the greater resistance preacher curls impart in an extended elbow position may allow them to train the brachialis and brachioradialis somewhat more effectively than other curls that lack resistance in this position. The brachialis and brachioradialis do not cross the shoulder joint, so they experience greater resistance at long muscle lengths during preacher curls compared to curls that position the elbow in a neutral position (like a seated curl) or extended position (like an incline curl or Bayesian curl). However, both the long head and the short head of the biceps originate from the scapula and have shoulder flexion moment arms that result in them being shortened in a flexed shoulder position compared to a neutral or extended shoulder position. The available evidence indicates that training a variety of muscles at longer peak lengths, up to a certain point, is likely to promote greater hypertrophy than training at shorter peak lengths. Consequently, curls performed with an extended shoulder position have the potential to train the biceps mildly more effectively than preacher curls.

Attarieh et al. (2025) provided direct insight into this matter by assigning 15 untrained men to a 10-week intervention training single arm underhand grip cable curls twice per week for 3-5 sets of 8-12 reps to failure. For each participant, one arm performed a preacher curl facing toward the cable station with 50 degrees of shoulder flexion, while the other arm performed a Bayesian curl through the same elbow range of motion, facing away from the cable station with 50 degrees of shoulder extension. Assigning one arm of each participant to a different curl variation helps control for many individual factors (e.g., genetics, diet, lifestyle, prior training history) that can confound results when each person is assigned to only one exercise. Following the intervention, the thickness of both the biceps and brachialis similarly increased for the two curl groups. The absence of any difference for the brachialis is not particularly surprising, given that it does not cross the shoulder joint. However, similar biceps growth across the two exercises suggests that the biceps may respond differently to lengthened training than other biarticular (i.e., crossing two joints) muscles. Other biarticular muscles like three of the hamstrings, the calf’s gastrocnemii, the quad’s rectus femoris, and the long head of the triceps have been reported to experience significantly greater growth when trained with a variation that loads them at longer lengths compared to a variation that loads them at shorter lengths. While the difference was not statistically significant, it is worth noting that the average increase in biceps size still slightly favored the Bayesian curl. The preacher curl group increased mean thickness at the proximal (closest to the shoulder), middle, and distal (closest to the elbow) sites of the biceps, respectively, by 6%, 7%, and 7%, while the preacher curl group increased thickness by 9% at all three sites. On the other hand, the brachialis increased thickness by an average of 8% in the Bayesian curl group and by 10% in the preacher curl group. These small differences may reflect a combination of chance and measurement error; however, additional evidence indicates that curling from an extended shoulder position may train the biceps more effectively than curling from a flexed shoulder position.

In a study presented as a conference abstract that did not undergo peer review, Kobayashi et al. (2024) described an experiment similar to that conducted by Attarieh et al. (2025). Kobayashi et al. (2024) assigned 21 untrained participants to a 12-week intervention training single arm dumbbell curls twice per week for 5 sets of 10 reps each session. For each participant, one arm performed a preacher curl with 50 degrees of shoulder flexion, while the other arm performed an incline curl with 50 degrees of shoulder extension. After the intervention, the authors reported that the incline and preacher curl groups, respectively, increased biceps volume by 18% and 14.9%, which was a statistically significant difference between groups. However, the preacher curl group experienced a 21% increase in brachialis size, which exceeded the 17% increase for the incline curl group. Similarly, the brachioradialis increased its volume by a greater degree for the preacher curl group (13%) than the incline curl group (10%). Whenever a study is presented only as an abstract not submitted for peer review in a journal, the validity of its results should be appraised with a healthy dose of skepticism. Nonetheless, Kobayashi et al. (2024) included renowned researchers in this field who have produced high quality peer-reviewed publications, so their findings are worth mentioning despite the limitations from this form of evidence.

Kassiano et al. (2025) also compared the effects of training dumbbell preacher curls at approximately 45 degrees of shoulder flexion with dumbbell incline curls at approximately 30 degrees of shoulder extension. They assigned 63 untrained women to either group, which performed 3 sets of 8-12 reps until failure twice per week for eight weeks. In contrast to Attarieh et al. (2025) and Kobayashi et al. (2024), Kassiano et al. (2025) measured elbow flexor thickness of the biceps and brachialis combined at three different upper arm sites, so we cannot cleanly differentiate whether these two muscles may have individually responded. Following the intervention, the thickness of the proximal site increased by 11.2% for the preacher curl group, exceeding the 8.3% increase for the preacher curl group. While the two groups experienced similar increases in middle site muscle thickness, the preacher curl group's increase in thickness of 10.3% at the distal site was greater than the incline curl group’s 6.4% increase at this distal site. While speculative in nature, these regional differences potentially may be influenced by the biceps reflecting a greater proportion of total elbow flexor muscle thickness at the proximal site than the distal site, which is where the brachialis may be thicker.

If the biceps grew more from incline curls, it would be sensible that the total elbow flexor muscle thickness would favor the incline curl group at the proximal site, most reflective of biceps size relative to brachialis size. Similarly, it would be sensible that the total elbow flexor muscle thickness would favor the preacher curl group at the distal site, most reflective of brachialis size relative to biceps size, if the brachialis grew more from preacher curls. More likely than the shoulder position itself, the resistance profile from free weight preacher curls may contribute to the brachialis potentially being trained somewhat more effectively by these curls involving a flexed shoulder position. Even though the brachialis reaches the same lengths during each variation, the ability to be meaningfully loaded at its longest length during a preacher curl potentially may amplify the stimulus provided to this muscle when using free weights. This may also explain why Kobayashi et al. (2024) found that the brachioradialis experienced greater growth during the dumbbell preacher curl.

While the resistance profile may have an effect, its role remains speculative at this time, and we cannot draw strong conclusions about the differential effects of training curls from a flexed versus an extended shoulder position. There is reason to think that curls with an extended shoulder position may train the biceps mildly more effectively than curls with a flexed shoulder position, while curls that have tension at the start of each rep (like preacher curls) may be ideal for training the brachialis and brachioradialis. However, the evidence is insufficient to support either possibility with a high degree of confidence. Nonetheless, you can be confident that curls effectively train your primary elbow flexor muscles, regardless of whether they involve an extended or flexed shoulder position and whether they use high resistance at the start of each rep. These variables may have some effect, but curl variations are far more similar than different, and you have many viable options from which to select to develop your arms.
