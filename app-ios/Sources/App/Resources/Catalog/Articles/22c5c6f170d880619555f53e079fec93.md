As their names suggest, the primary function of the pronator teres and pronator quadratus is forearm pronation, so they can be most effectively developed through a resisted pronation exercise. While the pronator teres has a small elbow flexion that may allow curls to train it to some degree, the pronator teres and pronator quadratus are unlikely to be trained particularly well by any other type of exercise besides pronation exercises. Consequently, including at least one pronation exercise in your program will be important if you prioritize increasing the size and strength of these two muscles. Training the two pronator muscles directly may be invaluable for maximizing arm wrestling performance and beneficial when rehabbing golfer’s elbow (a type of tendinopathy often involving the pronator teres), but they are otherwise not especially noteworthy for typical physique or strength goals. Together, the pronator teres and pronator quadratus have been measured by Holzbaur et al. (2007) to have a combined volume that is only about 9% of the total forearm muscle volume. Consequently, they are noticeably smaller than the wrist flexor muscles that constitute close to half of the forearm’s muscle volume or even the wrist extensor muscles that reflect about one fifth of the forearm musculature. Nonetheless, pronation exercise will likely be required to maximize forearm muscle size since the two pronator muscles will otherwise not be robustly trained by other movements.

While the pronator teres and pronator quadratus may be trained the most effectively by pronation exercise, three other forearms also have leverage to generate pronation torque with the flexed elbow position and forearm joint angles that are utilized during this exercise. These additional muscles are the brachioradialis, flexor carpi radialis, and palmaris longus, which have a combined volume equivalent to about one fifth of the total forearm muscle volume. How effectively they can be developed by a pronation exercise is unclear, but data from Bremer et al. (2006) indicates that they can have leverage to pronate the forearm of magnitudes that are similar to the pronator teres and pronator quadratus over certain joint angle ranges. When tested in neutral or supinated forearm positions, Bader et al. (2017) also found the flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, and brachioradialis to exhibit significantly greater EMG activity during maximal isometric (static) forearm pronation compared to isometric supination, further supporting the pronation function these muscles may have. While these three muscles should be engaged during this pronation exercise, each has better leverage when trained through different movements. In particular, the brachioradialis has much better leverage to function as a primary elbow flexor, allowing it to be trained effectively by curl variations. On the other hand, the flexor carpi radialis and palmaris longus have better leverage to function as wrist flexors, allowing them to be trained effectively by wrist curl variations, which will train a greater volume of forearm musculature than pronation exercise.
