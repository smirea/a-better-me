Consistently performing the lowering phase of each rep through your full ankle dorsiflexion range may help this exercise function as a form of loaded stretching and increase your available ankle dorsiflexion in a flexed knee position. Weight training may not have the same reputation for flexibility as static stretching, but training through a full range of motion that loads the working muscles at long lengths can improve flexibility just as well while improving your strength in those positions to a much greater degree. When pooling the results of all relevant studies in a systematic review and meta-analysis, Alizadeh et al. (2023) found that resistance training and static stretching interventions yielded similar increases in available range of motion. In addition to performing each rep of your seated calf raise through a full range of motion, you also can finish a set with a bout of sustained static stretching (such as for about 30-45 seconds) in the bottom position to amplify the ankle mobility stimulus even further. If you opt to do so, be aware that adding this sustained stretch may reduce how many reps you can complete on subsequent calf raise sets when using the same load.

Of any ankle motion, ankle dorsiflexion range of motion is generally the most relevant for lifters, and lifters reporting to have “tight ankles” are normally referring to having limited ankle dorsiflexion range of motion. This is because ankle dorsiflexion range of motion can meaningfully affect someone’s technique when performing squatting variations, along with exercises involving similar lower body movement like leg pressing. Ankle dorsiflexion range of motion, which is measured as the angle between the tibia and foot, determines how far someone’s knees can track forward when squatting down while keeping the heels in contact with the ground. If someone reaches the end of their available ankle dorsiflexion range of motion while squatting, descending further may require a more horizontal trunk position and greater hip flexion range of motion. This is not necessarily problematic, but the lifter may also reach the end range of hip flexion before achieving the desired depth. Consequently, squat depth can be limited, and a lifter may be more likely to experience “butt wink,” where the pelvis rotates posteriorly (backward) in a manner that is accompanied by dynamic lumbar spine flexion (lower back rounding). While not inherently injurious, repeatedly experiencing “butt wink” under conditions of high loading can be provocative of low back pain for some individuals.

Ankle dorsiflexion range of motion is not the only variable that can affect squat depth, the degree of horizontal trunk lean, and the presence of “butt wink,” but a limitation in this ankle motion commonly serves as a contributing factor. Ankle dorsiflexion range of motion differs depending on the degree to which the knee is flexed because the calf’s gastrocnemii muscles cross the back of the knee joint and are lengthened as the knee straightens and/or as the ankle dorsiflexes. When the knee is fully straight, ankle dorsiflexion range of motion will normally be limited by the flexibility of the gastrocnemii, which can be indicated by feeling a stretching sensation in the back of the knee. Performing straight-knee calf raise variations into end range ankle dorsiflexion at the bottom of each rep can increase the flexibility of the gastrocnemii and enhance ankle dorsiflexion range of motion in this knee position. However, flexibility of the gastrocnemii does not limit ankle dorsiflexion range of motion in higher angles of knee flexion since the gastrocnemii shorten as the angle of knee flexion increases. Alternatively, ankle dorsiflexion range of motion from a flexed knee position can be limited by the extensibility of the ankle’s joint capsule or the calf’s soleus, which exclusively crosses the ankle joint. At the bottom of a straight-knee calf raise, these two structures are unlikely to be stretched effectively since the gastrocnemii reach their maximal lengths beforehand. However, a seated calf raise allows the ankle’s joint capsule and soleus to be elongated to greater degrees since a greater angle of dorsiflexion can be reached when the flexed knee position slackens the gastrocnemii. Ankle dorsiflexion range of motion from a flexed-knee position is what matters for movements like squats, since the knees are flexed at the bottom of a squat. Therefore, a full range of motion calf raises, specifically from a seated position, can train ankle mobility relevant to squatting.
