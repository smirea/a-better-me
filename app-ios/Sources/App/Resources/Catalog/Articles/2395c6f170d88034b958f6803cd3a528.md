Pause variations can be performed with the pause at any point in the lift’s range of motion, so where the pause is utilized can be individualized to address someone’s specific weakness or technical deficiencies. You can certainly try pausing at different points in the rep; pausing near the start of the lifting phase, when the working muscles are at their longest length, can generally be an effective approach. During the pause portion, muscles act isometrically (statically), without changing their length, in contrast to concentric shortening during the lifting phase or eccentric lengthening during the lowering phase. Compared with performing isometric exercise at shorter muscle lengths, training isometrically at longer lengths may contribute to greater muscle hypertrophy and improved strength across a wider range of the lift. Isometrically training at long muscle lengths can transfer to better performance at short lengths, while isometrically training at short lengths is comparatively more limited in its ability to improve strength at long muscle lengths.

Along with having greater direct transfer to strength throughout the range of motion, pausing near the start of the lifting phase can often improve your positioning during subsequent parts of the lifting phase. If you begin lifting in a mechanically advantageous position, it may be easier to complete the rep than if you start with suboptimal positioning.

Increasing strength at the start of the lifting phase can make it easier to complete the initial portion of the rep, which may increase speed and reduce difficulty in subsequent parts of the lift. If you have greater momentum entering into a later portion of the rep, you can be less likely to fail than compared to if the bar was moving quite slowly into that point from having to grind to begin the lifting phase. For instance, lifters who consistently fail their bench press at halfway up may be able to increase the weight they can handle before reaching their limit at that point. When the bar already has meaningful upward momentum that was generated by an explosive start off the chest, you will not have to generate as much force midway through the lift compared to if the bar moved with a lesser velocity from the start. To effectively transfer improved starting strength to increased bar speed with a touch-and-go bench press, include both the touch-and-go and pause variations in your training. Exclusively training the pause variation may fail to optimize your technique when you're not pausing, so keep that in mind if you prioritize bench press volume without pauses.
