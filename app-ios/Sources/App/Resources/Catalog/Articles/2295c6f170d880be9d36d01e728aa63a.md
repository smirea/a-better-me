_Key takeaway: Squats are unlikely to train one of the quad muscles as effectively as the others, but that muscle can be robustly trained with leg extensions._

For the same reason that squats do not train the hamstrings effectively, squats also neglect to train one of the quad muscles as effectively as the others. Three of the four quad muscles originate from the femur and exclusively function as knee extensors. These monoarticular (crossing only one joint) quad muscles have been consistently measured to experience significant growth following squat intervention studies that tracked changes in their size. However, the rectus femoris is the one quad muscle that originates from the pelvis and crosses the hip to function as a hip flexor, in addition to acting as a knee extensor. Notably, the rectus femoris does not just weakly assist in flexing the hip, but rather functions as a primary hip flexor that has greater leverage for producing hip flexion torque than any other muscle in the human body at certain joint angles. Because contraction of the rectus femoris can produce a substantial amount of hip flexion torque, its recruitment during a movement that requires both hip and knee extension is less biomechanically efficient than the recruitment of the other muscle bellies of the quadriceps. As the rectus femoris contracts more forcefully during movements like squat, leg press, and lunge variations, the hip extensors must work harder to resist the hip flexion torque it produces, so it is unlikely to be engaged to the same degree as the other three quad muscles that only generate knee extension torque. This would explain why the available body of evidence indicates that the rectus femoris is unlikely to grow as effectively as the other quad muscles after squatting interventions.

While recognizing that squats have the potential to induce some rectus femoris growth (particularly in individuals with limited training experience), you can be much more confident in your ability to grow the rectus femoris with a quad exercise that does not simultaneously train hip extension, such as a machine leg extension. In contrast to squats, every study involving a machine leg extension training intervention has found meaningful rectus femoris hypertrophy, with a magnitude similar to or even greater than that of the three monoarticular quadriceps muscles. Exercises like leg extensions (which include reverse Nordic curls and sissy squats) may be able to help you maximize your quad growth, but squats can still allow you to train the vast majority of your quad musculature in a very effective manner. The rectus femoris has been measured to contribute only 15% to total quad muscle volume, so you will not be missing out on a ton of quad growth if you rely on compound exercises like squats to develop your quads while simultaneously training other muscles very effectively.
