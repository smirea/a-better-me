While the rear delt may be the most effectively trained muscle during a rear delt fly (AKA reverse fly), rear delt flies can still train multiple other muscles and, correspondingly, do not actually isolate the rear delt. When seeking to determine how effectively a particular muscle may be trained by an exercise, it is ideal to measure the size of that muscle before and after a training intervention involving that muscle without any other exercises that may plausibly train it. At the time of writing this passage, no applicable training studies have yet tested the degree to which different muscles may grow following an intervention exclusively using a rear delt fly variation.

In the absence of that data, we can look at research modeling the moment arms (AKA lever arms) muscles have to contribute to shoulder horizontal extension, which is the shoulder action that is resisted during a rear delt fly, as you sweep your arm out away from the midline of your body. A moment arm quantifies how much leverage a muscle has for producing a particular type of joint torque, so a muscle with a sufficiently large moment arm for generating the type of joint torque strongly resisted by an exercise is well-suited to be trained by that exercise. Essentially every muscle that is widely recognized to be effectively trained by a particular exercise has a moment arm that allows it to produce torque for a joint action meaningfully resisted by that exercise. When studies measure significant growth to occur in a muscle after a resistance training intervention, that muscle almost always has been found to have a notable moment arm that lets it generate a type of joint torque resisted by an exercise from the training intervention. Consequently, muscle moment arms can help us determine which muscles may most likely be trained effectively by an exercise, even if changes in the size of potentially involved muscles have not been assessed following an intervention using that exercise.

Multiple studies have unsurprisingly found the rear delt to have a prominent shoulder horizontal extension moment arm. While more widely known for its shoulder abduction function, which is trained during lateral raises, the lateral delt has also been reported to have a meaningful shoulder horizontal extension moment arm. Similarly, the rotator cuff’s infraspinatus and teres minor, which are situated on the back of the shoulder blade, additionally have notable leverage to function as shoulder horizontal extensors. Another rotator cuff muscle, namely the supraspinatus (which lies on the top of the shoulder blade deep to the upper trap), also has a favorable shoulder horizontal extension moment arm, specifically with its posterior (closer to the backside) portion, but its anterior (closer to the front) portion lacks this moment arm.

Further evidence is provided by a variety of studies measuring EMG activity of the aforementioned muscles with shoulder horizontal extension moment arms. During resisted shoulder horizontal extension exercises like rear delt flies and prone T-raises, these muscles have been reported to demonstrate levels of EMG activity indicative of meaningfully contributing to these exercises. In isolation, a single EMG study does not conclusively demonstrate that a muscle will be effectively trained by a particular type of exercise. However, when the findings of a variety of EMG studies align with what would be predicted from the muscle moment arm data, their findings can serve as additional evidence supporting the contribution that the lateral delt and three rotator cuff muscles have during rear delt flies.

With all of that said, if you want to maximize the development of your side delts and rotator cuff muscles, it would be unwise to rely exclusively on rear delt flies. Rear delt flies are not necessarily a replacement for lateral raises (to target the lateral delt and supraspinatus) and shoulder external rotation exercises (to target the infraspinatus and teres minor) when striving to optimally train these muscles. However, the available evidence indicates that rear delt flies likely can stimulate a non-negligible increase in size and strength for these muscles, so they can provide more bang for your buck than just training the rear delts very effectively.
