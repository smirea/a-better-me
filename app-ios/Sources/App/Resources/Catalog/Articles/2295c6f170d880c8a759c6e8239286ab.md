When performing a horizontal pressing exercise like bench press variations, your shoulders can move through one of two planes of motion, or a combination of the two, which most often occurs. One of these planes of motion is the transverse plane, which divides your body into upper and lower halves and allows shoulder horizontal flexion and extension, with the elbows level with the shoulders and fully flared out away from the sides. With an overhand grip dumbbell bench press, using fully flared elbows that result in your upper arms forming a “T-shape” with your torso at the bottom, your shoulders can flex horizontally during the lifting phase and extend horizontally during the lowering phase. Shoulder horizontal flexion can also be trained with chest fly variations, such as the pec deck machine fly and the flat dumbbell fly. The other plane of motion is the sagittal plane, which divides your body into right and left halves and allows for shoulder flexion and extension with the elbows tucked close to your sides. With a neutral grip dumbbell bench press, use fully tucked elbows so your upper arms are parallel to your torso at the bottom; this allows your shoulders to flex during the lifting phase and extend during the lowering phase. Sagittal shoulder flexion can similarly be trained with dumbbell front raises, while sagittal shoulder extension can be trained with pullover variations.

Certain horizontal pressing variations can allow you to train your shoulders exclusively through one plane of motion, but most presses involve a hybrid shoulder motion that occurs in between these two planes. If your upper arm forms a 45-degree angle with your torso when your elbow is level with your shoulder toward the end of a press’s lowering phase, the shoulder can be moving symmetrically in between the two planes, resulting in shoulder horizontal flexion and sagittal flexion being trained in equal proportions. As this angle progressively decreases from 45 degrees, the press becomes more sagittal flexion-dominant, while it becomes more horizontal flexion-dominant with angles greater than 45 degrees. Dumbbells allow you full freedom to move in whichever plane you wish, but the grip you use for a barbell bench press will constrain you to a particular type of shoulder motion while keeping the wrists stacked over the elbows for stability. The elbows naturally flare further out as the grip widens, so wide grip bench presses are typically shoulder horizontal flexion-dominant, while close grip presses can be comparatively more shoulder sagittal flexion-dominant, with the medium grip press lying in between them.

The muscles that act on the shoulder have different leverages to function as sagittal flexors or horizontal flexors, so the manner in which lifters press may influence how much of a contribution to the press different muscles have. Multiple muscles can contribute to the shoulder motion that occurs when performing a horizontal press, but the pectoralis major and anterior deltoid serve the most prominent role. The anterior deltoid has meaningful leverage to function as a primary sagittal flexor or horizontal flexor, so it will serve a notable role in any pressing exercise. However, the available evidence indicates that its leverage to generate shoulder horizontal flexion torque steadily declines as the angle of shoulder horizontal flexion progressively increases, as it would throughout the lifting phase of a wide grip bench press. In contrast, the anterior deltoid retains excellent leverage to function as a sagittal flexor throughout the shoulder angles that could be present in horizontal pressing exercises. The anterior deltoid has greater leverage to serve as a sagittal flexor than any other muscle in the body, but the pec major has greater leverage to serve as a horizontal flexor throughout most of a horizontal press’s range of motion. Consequently, the shoulder torque produced by the anterior deltoid may be more impactful during a sagittal flexion-dominant press compared to a horizontal flexion-dominant press.

The reverse can be true for the pectoralis major, which functions as the most potent shoulder horizontal flexor in the body. In terms of its contribution to shoulder sagittal flexion, the different regions of the pectoralis major can have a pronounced impact. The clavicular region that constitutes the upper pec major has very favorable leverage to function as a shoulder sagittal flexor, so it can meaningfully contribute to sagittal flexion-dominant or horizontal flexion-dominant presses. However, the sternocostal head that constitutes its middle and lower regions and forms about four-fifths of the total pectoralis major muscle volume, lacks any leverage to function as a shoulder sagittal flexor. Correspondingly, this large portion of the pec major may serve a more prominent role during a horizontal flexion-dominant press compared to a sagittal flexion-dominant press.

In light of the aforementioned differences in leverage these muscles have to contribute to the two shoulder actions, there is reason to think that a horizontal flexion-dominant press will likely develop the middle and lower chest more effectively than a sagittal flexion-dominant press. On the other hand, a sagittal flexion-dominant press may be somewhat more effective for developing the anterior deltoid. Nonetheless, these potential differences are speculative in nature rather than being supported by any conclusive evidence. No study has yet measured changes in muscle size after participants completed bench pressing interventions with either a wide grip or a close grip that would facilitate firmer conclusions. The EMG muscle activity results from different studies are also inconclusive, given that certain studies have reported significant differences in EMG values when comparing different grip widths, while others have found similar EMG activity to be present. Consequently, it is unclear how much the shoulder plane of motion affects muscular development during horizontal press variations. With that said, if you wish to develop your chest from pressing, keeping your elbows completely tucked by your sides to move your shoulders exclusively in the sagittal plane is highly unlikely to train your middle and lower pec major as effectively compared to if you utilize at least some elbow flare. When comparing other ways in which you can press besides that extreme end of the spectrum, they are far more similar to each other than they are different, so you should be best served selecting an option that you feel comfortable progressively overloading over time.
