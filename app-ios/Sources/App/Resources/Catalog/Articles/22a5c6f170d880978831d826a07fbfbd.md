Unlike seated wrist curls with a barbell or dumbbells, the standing barbell wrist curl only permits you to train wrist flexion through a partial range of motion. During the standing variation, the wrist can transition from a neutral position to a fully flexed position, in contrast to the seated variations, which allow you to reach a full extended wrist position at the start of each rep. Given the vertical vector of gravitational force, resistive torque is also absent on the wrist when the wrist is in a neutral position during a standing barbell wrist curl, since the weight is situated directly under the wrist in this starting position. With the standing wrist curl, resistive torque peaks at full wrist flexion, when the weight is horizontally farthest from the wrist, resulting in the greatest lever arm of resistance acting on the wrist. Because the wrist flexor muscles are shortened in a flexed wrist position, standing barbell wrist curls predominantly load them at short muscle lengths in contrast to seated wrist curls with free weights, which can train them at longer lengths.

Currently, no study has yet investigated how hypertrophy of the forearm muscles may be influenced by the peak muscle length reached while resistance training. However, research on a variety of other muscle groups has indicated that training muscles at longer peak lengths up to a certain point may promote greater magnitudes of hypertrophy than training at shorter peak lengths. The effect may vary among different muscle groups, and training at very long peak muscle lengths does not necessarily induce greater hypertrophy than training at moderately long peak muscle lengths, as data from Larsen et al. (2025) suggests. However, with one study by Goto et al. (2019) being an exception, the available body of evidence indicates that training at longer peak muscle lengths is likely to promote greater or at least equal magnitudes of hypertrophy relative to training at shorter peak lengths. This does not mean that standing barbell wrist curls are ineffective for training the forearm muscles, but variations (like seated barbell wrist curls) that allow you to load them at longer muscle lengths have the potential to be somewhat more effective to induce increases in the size of these muscles.
