Just as the terms “straight-leg deadlift” and “stiff-leg deadlift” are commonly used as synonyms of each other, so too are the terms “Romanian deadlift” and “stiff-leg deadlift” often used interchangeably to describe the same exercise by scholarly articles and lifters in general. However, some people consider these names to refer to different hip variations, and this database includes three different exercises to allow you to select whichever specific variation you wish to utilize. Under this definition, the straight-leg deadlift employs a straight knee position, in contrast to the mild knee bend used in the Romanian or stiff-leg deadlift from the floor. This conception of the straight-leg deadlift, as demonstrated by Hegyi et al. (2018) and Piper and Waller (2001), may be used less frequently than the other two variations.

Both the straight-leg deadlift and the Romanian deadlift begin in an upright position and use a range of motion tailored to individual flexibility, as the plates often do not contact the floor. If someone has sufficient hamstring flexibility for the plates to contact the floor during either variation (which is much rarer with the straight-leg deadlift), a light tap to the ground is used. In contrast, the stiff-leg deadlift from the floor begins with the plates fully on the floor, as in a conventional deadlift, and results in the full weight of the bar being transferred to the ground at the end of each rep. All three variations are hip-hinge exercises that train the same muscle groups, but the relative emphasis on different muscles may vary slightly across variations. Compared with the other two variations, the straight-leg deadlift results in a lower range of motion for most people, as the hamstrings are lengthened to a greater extent with fully straight knees than with mildly bent knees. Consequently, someone can achieve maximal hamstring elongation earlier during the descent of a straight-leg deadlift, thereby limiting the degree to which the hips can flex before the lifting phase begins. Compared to using a mild knee bend, a straight-leg deadlift also constrains how far you can sit your buttocks backward and results in the bar drifting farther out in front of your legs during the descent.
