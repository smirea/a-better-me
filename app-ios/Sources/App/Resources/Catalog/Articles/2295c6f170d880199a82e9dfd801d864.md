*Key takeaway: Squats are ineffective for training the hamstrings because knee flexion opposes the quadriceps.*

To lift yourself out of the bottom of a squat, your knees and hips must simultaneously extend against meaningful resistance. Like the gluteus maximus, the hamstrings have excellent leverage for hip extension, so contraction of these muscles generates hip extension torque that is conducive to squatting. However, no study measuring changes in hamstring muscle size after a squatting intervention has found significant increases in hamstring size. This lack of meaningful hamstring hypertrophy may be due to the fact that the hamstrings cross the knee joint and function as primary knee flexors, and thus can be trained effectively with hamstring curls, in addition to acting as hip extensors.

When a muscle contracts, it fundamentally generates a force that pulls the bones to which it attaches closer together. This pulling force cannot be exerted on one end of the muscle without simultaneously acting on the other end. As a result, contraction of a muscle that crosses two joints, known as a biarticular muscle, inevitably generates torque at both joints it crosses. Consequently, it is physically impossible for hamstring contraction to produce hip extension torque without concurrently generating knee flexion torque. During a squat, the knee flexion torque produced by hamstring contraction directly opposes the quadriceps as they contract to generate the knee extension torque required to stand upright during the lifting phase and to control the descent during the lowering phase.

To maximize efficiency, the nervous system appears to limit hamstring recruitment during squatting, favoring activation of the gluteus maximus and adductor magnus to generate the desired hip extension torque without producing knee flexion torque. This likely explains why squatting interventions do not yield significant hamstring growth but do induce significant growth of the gluteus maximus and hip adductors. A similar phenomenon almost certainly exists with other compound exercises that meaningfully resist both knee extension and hip extension, such as leg press, lunge, and split squat variations. However, changes in hamstring muscle size following these exercises have not yet been studied, in contrast to squats.

The hamstrings remain somewhat active in a stabilizing role during squatting, but not to a degree sufficient to train them robustly. Squats can effectively train a substantial portion of the body’s musculature, but for specifically developing the hamstrings, they cannot match hamstring curl or hip hinge variations such as Romanian deadlifts.
Nonetheless, many lifters report feeling soreness in their inner hamstrings after squatting. This common occurrence can be attributed to soreness of the adductor magnus, which lies directly next to the semimembranosus on the inner portion of the back of the thigh. The adductor magnus functions as a powerful hip extensor, particularly at high degrees of hip flexion, such as at the bottom of a squat, and therefore serves as a prime mover during squatting. As a result, it can become sore after a squat session. The adductor magnus can also play a prominent role during leg press, lunge, and split squat variations, all of which can elicit soreness that is often mistaken for hamstring soreness.
