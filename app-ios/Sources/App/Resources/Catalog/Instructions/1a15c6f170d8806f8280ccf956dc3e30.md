## Setting up

1. Approach a flat exercise bench or a sturdy object of similar height that allows you to place both hands on top of it, about shoulder-width apart.
   1. In addition to a plyometric box (or a pair of plyometric boxes, depending on the width), you can use an aerobic step elevated on top of risers at a gym.
   2. Outside a gym, you can use objects like a chair (that will not slide), a couch, or a park bench.
2. Sit down on the edge of the bench with an upright torso.
   1. You should be seated with the long part of the bench aligned with your sides.
3. Place your palms on the edge of the bench approximately shoulder width apart, resulting in your arms being directed straight down with straight elbows.
   1. The standard technique involves pointing your knuckles forward with your fingers wrapped around the edge of the bench.
   2. However, pointing your knuckles out to the sides with straight fingers may be preferable. Relative to the standard setup that internally rotates your shoulders, this approach can help you externally rotate your shoulders, as you point your biceps straight forward rather than angling them inward. This difference in shoulder position can result in some people finding bench dips to feel more comfortable for their shoulders when pointing their fingers out to the sides, compared to straight forward.
4. While supporting yourself with your hands, scoot forward until your buttocks are just off the bench. Position your legs out in front of you with straight knees.
   1. You should be bearing weight mostly through the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands).
   2. The back of your heels should be on the ground.
5. Pull your shoulder blades back and down to assume a “proud chest” posture.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing your core throughout your set.
2. As you keep them tucked close to your sides, bend your elbows to lower your body in a controlled manner.
   1. Throughout your set, keep your shoulder blades pulled back and down as you avoid letting your shoulders roll forward or shrug up. It can help to think about keeping your chest up.
   2. Keep your hips just in front of the bench rather than letting them drift forward.
   3. Avoid flaring your elbows out away from your sides. When viewed from behind, your elbows should remain aligned with your wrists rather than tracking noticeably outside your wrists.
3. Continue descending through the full range of motion that your shoulder mobility comfortably permits.
   1. It is common for people with healthy shoulders to reach the point at which their elbows are bent by at least 90 degrees, but specifically how deeply you should descend will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
   2. At your end range, it is normal to feel a stretching sensation in the front of your shoulders, but you should not feel any shoulder discomfort or pain.
   3. If you feel joint discomfort or pain at the bottom, you most likely lowered yourself excessively far relative to your current mobility. Training bench dips over time may improve your mobility, but you should not force yourself into an aggravating range.
   4. While you do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, using a slow and controlled tempo as you approach the bottom position is recommended.
4. Reverse the movement by pushing through the bases of your palms to lift yourself up until you have fully straightened your elbows.
   1. You can think about driving your elbows straight down.
5. Alternate between steps 2-4 until you have completed your set.
   1. Once you have fully straightened your elbows after your last rep, scoot your hips back to sit on the bench.

## Description

While training your front delts and pec major to a lesser degree than other dip variations, this exercise will primarily develop your triceps with an emphasis on its lateral and medial heads.
