## Setting up

1. Determine the pair of bars/handles that you will use for your dip.
   1. IIdeally select a pair of bars that enables you to use a near shoulder-width grip for this variation.
   2. A variety of pieces of equipment can be used to perform this exercise.
   3. You can use bars that are completely parallel to each other or angled “V-grip” bars that converge to be closer together near the base and wider apart at the free ends. In contrast to stationary parallel bars, angled bars allow you to adjust your grip width based on specifically where you grasp them. While gymnastics rings can also be used to train dips, this variation specifically uses bars.
   4. Gyms commonly have a power tower (which has dip bars protruding from its vertical frame and often has a pull-up bar and/or forearm pads to use for leg raises) or a dip stand/station (which is specifically designed for dips).
   5. You can alternatively use a dip attachment that is connected to a power rack.
   6. Some assisted dip/pull-up machines also permit you to flip the knee pad or foot bar up, therefore enabling you to train dips without machine assistance. Certain machine models allow you to adjust the distance between the bars and position them near shoulder width apart.
   7. Other options include portable parallettes (which can be positioned closer or wider apart to adjust your grip width) or parallel bars located at a playground.
2. While standing in front of the bars, add your desired load (in the form of weight plates or a kettlebell) to a dip/pull-up belt you have around your waist.
   1. The back of your belt should be securely resting on top of your buttocks. You can loop the belt’s chain or strap (depending on the belt model) through the center hole of a weight plate or under the handle of a kettlebell. If you have a loading pin for your belt, you would alternatively loop the chain or strap through the loading pin after adding weight plates to it.
   2. It will be easiest if you carry the weight over to the dip bars and add the weight to your belt while you are under the bar. Adding the weight to your belt farther away from the bar before walking over to the bar can be awkward.
   3. As an alternative to using a pull-up/dip belt, you can wear a weighted vest.
   4. While less stable and more awkward than using a belt or weighted vest, you also have the option of holding a dumbbell between your thighs or feet. This approach is suboptimal and will limit how much weight you can use.
3. Firmly grasp the bars with your palms facing each other and your elbows straight. Hold the handles directly under the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) with mostly straight wrists.
   1. Squeezing the handles tightly can help you avoid letting your wrists excessively cock back.
   2. If you are using angled bars, you can grasp the bars at the point where they are near shoulder width apart for you.
   3. Depending on the equipment you use, you initially may have to step up onto the equipment or a step you position nearby before you can grasp the bars with straight elbows.
4. While keeping your elbows straightened and torso upright, lift your feet off the ground, and position your legs against the weight (if you are using a dip belt).
   1. Depending on the equipment you use, you may have to bend your knees in order for your feet to clear the ground, or you may be able to keep your knees straight.
   2. Based on your preference, you can spread your legs just enough to position the weight between them, or you can position your legs together with the weight just in front of them. Which option is most comfortable will be influenced by how you set up your belt and the shape of your weight(s). Contacting the weight with your legs can help you prevent the weight from excessively swinging during your set.
   3. If you opt to bend your knees, you can cross your ankles for additional stability
   4. Compared to positioning your legs further backward or forward, keeping your thighs close to vertical can generally promote an upright torso.
5. Pull your shoulder blades back and down to assume a “proud chest” posture.
   1. You chiefly want to prevent your shoulders from rolling forward or shrugging up during this exercise.
   2. It is not required to keep your shoulder blades packed down further than they normally are at rest, but the intent to pack them down will prevent your torso from sagging down, which would place you into a shrugged shoulder position.
   3. Similarly, you do not necessarily need to have your shoulder blades fully back in the starting position, but pulling them back can make it easier to avoid tilting your shoulders forward, which is undesirable.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing your core throughout your set.
2. As you keep them tucked close to your sides, bend your elbows to lower your body in a controlled manner.
   1. While you do not have to maintain a completely upright torso, keep your torso mostly upright for this triceps dip rather than more prominently leaning your torso forward like you would during a chest dip. A mild degree of torso lean can naturally occur without striving to lean forward and often contributes to the exercise feeling more comfortable than if you focus on being fully upright.
   2. Throughout your set, avoid letting your shoulders roll forward or shrug up.
   3. Avoid flaring your elbows out away from your sides. When viewed from behind, your elbows should remain stacked over your wrists and the bars rather than having your elbows move noticeably outside your wrists.
   4. Rather than swinging them, continue keeping your legs stationary relative to your hips until you finish your set. If you are using a dip belt, keeping your legs in contact with the weight can help you stabilize it. If the weight is between your legs, squeezing your thighs together can stop the weight from swinging.
3. Continue descending through the full range of motion that your shoulder mobility comfortably permits.
   1. It is common for people with healthy shoulders to reach the point at which their elbows are bent by at least 90 degrees, but specifically how deeply you should descend will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
   2. At your end range, it is normal to feel a stretching sensation in the front of your shoulders, but you should not feel any shoulder discomfort or pain.
   3. If you feel joint discomfort or pain at the bottom, you most likely lowered yourself excessively far relative to your current mobility. Training dips over time may improve your mobility, but you should not force yourself into an aggravating range.
   4. While you do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, using a slow and controlled tempo as you approach the bottom position is recommended.
4. Reverse the movement by pushing through the bars to lift yourself up until you have fully straightened your elbows.
   1. You can think about driving your elbows straight down.
   2. You should finish with your elbows close to your sides and your arms directed down toward the ground.
5. Alternate between steps 2-4 until you have completed your set.
   1. Once you have fully straightened your elbows after your last rep, step down from the bars.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop the lateral and medial heads of your triceps while also training its long head to a lesser degree, along with your front delts and pec major.
