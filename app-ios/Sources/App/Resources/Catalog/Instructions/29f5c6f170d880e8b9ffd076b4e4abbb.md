## Setting up

1. Adjust the rack’s J-hooks to near mid-chest height, and place a barbell on top of them.
   1. The optimal starting height should place the bar slightly above the tops of the J-hooks, with your feet flat on the floor, after you stand upright with the bar on your back.
   2. If you later need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground while walking out in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks, you initially positioned the J-hooks too high. Walking with your heels raised increases the risk of losing your balance and should be avoided when you have a barbell on your back.
   3. If you need to bend your knees and hips beyond a mild degree (e.g., more like a half squat than a quarter squat) initially to place the bar on your back, you positioned the J-hooks excessively low. This can make it more challenging to rerack the bar at the end of your set.
2. Position a sturdy box of your desired height centered within the rack, a couple of step lengths behind the J-hooks.
   1. To train your lower body muscles most effectively, you want to pick a box height that enables you to squat near the depth of what is your full range of motion back squat (i.e., the lowest point at which you can squat with quality technique while keeping your back flat and feet fully on the floor). This depth will vary from person to person, but people can often descend at least until their thighs are fairly close to being parallel to the ground.
   2. A squat box or plyometric box is an ideal choice for your box. You can position either type of box with one of its corners directed to the middle point between the J-hooks to give you the greatest freedom in how you situate your feet when box squatting.
   3. Plyometric boxes are often available in gyms at heights of 12in (30cm), 18in (46cm), and 24in (61cm). Box squatting with an 18in (46cm) box, which is near the typical height of a flat exercise bench, restricts the range of motion for most people who are not clearly taller than six feet (183cm). If you currently do not have the mobility to squat down to a 12in (30cm) box with proper form, you can increase the height of the box by placing a thick bumper plate on top of the box to elevate it.
   4. While others have fixed heights like plyometric boxes, the height of certain squat box models can be adjusted to accommodate how deeply you wish to box squat.
   5. If you do not have access to a squat box or plyometric box, you can use an aerobic step provided that the step is rated to withstand at least half the combined load of your bodyweight and the bar weight. You can adjust its height based on how many pairs of risers you stack underneath it.
   6. Alternatively, you may be able to use a flat exercise bench as your box. A bench is excessively tall for many lifters to squat through a full range of motion, but it can be suitable for rather tall individuals, people with limited mobility, and anyone who intentionally wishes to train through a partial range of motion. To fit either a bench or an aerobic step within a rack, you may have to position it with the shorter side facing the side of the rack where you will place the barbell.
   7. While not ideal due to its lower stability, you can also stack bumper plates on top of each other to form a “box” of your desired height. If you opt to do so, make sure that the plates are completely centered on top of each other.
3. Face the center of the bar with approximately a shoulder-width stance and grasp each side of the bar with a somewhat outside shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
   1. The proper grip width varies by person, and what feels best to you can depend on your shoulder mobility.
   2. If you are not accustomed to exercises that have a bar on your back, you may have to try out different hand placements to find one that personally suits you well.
4. Shallowly bend your hips and knees before walking under the bar and positioning it on your upper back in a spot that feels comfortable and stable for you.
   1. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should be securely resting on the muscles of your upper back when squeezing your shoulder blades together.
   2. If you feel the bar is putting pressure on your neck, it is most likely placed too high on your back.
   3. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
   4. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have it placed too low on your back.
   5. When positioning the bar on your upper back, you may need to adjust your grip if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
   6. If you are not accustomed to having a barbell on your back, you may not feel particularly comfortable doing so, especially when it is loaded with additional weight, but whichever position you use should not be painful.
5. Use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of box squat reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins prior to reracking the bar onto the J-hooks.
6. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends when squatting.
7. Repeat steps 3 and 4 of the setup before proceeding to the “exercise technique” section.

## Exercise technique

1. Pull down on the bar to keep it positioned on your upper back, and stand upright by straightening your knees and hips.
   1. Maintain this bar position throughout your set.
2. Take a couple of steps backward until the box is just behind you. Position your feet clearly outside shoulder width, and point your toes moderately out to the sides.
   1. Ultimately, you want to use a wide stance for this variation, but what is ideal for a wide stance varies among different individuals, so trying out different stances over time can be helpful.
   2. You can determine specifically how wide apart you position your feet and the degree to which you rotate your toes outward based on what feels comfortable and stable to you.
   3. Unless you typically squat with a rather wide stance, you can also use your standard stance as a reference point and moderately widen your stance from there.
   4. As a general rule of thumb, a stance width that is at least 150% of your shoulder width with your toes pointing about 30-45 degrees out to the side is an appropriate starting point.
   5. The soles of your feet should be fully planted on the ground, while your lower back is flat and your head is looking straight ahead.
3. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed, or simply sucking your belly button in.
   2. Continue bracing your core from this point on to maintain a rigid back position.
4. Initiate the descent by starting to sit your buttocks back. Progressively bend at your knees and hips to squat down in a controlled manner while keeping your chest up and back flat.
   1. Let your torso naturally lean forward at your hips to maintain your balance, but avoid letting your lower back round or chest cave down.
   2. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the ground as your weight is evenly distributed throughout your feet (rather than being all on your heels or toes) during your set.
   3. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet.
   4. As you bend your knees, it is normal for them to track forward over your feet (especially the deeper your squat) to help you maintain your balance.
5. Continue to squat down until you can sit onto the box in a controlled manner, as you avoid plopping onto the box or rocking back onto the box.
   1. As you sit on the box, your torso should maintain the same degree of forward lean that you had right before reaching the box.
   2. If your torso transitions to a more upright position after sitting on the box compared to at the end of your descent, you have rocked back onto the box.
6. While keeping your core braced and back flat, and feet fully on the ground, allow about half of your weight to deload into the box as you pause on the box for approximately one second.
   1. As a cue, you can think about a scale being on top of the box. If you weigh 200lb (91kg) and have 100lb (45kg) on the bar, the scale should read within the vicinity of 150lb (68kg).
7. Reverse the movement by driving through the ground while extending (straightening) your knees and hips to stand upright.
   1. Avoid rocking your torso forward to generate momentum before you start squatting upward.
   2. Continue keeping your chest up, back flat, feet fully on the ground, and knees aligned with your feet as you squat up.
8. Alternate between steps 4-7 until you have completed your set.
   1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
   2. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before initiating the descent for the next rep.
9. Walk forward until the barbell contacts the back of each J-hook before mildly bending your knees and hips to rerack the bar onto the J-hooks.
   1. Depending on how wide a stance you adopted, you may feel more balanced if you initially bring your feet closer together before walking forward to re-rack the weight.

## Description

While statically training your spinal extensors, this exercise primarily trains your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.
