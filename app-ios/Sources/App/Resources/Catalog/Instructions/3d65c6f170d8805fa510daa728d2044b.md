## Setting up
You can perform this exercise with an asymmetrically weighted implement by holding it by its lighter end, or you can use a symmetrically weighted implement by holding it closer to one end to provide a similar effect. Asymmetrical implements include wrist bars (a type of equipment specifically designed for wrist exercises that looks like an adjustable dumbbell where plates can be added only onto one side), adjustable dumbbells with plates loaded only on one side, and kettlebells. Symmetrical implements that may be used include dumbbells and weight plates. Based on your preference, you can also perform this exercise with your forearms supported on your thighs while seated, or you can kneel on the ground and rest your forearms on a flat bench in front of you.

### **Setting up** with an asymmetrically weighted implement

1. Firmly grasp a pair of asymmetrically weighted implements by their lighter ends with their heavier ends in front of you. If you are using kettlebells, firmly grasp them by their center handles.
    1. If you are using wrist bars or adjustable dumbbells weighted on only one side, grasping them further away from their weighted ends will increase the resistance they provide during this exercise.
2. Sit down on a seat or bench, and position your elbows and forearms on top of your thighs with bent elbows, your wrists a little in front of your knees, and your palms facing in toward each other. Alternatively, you can rest your elbows and forearms on a flat bench while kneeling on the ground with your palms facing each other.
    1. The appropriate position should let your elbows and forearms feel well-supported regardless of whether they are resting on your thighs or a bench.
    2. Your index fingers’ knuckles should be vertically stacked above your little fingers’ knuckles.
    3. If you are using kettlebells, their bases should now be directed forward.
    4. If you are using wrist bars or adjustable dumbbells weighted on only one side, the weighted ends should now be above your hands.

### **Setting up** with an asymmetrically weighted implement

1. Firmly grasp a pair of dumbbells with your little fingers contacting one end of the weight, leaving some space where your index fingers are by the other end. Alternatively, grasp a pair of weight plates with your thumbs against one side of the plates and your other four fingers against the other side.
    1. Maintaining a pinch grip with weight plates can be more challenging for your hand muscles compared to if you use dumbbells.
2. Sit down on a seat or bench, and position your elbows and forearms on top of your thighs with bent elbows, your wrists a little in front of your knees, and your palms facing in toward each other. Alternatively, you can rest your elbows and forearms on a flat bench while kneeling on the ground with your palms facing each other.
    1. The appropriate position should let your elbows and forearms feel well-supported regardless of whether they are resting on your thighs or a bench.
    2. Your index fingers’ knuckles should be vertically stacked above your little fingers’ knuckles.
## Exercise technique
1. Let the weights tilt your hands down through a range of motion comfortable to your wrists to assume the starting position of the exercise.
2. Tilt your hands up toward the thumb-side of your forearms through the full range of motion in which your weight provides resistance.
    1. Some implements will stop resisting radial deviation (the wrist motion you are performing) once you have achieved a neutral wrist position (i.e., when your hand is not tilted to either side), while others can provide resistance through the full range of motion in which you move your wrists.
    2. For instance, an adjustable dumbbell with plates loaded on only one side will provide a force that pulls you into greater radial deviation once you are radially deviated beyond neutral, so radially deviating beyond a neutral position is no longer training the target muscles. In contrast, kettlebells can provide resistance to your radial deviator muscles throughout the full range of motion of your wrists, because the center of mass of kettlebells remains in front of your wrists the whole time.
    3. Individuals with normal wrist mobility can typically tilt their hands only mildly beyond a neutral wrist position, so the range of motion for this exercise may look fairly similar when stopping in a neutral position or a radially deviated position.
    4. While you move your hands through wrist motion, keep the rest of your body stationary throughout the exercise, which includes keeping your forearms and elbows resting on your thighs/bench.
3. Reverse the movement in a controlled manner by letting your hands tilt toward the little finger-side of your forearms through your wrist’s comfortable range of motion.
    1. This will angle your knuckles at least mildly downward.
    2. Only move through the range of motion that is comfortable to your wrist and thumb.
    3. There is no need to perform this extremely slowly, but you should actively control the movement rather than letting the weight swing your hand down.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set.
## Description
This exercise can effectively train five forearm muscles, namely the two extensor carpi radialis muscles, flexor carpi radialis, extensor pollicis brevis, and abductor pollicis longus.
