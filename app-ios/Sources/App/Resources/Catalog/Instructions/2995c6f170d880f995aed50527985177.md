## Setting up

1. Adjust the rack’s J-hooks to just below the height of your collarbone, and place a safety squat bar on top of them.
   1. The optimal starting height should place the bar slightly above the tops of the J-hooks, with your feet flat on the floor, after you stand upright with the bar on your back.
   2. If you later need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground while walking out in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks, you initially positioned the J-hooks too high. Walking with your heels raised increases the risk of losing your balance and should be avoided when you have a barbell on your back.
2. Place the safety squat bar on top of the J-hooks with the handles angled down and toward the direction in which you will face during the good morning.
   1. When resting on J-hooks, most safety squat bar models are designed in a way that results in the handles being mildly angled forward or backward rather than being directed straight down.
   2. The direction in which the handles from those bars are directed will influence how far forward the weight is situated relative to the upper back pad, which influences the lever arm of the weight.
   3. If you set up with the handles angled back toward you, the weight will be pitched relatively farther forward and increase the difficulty of the exercise compared to positioning the bar in the standard way.
   4. If you wish to do so, you can intentionally position the bar in the opposite manner as its own variation, but be aware that lighter loads will be required with that approach.
   5. Certain safety squat bar models are designed with the front and back sides being identical, so their handles can be directed straight down, and how you place the bar on the J-hooks is irrelevant.
3. While facing the center of the bar, shallowly bend at your hips and knees in a quarter squat-like manner to then walk under the bar and grasp its handles while situating the bar on your upper back in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
   1. Your palms should be facing in toward each other while grasping the handles with bent elbows.
   2. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back.
   3. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back.
   4. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
   5. If you are not accustomed to having a bar on your back, you may not feel particularly comfortable doing so, especially when it is loaded with additional weight, but whichever position you use should not be painful.
   6. If you find that deeper than a mild bend at your hips and knees (e.g., more like a half squat than a quarter squat) is required to position the bar on your back, you can lift the bar out of the J-hooks, set it down, raise the height of the J-hooks, and place the bar back onto the J-hooks.
4. With the bar resting on your upper back muscles, stand up in a quarter squat-like manner by extending your knees and hips.
5. Use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of good morning reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins prior to re-racking the bar onto the J-hooks.
6. Load the bar with your intended weight and position the safety pins to be slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of the lowering phase of the good morning.

## Exercise technique

1. With the hand placement and bar positioning determined while setting up, un-rack the bar standing up in a quarter squat-like manner by extending your knees and hips while keeping the bar positioned on your upper back.
2. Take a couple of steps backward, and position your feet clearly outside shoulder-width stance with your toes moderately to mildly pointing outward.
   1. You can determine specifically how wide apart you position your feet and the degree to which you rotate your toes outward based on what feels comfortable and stable to you, since what is ideal for a wide stance varies among different individuals. Avoid taking so wide a stance that you feel unbalanced.
   2. The soles of your feet should be fully planted on the ground.
3. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward.
   1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
   2. After the deep “belly breath,” you can continue thinking about tensing the front of your abdominals, sides of your abdominals, and lower back muscles simultaneously in a manner that allows you to form a “360-degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
   3. As a helpful cue for your “360-degree brace,” you can tense the front and sides of your abdominals outward like you would if you are bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   4. Bracing will help you maintain a rigid trunk position while you move about your hips throughout your set.
4. Hinge at your hips through your available range of motion in a controlled manner to move your buttocks backward while you allow your knees to bend mildly.
   1. Keep your lower back near a neutral position, like you have when you are normally standing upright, rather than rounding your lower back while doing so.
   2. Keep your neck near a neutral position, like you have when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.
   3. Most of the motion should come from your hips, and your shins should remain in a vertical position throughout your set, but you should not keep your knees fully straightened.
   4. You do not need to focus on bending your knees, since the appropriately mild degree of knee bend can naturally occur when you sit your buttocks backward, without intentionally keeping your knees locked.
   5. As a cue, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks while your hands are carrying something, as you keep your chest up (rather than letting your chest collapse through back rounding).
   6. While you hinge at your hips, the bar will naturally descend, and your torso will transition to an angle that is closer to horizontal.
   7. How deeply you are able to hinge without meaningfully rounding your lower back or excessively bending your knees will be dependent on your hamstring flexibility.
   8. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch and can no longer sit your buttocks any farther back, you have finished descending through your available range of motion.
   9. If you positioned them appropriately, the safety pins should not restrict how far you can hip hinge, but if you are able to hinge until the bar contacts the safety pins, you can lightly tap the safety pins with the bar without resting the bar on the pins.
5. Reverse the movement by driving your hips forward until you have returned to the starting position with an upright torso.
   1. The lifting phase should simply be a reversal of what you just performed, so you should continue keeping your chest up without any back rounding.
   2. Once you have returned to the starting position, you should be able to draw a straight line from your shoulders through your hips to the middle of your feet when viewed from the side.
   3. Avoid driving your hips in front of your shoulders at the top, which would prompt your lower back to arch excessively. Squeezing your glutes at the end of the lifting phase can help you finish with your hips appropriately stacked under your shoulders to prevent excessive arching.
6. Alternate between steps 4 and 5 until you have completed your set.
   1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
   2. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.
7. Rerack the bar by walking forward until the barbell contacts the back of each J-hook, and you are ready to rest the bar on the J-hooks by once again bending at your hips and knees in a quarter squat-like manner.
   1. Depending on how wide a stance you adopted, you may feel more balanced if you initially bring your feet closer together before walking forward to re-rack the weight.

## Description

While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
