## Setting up
1. Prepare the barbell with your desired load by adding weight plates to it or leaving the barbell unloaded, which is recommended if performing this exercise for the first time.
    1. The setup for this exercise is simply performing the lifting phase of a conventional deadlift.
    2. As an alternative to a standard barbell, in which you can load plates onto its sleeves, you can similarly use a fixed-weight barbell for this exercise.
2. Optionally set up a thick bumper plate, deadlift block, or aerobic step centered under the bar if you wish to extend this exercise’s range of motion beyond what you could achieve when standing on the ground.
    1. When standing on the ground, individuals who are sufficiently flexible may find that the weight can contact the ground at the bottom of a rep before they reach their full range of motion. Standing on top of elevated objects (as you would during a deficit deadlift) can alternatively permit a greater range of motion. Compared to loading the bar with large diameter plates, exclusively loading it with small diameter plates can also serve a similar function as standing on an elevated object.
    2. If your range of motion is not restricted when standing on the ground during this exercise, there is no purpose in standing on an object to elevate your feet.
3. In a near hip-width stance, stand with the middle of your feet centered under the bar.
    1. At this point, you will naturally be standing on the elevated object if you wish to use it, or you can stand on the ground.
4. Sit your hips backward while bending your knees until you can grasp the bar with a near shoulder-width grip as your palms face backward (i.e., overhand grip) and your elbows are straight.
5. Drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar before thrusting your hips forward and squeezing your glutes to stand upright with the bar.
## Exercise technique
1. While allowing your knees to bend mildly, sit your buttocks backward to hinge at your hips through the full range of motion in which you can comfortably move without rounding your back.
    1. Throughout the set, keep the soles of your feet fully planted and your arms in a vertical position, perpendicular to the ground.
    2. This step is essentially the same as the lowering phase of a Romanian deadlift without keeping the weight as close to your legs (since the arms angle slightly backward during a Romanian deadlift, rather than hanging straight down during this exercise).
    3. Most of the motion should come from your hips, and your shins should remain in a vertical position throughout your set, but you should not keep your knees fully straightened. You do not need to focus on bending your knees, since the appropriately mild degree of knee bend can naturally occur when you sit your buttocks backward, without intentionally keeping your knees locked.
    4. While you hinge at your hips, your torso will transition to an angle that is closer to horizontal. How close to horizontal you can incline your torso will be affected by your hamstring flexibility.
    5. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch and can no longer sit back any farther, you have reached your personal end range.
2. In a controlled curling manner, allow your whole back to round slowly through your comfortable range of motion without moving your lower body.
    1. The degree to which you can round your back will be affected by your spinal mobility.
    2. It is normal to feel a stretching sensation in your back muscles in the bottom position, but you should not feel any discomfort at the end range. Only round your back as far as is comfortable.
    3. Rather than moving only one part of your back, strive to round your lower, middle, and upper back simultaneously. Due to the anatomy of the spine, most of the motion will occur at the lower half of your back, but moving the upper half of your back as well can help you train more of your back musculature.
    4. As a cue, you can think about bringing the bottom of your ribs toward the top of your hip bones.
    5. While keeping your lower body in the same position throughout each rep, you should continue feeling a stretching sensation in your hamstrings. During the descent, particularly avoid dropping your hips by bending your knees.
3. While maintaining the same hinged-hip position, uncoil your spine in a controlled manner as you transition from a rounded-back to a flat-back position.
    1. All the motion should come from your back muscles, extending (straightening) your spine without any movement at your hips or knees, which should prompt you to feel tension in the muscles running along your spine as you flare your ribs up and away from your hip bones.
    2. Based on your preference, you can finish this step once your back is straight or when your lower back is mildly arched, like it normally is when standing upright.
    3. Performing this lifting phase with a controlled tempo can help you isolate the intended spinal motion without inadvertently moving your hips or knees.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set and are ready to set the weight down as you lower your hips by bending your knees.
## Description
This exercise can dynamically train the erector spinae and deep intrinsic spinal extensor muscles that run along the length of your entire back.
