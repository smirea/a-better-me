## Setting up

1. While standing under a pull-up bar, reach up overhead and firmly grasp it with your palms facing forward (i.e., an overhand grip) and your hands situated near shoulder-width apart or somewhat wider.
   1. Depending on the specific pull-up bar you are using, your height, and your wingspan, the bar may be beyond your reach.
   2. If you cannot reach the bar while standing on the ground, you can initially step up on a sturdy box, aerobic step, or bumper plate to place the bar within your reach.
   3. While not required, elevating yourself to reach the bar may help you set up with a more stable hand position around the bar compared to if you jumped up from the ground to reach the bar.
   4. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   5. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide upward during the dead hang, which pulls on your hand skin in a manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
2. With your elbows straight and your hands firmly grasping the bar, lift your feet up off the ground by bending your knees to begin hanging on the bar.
   1. So long as your feet are above the ground, it is up to you how bent your knees initially are when hanging on the bar.
   2. While hanging, your thighs should be directed straight down toward the ground, and your torso should be in a vertical position.
   3. You will maintain a firm grip on the bar with straightened elbows throughout your set.

## Exercise technique

1. March up both knees toward your chest through your available range of motion while curling your hips forward and allowing your knees to progressively bend as they lift higher up.
   1. Curling your hips forward will round your lower back and result in your buttocks facing forward at the end of the lifting phase.
   2. Keep your chest facing straight ahead rather than leaning your torso backward.
   3. You do not consciously need to think about progressively bending your knees since they will naturally bend when marching your knees up unless you intentionally try to straighten them.
   4. How closely you are able to raise your knees up toward your chest will be influenced by the mobility in your hips and lower back, along with your strength.
   5. Whenever you feel like you cannot bring your knees any closer toward your chest, you have reached the end of the lifting phase.
2. Reverse the movement in a controlled manner by allowing your knees to descend while uncurling your lower back until you have returned to the starting position with your thighs directed straight down toward the ground.
   1. Actively lower your legs throughout the descent rather than letting gravity rapidly pull you back down, which could result in your legs swinging back behind the starting position as momentum takes over.
   2. Your knees can naturally transition to a less bent position as you lower your legs.
3. Alternate between steps 1 and 2 until you have completed your set and are ready to return your feet to the ground.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop your rectus abdominis while also training your obliques and hip flexors.

### **Straight knee leg raise versus bent knee leg raise**

With the exception of utilizing a straighter knee position, the technique for the straight knee leg raise is otherwise essentially the same as the bent knee leg raise, so both variations primarily train the same muscle groups. In terms of the training effect these exercises provide, the main difference is that the straight knee leg raise is a higher intensity variation due to how it shifts the center of mass of your legs farther forward relative to your trunk and hip. This results in a longer lever arm of resistance acting on your trunk and hip, which requires your abdominal muscles and hip flexor muscles respectively generating greater trunk flexion and hip flexion torque to overcome. Resistive torque can be increased by increasing the weight you move or by increasing the lever arm in which the weight acts on a particular joint, so the straight knee leg raise can function similar to a weighted knee raise without requiring additional weights. The greater magnitude of resistance from the straight knee leg raise almost certainly explains why McGill et al. (2014) found this variation to yield noticeably greater EMG activity of the abdominal muscles than the bent knee leg raise when participants performed three reps of each exercise.

Nonetheless, this does not mean that the straight knee leg raise is inherently superior to the bent knee leg raise for developing the abdominal muscles in the same way that squatting 200lb (91kg) is not necessarily better than squatting 150lb (68kg) for inducing quad hypertrophy. If you have the strength to perform both leg raise variations with sound technique, you simply will have to perform greater reps per set with the bent knee position compared to the straight knee position to achieve the same hypertrophic stimulus. If an equal number of sets are performed until the same proximity failure (e.g., performing as many reps as you can with each condition), a very similar degree of muscle growth may be produced by 6-rep sets, 30-rep sets, or anywhere in between. Correspondingly, the bent knee variation that provides less resistance can still be just as productive for developing the abdominals as the straight knee variation for most people when leaving the same number of reps in reserve for sets of either exercise. The one exception may be for individuals with especially strong abdominals who are able to perform a very high number of bent knee leg raise reps per set before reaching a pronounced degree of muscle fatigue, indicating that the resistance from these reps are very low relative to their strength. At some point an exercise may just be providing too low of a resistance to optimize how much muscle growth that a set can stimulate even if the set is performed until failure. Specifically where that threshold lies is currently unclear, but that should not be any concern for you unless you are able to perform over 30 reps per set, which will not be an issue for the vast majority of people performing bent knee leg raises with a controlled technique through a full range of motion.

### Hanging ab straps

If you have access to hanging ab straps, you have the option of using them for this exercise by hanging them from your pull-up bar about shoulder width apart from each other and resting your upper arms on top of the straps as you otherwise use the same form described under the exercise technique section. Using straps would ensure that your grip strength would not be able to function as the limiting factor during your set without otherwise affecting how your abdominal and hip flexor muscles are trained. Correspondingly, utilizing straps would remove the stimulus this exercise can provide to your forearm and hand muscles involved with gripping. Performing the exercise with straps would also result in the shoulders maintaining a moderately flexed position (as the upper arms remain parallel to the ground) in contrast to the fully flexed shoulder position that is adopted when hanging on the bar. This can result in strap usage removing the stimulus to improve or maintain shoulder mobility in an overhead position that can be experienced when hanging on the bar. On the other hand, the shoulder extensor muscles (e.g., lats, teres major, rear delt) will have to act isometrically (statically) to maintain the moderately flexed shoulder position when using straps. While these muscles are likely more engaged when performing the exercise with straps compared to without straps, they are still unlikely to be loaded enough to provide any meaningful training effect for most lifters.

### Is your grip strength limiting you?

As a simple approach you can use if you are unsure whether grip strength is limiting your performance rather than abdominal muscle strength, you can perform a set of as many quality reps as you can without using straps and compare your rep total to a set using straps. To perform an appropriate comparison, you would want to ensure that you are using the same technique for each condition, which will mean using the same range of motion and tempo. Additionally, you would want to perform each condition when you are similarly recovered. Practically, resting at least three minutes between the two sets should allow most fatigue to dissipate by the time you perform the second set, therefore enabling a useful comparison in terms of how performance of this exercise may be affected by your grip strength. However, if you really wish to optimize the validity of your self-assessment, you can compare your rep performance between these conditions on a second day where you reverse the order of the conditions. If you performed the set without straps prior to the set using straps on the first day, you perform the set using straps before the set without straps on a different day to control for how any fatigue resulting from the first set may influence the second set. When comparing how many reps you complete during each condition, you will be well informed as to how grip strength may be affecting performance of this exercise for you as an individual. If you find that you can complete meaningfully more reps when using straps, your grip strength is likely limiting you during this exercise, which can consequently limit the stimulus it provides to your abdominal muscles.
