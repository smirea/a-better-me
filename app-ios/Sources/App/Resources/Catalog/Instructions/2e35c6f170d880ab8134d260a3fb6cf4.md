## Setting up

Using a dedicated seal row bench is ideal for this exercise due to its convenience and its ability to help you train through a full range of motion, but you can alternatively take the time to elevate a standard flat bench without a seal row bench. Each approach is described in its own setup section, but the exercise technique can be essentially the same with either technique, so they share the technique section.

### **Setting up with a seal row bench**

1. Adjust the height of the pad or the bar holders to a setting that lets you grasp the bar with straight or slightly bent elbows once you are lying down on the pad.
   1. Which part of the bench can be adjusted varies based on the model you are using, and certain models may lack any adjustable features.
   2. Some trial and error may initially be needed to find the setting that makes it easy for you to unrack and rerack the bar.
2. Place a barbell onto the bar holders and load it with weight plates that will not touch the ground when your arms are hanging down with straight elbows when holding the bar in the starting position of your seal row.
   1. The diameter of the weight plates added to the bar is often irrelevant with an appropriately adjusted bench.
   2. However, for some people (particularly those with longer arms using non-adjustable benches), adding full diameter plates (such as 45lb (20kg) or any bumper plates) may cause the plates to contact the ground before the elbows can fully straighten during a rep. If this is the case for you, you exclusively load the bar with smaller diameter plates that will not restrict your row’s range of motion.
3. Lie down with the front of your torso flat on the bench as your neck is situated vertically above the bar.
   1. Position your head in a comfortable position, such as tucking your chin and lifting your head slightly up to keep your face off the bench.
   2. Situating yourself in a spot where your neck is situated above the bar (instead of your shoulders) will help you avoid hitting the bar holders during your set.
   3. While some seal row benches have one continuous flat pad to support your torso and legs in alignment, others have a torso pad and a lower leg pad onto which you can kneel with your hips bent.
4. Reach down and firmly grasp the bar with a near shoulder-width underhand grip such that your palms are facing forward.
5. Unrack the bar by lifting it up just above the bar holders before moving the bar slightly backward to position it directly under your shoulders. Let your arms hang with straight elbows as your shoulder blades are pulled apart from the bar’s weight.

### **Setting up without a seal row bench**

1. Symmetrically elevate each end of a flat bench on top of sturdy objects like aerobic steps with risers, bumper plates, plyometric boxes, or deadlift blocks. Elevate it to the level where you can just grasp a loaded barbell under you while lying face down on the bench
   1. A non-adjustable flat bench is generally preferable to an adjustable bench, since the former type of bench typically has more space under it for bar clearance.
   2. When elevating the bench, make sure that each base is level and that you stack the bench onto only objects that are able to handle the combined weight of you, your barbell, and the bench.
   3. To row through your longest range of motion, the bench should ideally be high enough that you can grasp the bar with straight elbows at the end of your setup. If you can grasp the bar with a bent elbow position, a greater degree of bench elevation would be preferable. Some trial and error may be needed to determine specifically how high a bench elevation is optimal for you.
2. Place a barbell centered under the bench near one of its ends, and load it with weight plates.
   1. The barbell should be situated in a spot where you can reach it while the bench supports your torso and thighs, which will involve positioning the barbell close to one end of the bench.
   2. If you are using an adjustable bench, place the barbell under the part of the bench that has the most space available under it.
   3. The diameter of the largest plates you add to the bar will affect how high off the ground the barbell lies, so both the size of your plates and your bench’s height can affect your row’s range of motion and your ability to reach the bar initially.
3. Lie down with the front of your torso and thighs flat on the bench as your shoulders are positioned above the bar.
   1. Based on your preference, you can either keep your knees straight or bent.
   2. Position your head in a comfortable position, such as tucking your chin and lifting your head slightly up to keep your face off the bench.
4. Reach down and firmly grasp the bar with a near shoulder-width underhand grip such that your palms are facing forward.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue keeping your core braced from this point on.
2. Pull the bar up toward your abdomen by driving your elbows simultaneously backward and upward while pulling your shoulder blades together.
   1. Rather than lifting your chest up through excessive lower back arching, keep your chest and the rest of your torso in contact with the bench throughout your set.
   2. Row through your full range of motion, which ideally results in your elbows finishing at shoulder height or close to this point. Your setup can affect your end range, since the bottom of your bench may physically determine how high you may lift the bar.
   3. As a cue for training your upper back muscles, you can think about squeezing your shoulder blades together at the top of the pull as if you were trying to pinch a pencil with your shoulder blades.
   4. Rather than meaningfully flaring your elbows out away from your sides, naturally keeping them tucked fairly close to your sides should feel the most comfortable.
3. Reverse the movement by lowering the bar in a controlled manner until you have returned to the starting position.
   1. If your bench is tall enough to do so, lower the bar until your elbows have fully straightened.
   2. If the plates reach the ground before your elbows have fully straightened, you can finish lowering the bar once the plates lightly tap the ground.
   3. Allow the weight to pull your shoulder blades apart to stretch your upper back muscles.
   4. You do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, but you should actively control the entire descent rather than letting the weight pull your arms down rapidly.
4. Alternate between steps 1 and 2 until you have completed your set.
   1. If you are using a seal row bench, you can rerack the bar following your last rep.
   2. If you are using a flat bench elevated on objects, you set the bar onto the ground once you have finished your set.

## Description

This exercise effectively trains much of the musculature of your middle and upper back, including your lat, teres major, rear delt, traps, and rhomboids.
