## Setting up

1. Attach your desired load (e.g., weight plates or a kettlebell) to a pull-up/dip belt around your waist, and stand upright under a pull-up bar.
   1. The back of your belt should be securely resting on top of your buttocks, and the belt’s chain or strap (depending on the belt model) should be looped through the center hole of a weight plate or under the handle of a kettlebell. If you have a loading pin on your belt, you can alternatively loop the chain or strap through the loading pin after adding weight plates.
   2. It will be easiest if you carry the weight over to the pull-up bar and add the weight to your belt while you are under the bar, rather than adding the weight to your belt farther away from the bar before walking over to the bar.
   3. As an alternative to using a pull-up/dip belt, you can wear a weighted vest.
   4. While less stable and more awkward than using a belt or weighted vest, you also have the option of holding a dumbbell between your thighs or feet, but you will be limited in how much weight you can add when using a dumbbell.
2. Reach up overhead and firmly grasp the pull-up bar with your palms facing forward (i.e., an overhand grip) and your hands situated well outside shoulder width.
   1. Positioning your hands approximately 50% wider than shoulder width is appropriate; however, a wide grip encompasses a range of grip widths, and the specific width you grasp the bar is up to your preference.
   2. What you consider a wide grip may also be influenced by the grip width you use for your standard pull-up, which you can widen to individualize your grip. For instance, for a wide grip, place your index finger on the bar just outside where you would otherwise place your little finger when performing a standard pull-up.
   3. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   4. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide up your hand while hanging on it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
3. With your elbows straightened, lift your feet up off the ground by bending your knees to begin hanging on the bar.

## Exercise technique

1. While hanging on the bar with straight elbows, simultaneously pull your shoulder blades down and mildly arch your middle and upper back to assume a “proud chest” that is angled slightly upward.
   1. Maintain this “proud chest” posture throughout your set.
2. Pull your chest up toward the bar by driving your elbows down and toward your sides as you progressively bend your elbows.
   1. Since the bar is fixed, your elbows will not naturally drive downward; however, maintaining the intention helps you pull yourself upward with an appropriate technique that effectively trains your back muscles.
   2. Strive to pull through your full available range of motion, which commonly results in the upper arms being close to the sides of the torso and the elbows being bent enough that the forearms contact the biceps. However, individuals may have different ranges of motion.
   3. Rather than swinging your legs, statically keep them in a similar position throughout the rep.
3. Reverse the motion by lowering your body in a controlled manner until you have returned to the starting position with your elbows straightened and arms fully overhead.
   1. You do not need to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, but you should actively control the entire descent rather than simply dropping.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set and are ready to return your feet to the ground before releasing the bar.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop your lat and teres major while also meaningfully training your biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis.
