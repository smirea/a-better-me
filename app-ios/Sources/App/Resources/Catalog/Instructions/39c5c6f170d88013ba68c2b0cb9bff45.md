## Setting up
1. Adjust the rack’s J-hooks to near mid-chest height, place a cambered squat bar on top of them, and face the center of the bar with a near shoulder-width stance.
    1. The optimal starting height should place the bar slightly above the tops of the J-hooks, with your feet flat on the floor, after you stand upright with the bar on your back.
    2. If you later need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground while walking out in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks, you initially positioned the J-hooks too high. Walking with your heels raised increases the risk of losing your balance and should be avoided when you have a bar on your back.
2. Based on your preference, grasp the horizontal bar with a somewhat outside shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you, or grasp the vertical posts.
    1. If you have the mobility to grasp the horizontal bar comfortably, you can set up just as you would with a straight bar good morning.
    2. If using a straight bar prompts you to feel pain or discomfort in your shoulders, elbows, and/or wrists due to mobility limitations and/or prior injuries, holding the vertical posts may be more comfortable for you.
    3. Which part of the vertical posts is ideal to hold can vary based on the design of your specific bar and your mobility. The appropriate height for your hand placement should feel comfortable and stable to you once you are under the bar, while allowing you to keep your upper back tight. Finding which hand position best accomplishes these objectives for you as an individual can require some trial and error.
3. Shallowly bend your hips and knees before walking under the bar and positioning it on your upper back in a spot that feels comfortable and stable for you.
    1. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should be securely resting on the muscles of your upper back when squeezing your shoulder blades together.
    2. If you feel the bar is putting pressure on your neck, it is most likely placed too high on your back. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
    3. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have it placed too low on your back.
    4. While you can use a lower bar placement, this variation is often performed with the bar resting on the muscular shelf formed by your traps above your rear delts (on the back of your shoulders) and above the bony ridge running across your shoulder blades (known as the scapular spine).
    5. When positioning the bar on your upper back, you may need to adjust your grip if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
4. Use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of good morning reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins prior to reracking the bar onto the J-hooks.
5. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of the lowering phase of the good morning.
6. Repeat steps 2 and 3 of the setup before proceeding to the “exercise technique” section.
## Exercise technique
1. Unrack the bar by standing up in a quarter squat-like manner as you straighten your knees while pulling down on the bar to keep it positioned on your upper back.
2. Take a couple of steps backward and position your feet into approximately a hip-width to shoulder-width stance with the soles of your feet fully contacting the floor.
    1. Alternatively, you can adopt a wide stance outside shoulder width if you prefer.
    2. With a closer stance, pointing your toes straight ahead tends to feel comfortable, but pointing toes somewhat out to the sides typically feels better with a wider stance. Use whichever degree of toe flair feels best to you.
3. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. Continue bracing your core from this point onward to help you maintain a rigid back position while using your lower body muscles to lift the weight.
4. While allowing your knees to bend mildly, sit your buttocks backward to hinge at your hips in a controlled manner as you keep your lower back flat and chest up. Only start the descent once the bar is motionless.
    1. Avoid starting your descent if the bar is still oscillating following your walkout. You may need to stand still for a moment longer compared to when you use a straight bar.
    2. To prevent the bar from swinging excessively, it may be helpful to descend a bit slower than you typically do when back squatting. While you do not need to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, you should clearly be in control of the entire descent.
    3. While you hinge at your hips, your torso will transition to an angle that is closer to horizontal as you lower the bar.
    4. Most of the motion should come from your hips, and your shins should remain vertical throughout your set, but do not keep your knees fully straight. You do not need to focus on bending your knees, since the appropriately mild degree of knee bend can naturally occur when you sit your buttocks backward without intentionally keeping your knees locked.
    5. As a cue, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks while your hands are carrying something.
    6. Throughout the set, keep your feet fully planted on the ground and your neck near a neutral position, as when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.
5. Continue hinging at your hips through the full range of motion in which you can comfortably move.
    1. How deeply you can hinge without rounding your lower back or excessively bending your knees depends on your hamstring flexibility.
    2. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch and can no longer sit back any farther, you have reached your personal end range. Descending past this point would require you to begin rounding your back or bending your knees in a squat-like manner.
    3. If you positioned them appropriately, the safety pins should not restrict how far you can hip hinge, but if you are able to hinge until the bar contacts the safety pins, you can lightly tap the safety pins with the bar without resting the bar on the pins.
6. Reverse the movement by thrusting your hips forward to lift your torso upright into the starting position.
    1. The lifting phase should simply be a reversal of what you just performed, so keep your lower back flat, chest up, core braced, and feet fully on the ground.
    2. When you have finished lifting the bar, you should be able to draw a straight line from your shoulders through your hips to the middle of your feet when viewed from the side.
7. Alternate between steps 4-6 until you have completed your set.
    1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
    2. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.
8. Rerack the bar by walking forward until the barbell contacts the back of each J-cup, and you are ready to rest the bar on the J-hooks by once again bending your knees in a quarter squat-like manner.
## Description
While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise dynamically trains your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
