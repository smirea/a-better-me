## Setting up
1. Add weight plates of your desired load to a barbell situated on the ground.
    1. The size of the weight plates you add to the bar will determine how high the bar lies above the ground, which consequently affects your range of motion. Adding a pair of full-diameter plates (like 45lb (20kg) plates or any bumper plates) will elevate the bar to the standard height (about 9in (23cm)) when deadlifting from the floor. If you only use smaller diameter plates, the exercise will function as a deficit deadlift with a greater range of motion that may exceed the range of motion in which some people wish to train if they lack the mobility to use appropriate technique through the deeper range. If you do not have access to bumper plates and are using loads that are not heavy enough to utilize 45lb (20kg) plates, you can perform a block pull or rack pull with the bar elevated to where it would be when deadlifting from the floor with full diameter plates.
2. With your toes pointing straight ahead or slightly out to the sides, stand in an approximately hip-width stance with the center of the bar just behind you.
    1. While using a near hip-width stance places most people into a viable position, the precise stance width that is ideal for you as an individual may be influenced by your body structure and mobility, so you can feel free to modify your stance a bit based on where you feel strong and comfortable.
    2. You can similarly decide whether to point your toes straight ahead or slightly out to the side based on what feels best to you.
3. With your arms pointing straight down toward the bar and your shoulders packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up), sit your hips back and down while bending your knees until your hands are level with the bar.
    1. Based on your preference, you can let your back modestly round or aim to keep it flat with your chest up. If you adopt a mild degree of back rounding, strive to have it distributed throughout the entirety of your lower, middle, and upper back rather than only rounding your lower back. Setting up with a somewhat rounded back can help you keep your hips not as far behind the bar, which reduces the degree to which your hamstrings can get in the way of the bar when lifting it up. However, you can set up with a flat back if that feels more comfortable to you.
    2. Compared to a conventional deadlift, you can bend your knees to a greater degree and let them track further forward while not sitting your hips as far back during this variation.
    3. Throughout this exercise, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
    4. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and help engage your back muscles.
4. With your elbows fully straightened, firmly grasp the bar with an approximately shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
    1. As an alternative to the double overhand grip, you can naturally use a mixed grip (with one palm facing forward and the other facing backward) or lifting straps with the double overhand grip if you wish to do so.
    2. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
    3. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. Continue bracing your core from this point on.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
    1. Doing so can help you wedge your hips into an advantageous starting position through a maneuver known as pulling the slack out of the bar.
    2. When creating “pre-tension,” pressing through the ground should pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
3. Drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar as you “push the floor away” while extending (straightening) your hips and knees without losing back tightness.
    1. Regardless of whether you set up with a modestly rounded back or a flat back, you should keep your back locked in a stable position when starting the rep, rather than letting it dynamically round.
    2. As you move about your knees and hips, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the lift rather than inclining further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
4. Once the bar reaches near the middle of your knees, focus on thrusting your hips forward as you continue extending your knees and hips until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
    1. It is normal for the bar to drag up your hamstrings during this step when appropriately keeping the bar close to your body.
    2. Thrusting your hips forward is key to minimizing how much your hamstrings may get in the way of the bar (which can be more notable for lifters with thick thighs).
    3. Lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
    4. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your shoulders when viewed from the side.
5. Reverse the movement by bending your knees and hips to lower the bar until the plates have returned to resting on the ground.
    1. Like when setting up, you could keep your back flat or let it round modestly, but you should avoid an excessive degree of lower back rounding regardless of which approach you prefer to use.
    2. Letting your knees move further forward than you would with a conventional deadlift can help keep your hamstrings from sitting onto the bar. By repeatedly performing this exercise, you can better determine specifically how much forward knee travel feels best with your body proportions.
6. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the ground for a moment (rather than simply tapping the ground and immediately deadlifting the bar up again) before continuing to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
    1. While you do not need to pause with the bar on the ground for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferred between reps.
    2. You will be able to brace most effectively when you breathe in between reps while the weight is on the ground rather than during a rep. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before the next rep.
## Description
While still training your erector spinae and gluteus maximus, this exercise can train your quads more effectively than most other deadlift variations.
