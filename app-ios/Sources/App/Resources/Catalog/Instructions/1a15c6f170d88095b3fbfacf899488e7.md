## Setting up

You can set up for this exercise in essentially the same way you would for a close grip bench that situates your hands shoulder-width apart without arching your back.

1. Determine whether you will set up within a power rack, which could be modified to perform a variety of barbell lifts and would require you to position a mobile flat bench within the rack, or within a specific bench press station in which the bench is fused to the rack.
2. With a power rack, position the J-hooks to a height that initially allows you to grasp the bar with slightly bent elbows once you are set up on the bench. With a bench press station, find which J-hook height places you closest to this position.
   1. After fully straightening your elbows to lift the bar off the J-hooks, the bar should ideally be close to 2in (5cm) above the top of the J-hooks while keeping your shoulder blades tucked back together.
   2. The proper height easily enables you to avoid bumping the J-hooks when unracking the bar while simultaneously not requiring much effort to lift the bar initially.
   3. Some trial and error may be needed to find the best J-hook height for you.
3. After situating a bar on top of the appropriate J-hooks, use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of JM press reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins/spotter arms prior to re-racking the bar onto the J-hooks.
4. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins/spotter arms to be slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descended by the end of the lowering phase of your JM press.
   1. The safety pins/spotter arms should be high enough to stop the bar from reaching your neck.
   2. While the safety pins/spotter arms can provide additional safety, you should be able to close grip bench press the bar up if you happen to fail a JM press rep.
   3. If you are not training with safety pins/spotter arms that can be positioned above your neck, you can also avoid adding a pair of collars to the end of the bar. This would allow you to dump the weight to one side and allow the weights to slide off the bar if you become pinned underneath the bar after failing a rep. The con of not using collars is that the plates can accidentally slide to one side when pressing if you happen to tilt the bar asymmetrically, but it can be an acceptable tradeoff to make sure that you can avoid being pinned if you fail a rep.
5. Lie down with your back on the bench. Position yourself so that your eyes are close to being directly underneath the barbell, and your knees are bent with your feet on the floor approximately shoulder-width apart.
   1. Based on what feels most stable, you can position the soles of your feet flat on the ground with your shins close to being vertical, or you can bend your knees beyond this point to dig the balls of your feet and toes into the ground with your heels off the ground.
   2. Similarly, you can decide whether you point your toes moderately out to the sides or straight ahead based on your preference.
6. Firmly grasp the bar with your palms facing forward (resulting in an overhand grip) with your hands near shoulder width. Let the bar sit on top of the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) with reasonably straight wrists.
   1. A shoulder-width grip is an appropriate starting point, but you can try mildly different grip widths over time to determine where you personally feel best. The proper grip width should allow your elbows, wrists, and shoulders to feel comfortable while emphasizing the triceps to a greater degree than your close grip bench press.
   2. The traditional technique for this exercise involves a thumbless grip with the thumbs placed on the same side of the bar as the other fingers. However, if you are new to this exercise, you have the option of wrapping your thumbs around the bar to fully enclose the bar with your hands, which can make it easier to hold the bar securely.
   3. While your wrists do not need to be completely straight, you should avoid grasping the bar with your wrists cocked back into an excessively extended position that may contribute to an increased risk of experiencing wrist discomfort. Simply squeezing the bar as hard as possible will usually keep your wrists from cocking back excessively.
7. Assume a “proud chest” posture that puffs your chest slightly up toward the ceiling by tightly pulling your shoulder blades back together. Maintain this chest position throughout your set.
   1. As a cue for pulling your shoulder blades back, you can think about trying to squeeze a pencil between your shoulder blades. This should prompt you to feel your upper back muscles contracting like at the end range of a rowing exercise.
   2. Maintain the natural curvature of your lower back, like when you are normally standing upright. Regardless of whether you meaningfully arch your lower back when bench pressing, the JM press involves a mostly flat back position.

## Exercise technique

1. Drive your feet into the ground, squeeze your glutes, and brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing in this manner throughout your set. This will help create full-body tension and allow your body to function as a stable platform from which to press.
   2. From this starting position, your feet should be firmly dug into the ground, while your buttocks, upper back, and head are firmly planted on the bench. You will maintain these five points of contact throughout your whole set to maximize your stability and performance.
2. Press the bar off the J-hooks by straightening your elbows, and move the bar forward away from the J-hooks until it is approximately above your shoulders, where you will hold it for a moment before starting to lower the bar.
3. Lower the bar toward your throat or chin in a controlled manner by progressively bending your elbows. Keep your elbows higher than your chest (when viewed from the side) while flaring your elbows out about 45 degrees from your sides.
   1. Avoid letting your wrist cock back, which would point your palms away from your forearms. Rather, curl your wrists mildly to angle your palms closer toward your forearms while securely holding the bar.
   2. In contrast to a strict lying triceps extension, you will somewhat lower your elbows, but to a meaningfully lesser degree than with a close grip bench press.
   3. The degree to which your forearms are angled relative to the ground can indicate whether you are moving your elbows (through shoulder motion) appropriately. By the end of the lowering phase, your forearms should be closer to horizontal than vertical. If your forearms remain closer to vertical at the bottom, you are excessively lowering your elbows and performing too much of a pressing movement. If your forearms angle beyond horizontal (with your wrists lower than your elbows), you are keeping your elbows excessively high and performing an exercise closer to a strict triceps extension than is intended.
   4. The amount of elbow flare is similar to what you can use for a close grip bench press. Strive to reach the middle ground between 90 degrees of elbow flare (which would cause your upper arms to form a “T-shape” with your torso) and 0 degrees of elbow flare (which would result in your upper arms being completely parallel to your torso).
   5. When lowering the bar toward your throat or chin, the bar will track slightly backward (toward your face) but to a notably lesser degree than with a strict triceps extension.
4. Continue lowering the bar until you have fully bent your elbows or until the bar is just about to contact your chin or throat.
   1. The end range can be determined by when your biceps contact your forearms, so what constitutes your personal end range can be influenced by the circumference of your arms.
   2. If the bar is about to touch your chin or throat before you have fully bent your elbows, you naturally can finish lowering the bar before achieving a full elbow bend.
5. Reverse the movement by straightening your elbows to lift the bar up into the starting position.
   1. You should finish with your elbows fully straightened and the bar situated above your shoulders.
   2. Your elbows will move upward and inward during the lifting phase, but you can predominantly focus on extending your elbows to lift the weight.
   3. Uncurl your wrists while lifting the bar to maintain a secure grip.
6. Alternate between steps 3-5 until you have completed your set and are ready to rerack the bar.
7. After completing the lifting phase of your last rep, keep the bar motionless over your shoulders for about one second before moving the bar back until it contacts the back of both J-hooks.
8. Bend your elbows slightly to lower the bar onto the J-hooks to finish reracking the weight.
   1. Only begin lowering the bar after you have already contacted the back of the J-hooks.

## Description

While training the pec major and front delts to a lesser degree than a close grip bench press, this exercise can develop the triceps particularly well.
