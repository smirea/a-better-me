## Setting up
1. After adding weight plates to the barbell situated on the ground, stand upright close in front of the middle of the bar as the bar is approximately over the middle of your feet.
    1. For most lifters, this situates the bar about one inch (2.5cm) away from the shins.
    2. The size of the weight plates you add to the bar will determine how high the bar lies above the ground, which consequently affects your range of motion. Adding a pair of 45lb (20kg) plates (or 25kg plates common in gyms outside the USA) or bumper plates will elevate the bar to the standard height (about 9in (23cm)) when deadlifting from the floor. When only using smaller diameter plates, the exercise will function as a deficit sumo deadlift with a greater range of motion that may exceed the range of motion in which some people wish to train if they lack the mobility to perform a deficit deadlift with appropriate technique. If you do not have access to bumper plates and are using loads that are not heavy enough to utilize the largest diameter plates, you can perform a sumo block pull or rack pull with the bar elevated to where it would be when deadlifting from the floor with large diameter plates.
2. Spread your legs apart into a wide stance that is clearly outside shoulder width, with your feet moderately rotated out away from your sides.
    1. Provided that your feet are wider apart than your shoulders but closer together than the plates, you can use a range of stance widths for your sumo deadlift.
    2. The ideal stance width can meaningfully vary among different individuals based on someone’s body structure and hip mobility.
    3. Trying out different foot positions will normally be required to determine which stance specifically feels best to you.
    4. As a starting point, you can widen your feet as far apart as you feel comfortable and stable while being able to push your knees out approximately over your heels, resulting in close to vertical shins. You will push your knees out when descending to reach the bar, so subsequent aspects of the setup can help you gauge if you should adjust your stance.
    5. The ideal amount of toe flare also varies among different lifters, but you can start with your toes pointing about 30-45 degrees out to the side before adjusting from there based on what feels best to you. Zero degrees of toe flare would mean your toes are pointing straight forward, while 90 degrees would mean your toes are pointing directly out to your side.
3. Direct your arms straight down toward the ground with straight elbows as your shoulders are packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up).
    1. Maintain this position from here onward.
4. Sit your buttocks back to hinge at your hips while bending your knees and pushing your knees out until your hands are level with the bar.
    1. More motion should come from your hips than your knees, and your knees should essentially not track forward (although a slight degree of forward knee tracking can be used if that feels best to you).
    2. As a cue for hinging, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks as you sit back while your hands are carrying something. After you have hinged through your available hip range motion, you can bend your knees further to reach the bar.
    3. Try to push your knees far enough out to the sides that they are vertically stacked over your heels.
    4. Throughout this exercise, keep your lower back looking flat near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright, rather than excessively rounding your lower back while doing so.
    5. Similarly, keep your chest up in a “proud chest” posture and your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
    6. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and engage your back muscles.
    7. The bar should be directly in front of your shins.
5. With your shoulders stacked vertically above the bar, firmly grasp the bar with a shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
    1. Your arms should be directed straight down such that they form approximately 90-degree angles with the bar.
    2. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
    3. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath). This should expand the front and sides of your abdominals outward as you tense these muscles, like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. Strive to tighten the entirety of your core to form a “360-degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
    3. Continue bracing your core throughout your set. This will help you maintain a rigid trunk position while you move through your hips and knees.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
    1. This can help you wedge your hips into an advantageous starting position through a maneuver known as pulling the slack out of the bar.
    2. When creating “pre-tension,” pressing through the ground should pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
3. Drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar as you “push the floor away” by extending (straightening) your hips and knees.
    1. Keep your chest up, back flat, and knees pushed out.
    2. As your hips and chest rise at the same rate, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the pull. Avoid letting your torso incline further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
    3. Keep the bar very close to your legs throughout the rep.
4. After the bar reaches near your knees, focus on thrusting your hips forward until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
    1. Lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
    2. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your back when viewed from the side.
5. Reverse the movement in a controlled manner by hinging at your hips and bending your knees to lower the bar until the plates lightly contact the ground.
    1. You can initially focus on sitting your hips back while keeping your shins vertical and allowing your knees to bend naturally. After the bar has cleared your knees, you can further bend your knees and hips to finish lowering the bar.
    2. Continue keeping your back flat and chest up.
6. Immediately after the plates touch the ground, fluidly initiate your next rep by repeating step 3 before continuing onto steps 4 and 5 again.
    1. Stay braced with full body tension when transitioning to your next rep.
    2. Using this touch-and-go deadlift technique should provide a mild rebound from the plates tapping the ground. This can make the start of the next rep a bit easier compared to if you let the plates come to a dead stop on the ground between reps.
    3. Do not bounce the plates off the ground in an uncontrolled manner that contributes to technique degradation.
    4. Throughout the entirety of the set, you should be in complete control over the weight as you prioritize using essentially the same form for your touch-and-go reps as you did with the first rep.
7. Alternate between steps 3-6 until you have completed your set.
    1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in the fully upright position after locking out a rep before you resume fully bracing your core.
## Description
While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise can dynamically train your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and quads.
