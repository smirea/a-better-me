## Setting up
1. In a deadlift motion (like what is subsequently described in the technique section) pick up a kettlebell and set it onto the ground with its handle facing up.
    1. Particularly when using what you consider to be a heavy weight, initially lift it up using your hips and knees with your lower back near a flat position rather than being excessively rounded over at your lower back.
2. Place a pair of aerobic steps, large diameter weight plates, or deadlift blocks on each side of the kettlebell close enough together that you can place your feet fully on them with a near shoulder width stance.
    1. While you can use a variety of deficit heights, selecting no greater than a moderate deficit height (less than or equal to 4in (10cm)) is initially recommended to facilitate the appropriate technique.
    2. You want to pick a height that allows you to keep your lower back flat at the bottom of the rep, and the height should still allow you to use a deadlift technique rather than squatting the weight up (which can occur with an excessively large deficit).
    3. Any of the aforementioned objects can be effectively used depending on your equipment availability and the height you wish to use.
    4. Aerobic steps without risers are typically near 4in (10cm), bumper plates usually range from about 1-3.5in (2.5-8.9cm) in thickness, and 45lb (20kg) cast iron weight plates are often near 1in (2.5cm) thick.
    5. If you use weight plates, you can stack them on top of each other to adjust your height.
3. With the soles of your feet completely on the elevated surfaces, stand upright in a near shoulder width stance with the handle of the kettlebell between the middle of your feet as your toes are slightly turned out to the sides or pointing straight ahead.
    1. While using a near shoulder width stance places most people into a viable position, the precise stance width that is ideal for you as an individual may be influenced by your body structure and mobility, so you can feel free to modify your stance a bit based on where you feel strong and comfortable.
    2. You can similarly decide whether to point your toes straight ahead or slightly out to the side based on what feels best to you.
4. With your arms directed down toward the ground, straight elbows, and your shoulders packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up), hinge at your hips while bending your knees until your hands are level with the handle.
    1. More motion should come from your hips than your knees as you descend, and your knees should not track particularly far forward, but you can allow your knees to shift slightly forward rather than maintaining completely vertical shins.
    2. As a cue for hinging, you can think about closing a car door with your buttock as you sit back while your hands are carrying something, and after you have hinged through your available hip range motion, you can bend your knees further to reach the handle.
    3. Throughout this exercise, keep your lower back looking flat near a neutral position like when you are normally standing upright rather than excessively rounding your lower back while doing so.
    4. Similarly, keep your neck near a neutral position like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
    5. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and engage your back muscles.
5. With your hands close together and your elbows still straight, firmly grasp the handle with an overhand grip, resulting in your palms facing backward.
    1. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the handle with the handle pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
    2. If you situate the handle closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the handle can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward as you tense these muscles like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. Strive to tighten the entirety of your core to form a “360 degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
    3. You will continue bracing your core throughout your set, which will help you maintain a rigid trunk position while you move through your hips and knees.
2. Before lifting the kettlebell, apply “pre-tension” into the handle as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force that would lift the weight if it was only half as heavy
    1. Doing so can help you wedge your hips into an advantageous starting position and pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
3. While keeping your chest up and back flat, drive through your legs to begin lifting the kettlebell as you “push the floor away” while extending (straightening) your hips and knees.
    1. As you move about your knees and hips, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the pull rather than inclining further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
4. After the handle reaches near the middle of your shins, focus on thrusting your hips forward as you continue extending your hips and knees until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
    1. You should lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
    2. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your back.
5. While keeping your back flat, reverse the movement by hinging at your hips while bending your knees to lower the kettlebell until it has returned to resting on the ground.
    1. You can initially focus on sitting your buttock back while keeping your shins vertical and allowing your knees to bend naturally until the handle has cleared your knees, after which you can allow your knees to track mildly forward as you further bend your knees and hips to finish lowering the kettlebell.
6. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the ground for a moment (rather than simply tapping the ground and immediately deadlifting the kettlebell up again) before continuing to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
    1. While you do not need to pause with the kettlebell on the ground for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferring between reps.
    2. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps with the kettlebell on the ground.
    3. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the next rep.
## Description
While also training your back’s spinal extensor muscles, this exercise can train much of lower body musculature including your buttock’s gluteus maximus along with the hamstrings, quads, and hip adductor muscles in your thighs.
