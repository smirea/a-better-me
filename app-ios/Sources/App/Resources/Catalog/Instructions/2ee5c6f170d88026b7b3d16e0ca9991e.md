## Setting up

1. Pick up a pair of kettlebells and stand upright in approximately a hip-width stance while holding the kettlebells with straight elbows.
   1. For the most stable grip, firmly wrap your fingers around the handles with the handles pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   2. If you are using kettlebells that you consider fairly heavy, reaching forward from a bent-over position to pick up both kettlebells simultaneously from a rack may be awkward and challenging.
   3. For an easier setup, you can pick up one kettlebell, carry it, place it on a flat bench (or sturdy box), and move the other kettlebell onto the bench before picking up both kettlebells from a mechanically advantaged position.
   4. Pick up the kettlebells with a deadlift or squat motion, where you lift them by extending (straightening) your hips and knees while keeping your back flat.
2. Position your palms right next to the outer aspect of the front of your thighs so that your palms angle diagonally backward and in toward each other.
   1. This results in a semi-overhand grip, which is a hybrid between an overhand grip that would direct your palms straight backward (with the kettlebells directly in front of your body) and a neutral grip that would direct your palms straight in toward your sides (with the kettlebells directly to the sides of your body).
   2. Throughout the exercise, keep your palms facing this way and your neck should be near a neutral position, as when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.

## Exercise technique

1. Shift your weight onto one foot and take a small step backward with your other foot so that its toes are just slightly behind your lead foot’s heel while keeping your feet hip-width apart.
   1. Naturally let your knees slightly bend.
   2. Avoid stepping inward to the extent that your rear foot is directly behind your lead foot. Keeping your feet hip-width apart can help keep you stable.
2. Place the balls and toes of your rear foot on the ground while keeping its midfoot and heel off the ground.
3. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, as if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or a forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing your core throughout your set to help you maintain a rigid back position.
4. While continuing to bear almost all your weight through your lead foot, sit your buttocks backward to hinge at your hips as you let your knees mildly bend.
   1. Throughout the set, keep your lower back flat, chest up, and lead foot fully planted on the ground.
   2. Your rear leg’s purpose is to assist with maintaining your balance so that the exercise can feel more stable than a true single leg Romanian deadlift.
   3. If scales were under both of your feet, the scale under your lead foot should ideally show that you are bearing at least 90% of your weight through your front leg.
   4. Continue keeping your elbows straight with the kettlebells just in front of your legs. Avoid letting the kettlebells drift forward away from you, which may be more likely to occur if you think about “bending over” rather than “sitting back.”
   5. Strive to keep your lead leg’s shin vertical with your weight distributed throughout the entirety of your lead foot. If you concentrate all your weight through your heel, your knee may track backward into an excessively straightened position. If you bear too much weight through the front of your foot, your knee may track forward and bend excessively.
   6. While motion will occur at both hips, you should focus on sitting your lead leg’s hip back (as if you were trying to close a car door with your buttocks) while letting your rear leg’s hip naturally move with it.
5. In a controlled manner, continue hinging at your hips through the full range of motion in which you can comfortably move.
   1. Lifters with normal flexibility can typically hinge until their hands are at least below their knees, but how deeply you can hinge without rounding your back or excessively bending your knees depends on your hamstring flexibility.
   2. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch and can no longer sit back any farther, you have reached your personal end range. Descending past this point would require you to begin rounding your back or bending your knees in a squat-like manner.
6. Reverse the movement by thrusting your hips forward to lift your torso upright into the starting position.
   1. The lifting phase should simply be a reversal of what you just performed, so keep your lower back flat, chest up, core braced, lead foot fully on the ground, and the kettlebells close to your legs.
   2. Avoid leaning back at the top through excessive lower back arching.
7. Alternate between steps 4-6 until you have completed your set.
   1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
   2. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.
8. Following your last rep, step forward with your rear foot to position your feet symmetrically before repeating the exercise with your other leg in the lead position.
   1. Once you are ready to set the kettlebells down, step forward again to position your feet symmetrically before sitting your hips back and bending your knees to lower the kettlebells onto the ground (or rerack them).

## Description

While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
