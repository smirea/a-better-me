## Setting up

1. Adjust the J-hooks of a power rack or squat stand to near mid-chest height, and place a barbell on the J-hooks.
   1. The optimal starting height should place the bar slightly above the tops of the J-hooks, with your feet flat on the floor, after you stand upright with the bar on your back.
   2. If you later need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground while walking out in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks, you initially positioned the J-hooks too high. Walking with your heels raised increases the risk of losing your balance and should be avoided when you have a barbell on your back.
2. Set up the sturdy object near knee height that you will use to elevate your rear foot sufficiently behind the bar.
   1. A cylindrical split squat roller pad is ideal to use if you have one, but flat exercise benches are most commonly used due to their availability, and a plyometric box with a somewhat compliant surface can also be viable. Objects with a flat hard surface (like a metal plyometric box or aerobic step elevated on risers) can be used to elevate the foot, but they can feel uncomfortable, so they are not the best options.
   2. With respect to placement, you could place the object about two “foot lengths” behind where your heels will be after you unrack the bar and take a couple of steps back away from the J-hooks. When initially walking back with the bar, the goal is to move far enough away that the bar will not risk bumping the J-hooks when your torso leans mildly forward during the exercise. From this position, step backward with one foot until its big toe is just behind your other foot’s heel before doing the same with your opposite foot. You can situate the front of the elevated object where the back of your rear foot’s heel is situated after those steps. While this strategy can consistently situate most people in a viable starting position for the exercise, a range of foot placements can be effectively used, including having your front foot two and a half “foot lengths” in front of where you started (which may be even more comfortable for some people).
3. Face the center of the bar with a somewhat outside shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
   1. The proper grip width will vary from person to person, and what feels best to you will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
   2. If you are not accustomed to exercises that have a bar on your back, you may have to try out different hand placements to find one that personally suits you well.
4. Shallowly bend your hips and knees before walking under the bar and positioning it on your upper back muscles, slightly below the base of your neck, in a spot that feels comfortable and stable for you.
   1. Aim for the bar to rest on the muscular shelf formed by your traps above your rear delts on the back of your shoulders and above the bony ridge running across your shoulder blades (known as the scapular spine). This is the high bar position, which is generally recommended over the low bar position for split squats and lunges.
   2. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable on your traps will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back as you squeeze your shoulder blades back together.
   3. If you feel the bar is putting pressure on your neck, it is most likely placed too high on your back.
   4. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
   5. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have it placed too low on your back.
   6. When positioning the bar on your upper back, you may need to adjust your grip if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
5. Use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins prior to re-racking the bar onto the J-hooks.
   1. Additionally, use these initial reps to check if you have situated your elevated object an appropriate distance behind the bar. The right distance will typically allow your rear knee to tap the ground lightly at the bottom of the rep while keeping your front foot’s heel fully on the ground.
   2. If the object is excessively far behind you, your range of motion may be limited by your rear leg’s hip flexor flexibility, which would result in a deep stretching sensation in the front of your rear leg’s upper thigh before your rear knee reaches the ground.
   3. If the object is not far enough behind you, you may feel a blocked sensation in your front ankle at the bottom before your rear knee reaches the ground, indicating that you have reached the limit of your front ankle’s mobility from your knee having to track far forward. To descend any further would require lifting up your front foot’s heel off the ground in this situation.
6. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins to be slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of the lowering phase.
   1. While not required, setting up safety pins is recommended in case you lose your balance or fail a rep during your work sets.
7. Repeat steps 3 and 4 of the setup and proceed to the “exercise technique” section.

## Exercise technique

1. Pull down on the bar to keep it positioned on your upper back, and stand upright by straightening your knees and hips.
   1. Maintain this bar position throughout your set.
2. While keeping your chest up, take a couple of steps backward and position your feet approximately hip-width apart, with your toes pointing straight ahead.
   1. Strive to step back far enough that your heels are now about “two foot lengths” in front of the object behind you.
3. Shift your body weight into one foot (that will be your front foot) before placing the top surface (on the side of your shoelaces) of your other foot on top of the elevated surface with that side’s knee bent.
   1. Strive to move your rear foot backward in a straight line to keep your feet approximately hip-width apart, rather than bringing your rear foot closer toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
   2. Throughout the set, keep the entire sole of your front foot firmly planted on the ground and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
   3. If you are using a cylindrical object to elevate your foot, you can contact the surface with the front of your ankle along with your foot, but only your foot will contact the elevated surface if it is flat, like a bench.
4. From your split stance position, bend your knees and hips to lower your body in a controlled manner.
   1. Rather than letting your back round, keep your back flat and chest up throughout the exercise.
   2. You should keep your torso mostly upright, but you can allow your torso to lean forward mildly if that feels natural to you.
   3. Continue keeping the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the ground throughout the rep.
   4. Keep your knees tracking forward in line with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
5. Continue descending through the full range of motion you can while keeping your front foot’s sole fully in contact with the ground and your back flat.
   1. With the right set-up, this commonly involves lightly tapping the ground with the rear knee in the bottom position. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your rear knee, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
   2. Alternatively, some people may notice that their front leg’s hip reaches the end of its mobility before the rear knee can tap the ground, which will produce a notable stretching sensation in the front leg’s glute.
   3. What chiefly matters is moving through your individual full range of motion with high-quality technique.
   4. Regardless of where you end the descent, you should strive to move through the full range of motion you can with high-quality technique.
6. While keeping both of your feet planted, reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knees and hips to lift your body up until you have returned to an upright position in your split stance.
   1. You can finish the ascent with a slight bend in your front knee rather than fully locking it out.
   2. Like during the descent, keep your torso mostly upright and knees aligned with your feet while doing so.
7. Repeat steps 4-6 until you have finished your set. Then lift your rear foot off the object and step forward to position your feet symmetrically.
   1. Now you have the option of proceeding directly into your next set with your other leg in the forward position, or you can rerack the bar and rest before doing so.
   2. When you are ready to rerack the bar, walk forward until it contacts the back of each J-hook before mildly bending your knees and hips to rerack the bar onto the J-hooks.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop your quads, gluteus maximus, and adductor magnus.
