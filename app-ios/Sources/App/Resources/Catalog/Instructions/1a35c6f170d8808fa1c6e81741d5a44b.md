## Setting up
1. Adjust the Smith machine’s bar to a height that allows you to position the bar on your upper back and unrack it in a stable position.
    1. As an initial approximation, you can position the bar such that it is a few finger widths below the height of your collarbone.
2. Stand upright, facing the center of the unloaded bar.
    1. If your Smith machine has angled sliding rails, you can position yourself to face the side in which the bar will track slightly forward as it descends (which results in the bottom of the rails being farther in front of you than the top of the rails). Facing this way can often feel more natural for good mornings, but you can alternatively face the other direction if you personally prefer to do so.
    2. If your Smith machine has purely vertical sliding rails, you can perform the same movement regardless of which direction you face.
3. Grasp each side of the bar with a somewhat wider than shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
    1. The proper grip width will vary from person to person, and what feels best to you will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
    2. If you are not accustomed to exercises that have a bar on your back, you may have to try out different hand placements to find one that personally suits you well.
4. While holding onto the bar, shallowly bend at your hips and knees in a quarter squat-like manner to then walk under the bar and situate the bar on your upper back in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
    1. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back.
    2. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back.
    3. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
    4. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have the bar placed too low on your back.
    5. While positioning the bar on your upper back, you may have to readjust where you are grasping the bar if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
    6. If you are not accustomed to having a bar on your back, you may not feel particularly comfortable doing so, especially when it is loaded with additional weight, but whichever position you use should not be painful.
5. Position the middle of your feet under the bar in an approximately shoulder-width to hip-width stance with your toes pointing straight ahead or slightly out to the sides.
    1. You can determine specifically how wide a stance and where you point your toes based on what personally feels most comfortable and stable to you, since the ideal stance can vary among different individuals.
6. Use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of good morning reps with the form described under the exercise technique section before reracking the bar to determine the appropriate height of the safety stops.
7. Position the safety stops slightly under the lowest point or directly at the lowest point at which the bar descended during your good morning.
    1. The appropriate safety stop position should allow you to hinge through your full range of motion while being high enough to receive the bar if you fail a rep and bend your knees a bit more to set the bar on the safety stops.
8. Load the bar with your desired weight, and repeat steps 2-5.
## Exercise technique
1. While pulling down on the bar to keep it stable on your upper back, un-rack the bar by starting to stand up as you extend (straighten) your knees and hips.
    1. Keep the bar on your upper back throughout your set.
2. After the bar is fully resting on your upper back rather than the safety bars, rotate the safety hooks out away from the safety bars through a slight wrist flick motion prior to standing fully upright with the bar.
    1. Rather than being rounded or excessively arched, your lower back should be near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright.
    2. Your neck should similarly be near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead.
    3. Keep your lower back and neck near a neutral position throughout your set.
3. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Tense these muscles like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or a forcible slap.
    1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. Continue bracing your core throughout your set.
4. While allowing your knees to bend mildly, sit your buttocks backward to hinge at your hips through your available range of motion in a controlled manner.
    1. Avoid rounding your lower back as you keep your lower back mildly arched or flat throughout the exercise.
    2. Most of the motion should come from your hips, and your shins should remain in a vertical position throughout your set, but you should not keep your knees fully straightened. You do not need to focus on bending your knees, since the appropriately mild degree of knee bend can naturally occur when you sit your buttocks backward, without intentionally keeping your knees locked.
    3. As a cue, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks while your hands are carrying something, as you keep your chest up (rather than letting your chest collapse through back rounding).
    4. Keep the soles of your feet fully planted on the ground the entire time.
    5. While you hinge at your hips, the bar will naturally descend, and your torso will transition to an angle that is closer to horizontal.
    6. How deeply you are able to hinge without rounding your lower back or excessively bending your knees will be dependent on your hamstring flexibility.
    7. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch and can no longer sit back any farther, you have reached your personal end range.
    8. If your end range corresponds to where you positioned the safety stops, you can gently tap the bar against the safety stops without resting the bar on the safety stops at the bottom.
5. Reverse the movement by driving your hips forward until you have returned to the starting position with an upright torso.
    1. The lifting phase should simply be a reversal of what you just performed, so you should continue keeping your chest up without any back rounding.
    2. Once you have returned to the starting position, you should be able to draw a straight line from your shoulders through your hips to the middle of your feet when viewed from the side.
    3. Avoid driving your hips in front of your shoulders at the top, which would prompt your lower back to arch excessively. Squeezing your glutes at the end of the lifting phase can help you finish with your hips appropriately stacked under your shoulders to prevent excessive arching.
6. Alternate between steps 4 and 5 until you have completed your set.
    1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
    2. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.
7. After finishing the set, rerack the bar by bending at your hips and knees in a quarter squat-like manner as you rotate the safety hooks onto the safety bars and return the bar to resting on the safety bars.
## Description
While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
