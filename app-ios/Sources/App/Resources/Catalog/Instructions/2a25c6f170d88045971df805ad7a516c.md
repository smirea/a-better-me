## Setting up
1. Position a sturdy object (such as a thick bumper plate or ideally an aerobic step) that you will use as a step onto the floor, somewhat in front of the Smith machine’s bar.
    1. You can use a range of different heights for your stepping surface, but selecting an object that is near four inches (10cm) high (which is a typical height of many aerobic steps without additional risers) can be an appropriate starting point.
    2. If your Smith machine has angled sliding rails, you will face and put the step in front of the side in which the bar will track slightly forward as it descends (which results in the bottom of the rails being farther in front of you than the top of the rails).
    3. If your Smith machine has purely vertical sliding rails, you can perform the same movement regardless of which direction you face, so it does not matter which side you place the step.
    4. Strive to place your step far enough in front of the bar that you can keep your torso fairly upright when performing your split squat with one foot on the step, which may take a bit of trial and error if you are new to this exercise.
    5. Due to the fixed bar path of the Smith machine, situating the step directly under the bar would require you to adopt a bent-over torso position during this exercise.
    6. When using a sufficiently wide object (such as an aerobic step) as a step, you can simply place the step so that it is centered with the Smith machine’s bar.
    7. If what you use as a step does not have a large enough surface area for you to stand on it in a hip-width stance with both of your soles fully in contact with the step, situate your step slightly toward the side on which you initially will place your foot on the step.
    8. The appropriate step placement will allow you to perform your split squat with your elevated foot fully in contact with the step while your feet are hip-width apart from each other.
2. Adjust the Smith machine’s bar to a few finger widths below the height of your collarbone.
    1. The ideal starting height should allow you to position the bar on your upper back and un-rack it in a stable position with the soles of your feet fully on the ground.
    2. If you later need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground in order to unrack the bar, you initially set it excessively high.
3. Position the safety stops (that are attached to the sliding rails) so that they are slightly below the height of the bar when your rear knee taps the ground at the end of the lowering phase of your split squat.
    1. To find this point for your body, you can perform a rep or two of the split squat with the unloaded bar as described in the “exercise technique” section, but you can initially put the safety stops a little below the top of your hip bones when standing upright as an approximation.
4. Add your desired weight to the bar and face the center of the bar with approximately a hip-width stance.
5. Grasp each side of the bar with a somewhat wider than shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
    1. The proper grip width will vary from person to person, and what feels best to you will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
    2. If you are not accustomed to exercises that have a bar on your back, you may have to try out different hand placements to find one that personally suits you well.
6. While holding onto the bar, shallowly bend at your hips and knees to then walk under the bar and situate it on your upper back in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
    1. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back.
    2. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back.
    3. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
    4. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have the bar placed too low on your back.
    5. While positioning the bar on your upper back, you may have to readjust where you are grasping the bar if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
    6. If you are not accustomed to having a bar on your back, you may not feel particularly comfortable doing so, especially when it is loaded with additional weight, but whichever position you use should not be painful.
## Exercise technique
1. Pull down on the bar to tighten your upper back muscles, and unrack it by starting to stand up as you straighten your knees.
2. After the bar rests fully on your upper back rather than the safety bars, rotate the safety hooks out away from the safety bars with a slight wrist flick before standing fully upright with the bar.
3. Step up onto the step with the entirety of your sole in contact with the step while leaving your other foot on the ground.
    1. If you positioned your step closer toward one side of your body (due to using a step with a small surface area), you will step up with the foot aligned with the step, while you can use either side if your step is centered.
    2. The knee on the side of your elevated foot should be mildly bent once on the step.
4. Shift your bodyweight into your elevated foot before taking a fairly long step backward with your other foot and contacting the ground with your forefoot (ball of your foot and bottom of your toes).
    1. To shift your bodyweight into your elevated foot, you can lean your torso mildly forward by hinging at your hip as you bend that side’s knee slightly more.
    2. Once you step back and resume transferring some of your weight into your rear leg, you can return to keeping your torso in an upright position.
    3. Step far enough backward that you will be able to contact the ground with your rear knee, which may initially require trying out different step lengths if you are new to split squats.
    4. Strive to step backward in a straight line rather than stepping closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
    5. Keep the entire sole of your elevated foot firmly planted on the step and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
    6. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
5. From your split stance position, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until your rear leg’s kneecap is just barely above the ground.
    1. Continue keeping your torso upright and the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the step.
    2. While the forefoot of your rear leg will stay in contact with the ground, the rest of the foot’s sole will be off the ground as it is directed backward.
    3. Keep your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
6. Hold the split squat position for your target duration as you remain completely stationary with your rear knee hovering above the ground.
    1. Continue keeping your knees aligned with your feet, the sole of your front foot fully on the step, and your torso upright.
    2. Since this is a static exercise, you will track how many seconds you hold the split squat rather than reps.
7. While keeping both of your feet planted, reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knees to lift your body up until you have returned to an upright position in your split stance.
    1. You can finish the ascent with a slight bend in your front knee rather than fully locking it out.
    2. Like during the descent, keep your torso upright and knees aligned with your feet while doing so.
8. Re-rack the bar by bending at your hips and knees as you rotate the safety hooks onto the safety bars and return the bar to resting on the safety bars.
9. After you have sufficiently rested, repeat the exercise while switching which leg is in the forward position on top of the step.
    1. Since your rear leg can be meaningfully engaged during a split squat hold, it is recommended to rest before performing your next set with your sides switching positions.
    2. If you wish, you can alternatively perform your sets continuously without reracking the bar and resting, but fatigue may interfere with your performance of the second set if you switch lead legs without any rest.
## Description
This exercise will primarily train your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.
