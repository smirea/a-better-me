## Setting up

1. Place a stability ball (also known as a Swiss ball or physio ball) on the ground.
   1. Ideally, select a ball with a diameter exceeding your shoulder width.
2. Kneel with the ball in front of you as your elbows are bent to approximately 90 degrees.
3. Hinge at your hips to lean your torso forward, and position your bent elbows on top of the ball about shoulder width apart.
   1. Lean forward enough that you can stack your shoulders directly above your elbows.
   2. With a sufficiently large ball, your forearms can rest on the ball with your elbows, but you will chiefly be bearing weight through your elbows.
   3. The appropriate elbow placement should ensure that the ball does not roll when you press down through your elbows.
   4. Once on the ball, you can optionally clench each hand into a fist to promote full-body tension.

## Exercise technique

1. Walk your legs back until your knees are fully straightened with the bottom of your toes dug into the ground.
   1. Position your feet approximately hip-width apart with your soles facing backward.
   2. You should be bearing your bodyweight into the ground through your feet and into the ball through your elbows.
2. Tighten your abs and squeeze your glutes to maintain a rigid back position while pushing your elbows into the ball and keeping your knees straight.
   1. Doing so keeps your hips held up (but not excessively hiked up) rather than letting them sag downward, which would result in an exaggerated arch of your lower back.
   2. In this position, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your shoulders when viewed from the side.
   3. Do not allow your chest to sag, as this can cause your shoulder blades to pinch together. Thinking about pushing your elbows down into the ball can help keep your shoulder blades apart.
   4. Keep your neck near a neutral position, like you would normally do when standing upright.
3. Smoothly move both elbows in a small circular motion on the ball in a controlled manner. Otherwise, maintain the same plank position you adopted from step 2.
   1. Only your arms should be moving through shoulder motion while you keep the rest of your body rigidly stationary.
   2. If someone were to watch your body from your middle back through your feet, you should ideally look like you are performing a static stability ball plank.
   3. The difficulty of this exercise will increase as the circumference of the circular motion increases, so you can determine the range of this movement based on your current ability. Only move through a range that allows you to execute all the technical aspects from step 2. If your hips or chest start sagging down, you may be moving through a range that presently exceeds what you can currently stabilize effectively. Moving in a very small circle is recommended if you are new to this exercise.
   4. You decide whether you start with a clockwise or counterclockwise motion, since you will be training both directions.
4. Cyclically repeat step 3 for your desired number of reps, then start stirring your elbows in the opposite direction.
   1. Specifically, how many revolutions you complete in a particular direction before switching is up to your preference. For instance, if you are striving to perform 20 total reps, you may perform five clockwise reps before performing five counterclockwise reps and then repeat this for another round. Alternatively, you may perform 10 clockwise reps followed by 10 counterclockwise reps. If you wish to do so, you can even switch directions every rep.
5. Alternate between bouts of stirring clockwise and counterclockwise until you have completed your set and are ready to bend your knees to return to kneeling on the ground.
   1. Having a small difference in the number of reps you perform clockwise and counterclockwise is inconsequential. However, you can aim to finish your set with close to equal reps performed in each direction.
   2. Strive to have your torso and lower body look the same during the last second of your set as they did during the first second after getting into position

## Description

This exercise can statically train your core muscles, including your rectus abdominis (AKA the “six-pack muscle”) and obliques as they function to maintain a rigid trunk position.
