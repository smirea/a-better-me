## Setting up

1. Position a pair of deadlift blocks (or a similarly sturdy substitute capable of safely withstanding the load you will use) on the ground far enough apart from each other that the weight plates on a bar will be able to rest fully on the blocks in a stable position.
   1. For this block pull variation, your blocks should be of a low height that situates the bar near the middle of your shins when the loaded bar is resting on the blocks, which often involves no greater than a few inches of elevation for most people.
   2. To appropriately position the blocks, it can be helpful to have a barbell on the ground and align the blocks about the same distance apart from each other as is the length of the barbell’s shaft (which is the middle part of the bar in between the two thick circular collars).
   3. Aerobic steps can often be used for block pulls while having the option to adjust the height by changing the number of risers you use, but you should be aware of the recommended weight limit if you opt to use heavy loads. The weight limit for aerobic steps will vary by brand, but some of the common brands recommend not exceeding 300lb (136kg) per step, which would result in a 600lb (272kg) limit when using a pair of steps for a block pull. The height of the aerobic step will also vary by brand, but the step itself is commonly near 4in (10cm) in height, with the risers being near 2in (5cm) in height.
   4. With thickness typically ranging from about 1-3.5in (2.5-8.9cm), depending on the weight and brand, bumper plates can also function as blocks that can be stacked on top of each other to provide a range of different heights.
2. After placing a barbell onto the blocks and loading weight plates to the bar, stand upright in approximately a hip width stance close in front of the middle of the bar as the bar is over the middle of your feet with your toes pointing straight ahead or slightly turned out to the sides.
   1. While using a near hip-width stance places most people into a viable position, the precise stance width that is ideal for you as an individual may be influenced by your body structure and mobility, so you can feel free to modify your stance a bit based on where you feel strong and comfortable.
   2. You can similarly decide whether to point your toes straight ahead or slightly out to the side based on what feels best to you.
3. With your arms directed down toward the ground, straight elbows, and your shoulders packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up), hinge at your hips while bending your knees until your hands are level with the bar.
   1. More motion should come from your hips than your knees as you descend, and your knees should not track particularly far forward, but you can allow your knees to shift slightly forward to bring your shins right next to the bar.
   2. As a cue for hinging, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks as you sit back while your hands are carrying something, and after you have hinged through your available hip range motion, you can bend your knees further to reach the bar.
   3. Throughout this exercise, keep your lower back looking flat near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright, rather than excessively rounding your lower back while doing so.
   4. Similarly, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
   5. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and engage your back muscles.
4. Firmly grasp the bar with a shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
   1. Your arms should be directed straight down such that they form approximately 90-degree angles with the bar.
   2. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   3. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward as you tense these muscles like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
   2. Strive to tighten the entirety of your core to form a “360 degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
   3. You will continue bracing your core throughout your set, which will help you maintain a rigid trunk position while you move through your hips and knees.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
   1. Doing so can help you wedge your hips into an advantageous starting position through a maneuver known as pulling the slack out of the bar.
   2. When creating “pre-tension,” pressing through the ground should pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
3. While keeping your chest up and back flat with the bar close to your shins, drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar as you “push the floor away” while extending (straightening) your hips and knees.
   1. As you move about your knees and hips, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the pull rather than inclining further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
4. Immediately after the bar lifts off the blocks, focus on thrusting your hips forward as you continue extending your hips and knees until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
   1. The “push the floor away” cue only applies to the very start of the rep since thrusting your hips forward is chiefly what will allow you to lift the weight during this variation.
   2. You should lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
   3. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your back.
5. While keeping your back flat, reverse the movement by hinging at your hips while bending your knees to lower the bar until the plates have returned to resting on the blocks.
   1. You can initially focus on sitting your buttocks back while keeping your shins vertical and allowing your knees to bend naturally until the bar has cleared your knees, after which you can allow your knees to track slightly forward as you further bend your knees and hips to finish lowering the bar.
6. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the blocks for a moment (rather than simply tapping the blocks and immediately deadlifting the bar up again) before continuing to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
   1. While you do not need to pause with the bar on the blocks for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferring between reps.
   2. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps with the bar on the blocks.
   3. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the next rep.

## Description

While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise dynamically trains your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
