## Setting up
1. Position the weight you intend to use at the base of the Roman chair in between its handles such that you can reach the weight when your feet are on its foot plate.
    1. For this variation, you will be holding the weight to your chest, so a weight plate, dumbbell, kettlebell, or medicine ball is a viable option that you can select based on equipment availability and preference.
2. Adjust the height of the Roman chair’s pad to a level that positions the top of your hip bones somewhat above the top of the pads when you set up with your feet on the foot plate.
    1. This position will allow you to move freely about your hips in contrast to if the pad is contacting the top of your hip bones, which would restrict hip motion and prompt you to perform the exercise via spinal motion.
    2. If the top of the pad is positioned much lower than the top of the hip bones (rather than only somewhat lower), some individuals may feel knee discomfort, so a sweet spot can exist for pad placement.
    3. Some trial and error may be needed to find your ideal pad position after initially completing step 2.
3. While placing your hands onto the handles for balance support, step onto the foot plate one leg at a time with the back of your heels in contact with the back of the foot plate, so that the entirety of your soles are in contact with the foot plate.
    1. Position your feet symmetrically in a stance that feels comfortable to you, which can involve pointing your toes somewhat outward or pointing them fairly straight forward.
    2. Situating your feet approximately hip width to shoulder width apart from each other will result in a viable stance width, but how wide of a stance you can use will be influenced by the width of your Roman chair’s foot plate.
    3. Your knees should be straight after you have finished setting your feet on the foot plate.
4. Check to make sure that the pad is positioned as described by step 1, and readjust the pad height if it is not prior to resuming your setup.
## Exercise technique
1. Begin bracing your core by taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward.
    1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. After the deep “belly breath,” you can continue thinking about tensing the front of your abdominals, sides of your abdominals, and lower back muscles simultaneously in a manner that allows you to form a “360 degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
    3. As a helpful cue for your “360 degree brace,” you can tense the front and sides of your abdominals outward like you would if you are bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    4. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the upright position.
    5. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.
2. Hinge at your hips until you are low enough to pick up your weight and hold it to your chest.
    1. Throughout your set, keep your lower back and neck near a neutral position, like you have when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.
    2. Nearly all of the motion should occur from you moving at your hips as you keep your trunk in a static position that maintains the natural curvature of your lower back (as opposed to bending over by rounding your lower back).
    3. Maintain a straight knee position throughout your set, but you should avoid intentionally locking your knees out by squeezing your quads.
    4. Depending on your limb lengths and the height of your Roman chair, you may have to round your upper back somewhat to grasp the weight initially.
    5. The precise way in which you will hold the weight to your chest may vary based on the specific implement you selected, but what matters is that you have a firm grip on the weight and are able to keep it in a stable position.
3. While keeping the weight held to your chest, lift your torso up through your hips until you can draw approximately a straight line from your legs through your hips and to your torso.
    1. You can finish the lifting phase by fully squeezing your glutes, which helps cue hip movement at the top as opposed to spinal movement.
    2. Avoid arching your lower back excessively at the top since the lifting phase should simply be a reversal of the hip motion you just performed during the lowering phase.
4. Lower your torso by hinging at your hips through your available range of motion in a controlled manner as you continue keeping your trunk in a statically braced position.
    1. While hinging at your hips and keeping the weight by your chest, you should feel a stretching sensation in your hamstrings that progressively intensifies.
    2. Most people will be able to descend until their shoulders are lower than their hips, but how deeply you are able to hinge without meaningfully rounding your lower back will be dependent on your hamstring flexibility.
    3. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch, you have finished descending through your available hip range of motion.
5. Alternate between steps 3 and 4 until you have completed your set and are ready to set the weight back down on the ground.
## Description
While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise dynamically trains your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
