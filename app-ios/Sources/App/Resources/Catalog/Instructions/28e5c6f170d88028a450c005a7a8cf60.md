## Setting up
When front squatting, you can hold the bar in different ways depending on your mobility. These options include the full hand clean grip, fingertip clean grip, and cross-arm grip, in addition to using straps. The full hand clean grip requires the most mobility to utilize effectively, followed by the fingertip clean grip and then the cross arm grip and strap technique, both of which have minimal mobility requirements. Each of these positions is described as its own front squat page in the database to communicate specifically how you would set up with each grip. Because the grip does not affect how your lower body or back muscles are trained in any meaningful way, you should utilize whichever grip feels most comfortable and stable to you. This page describes the front squat instructions that are shared in common among each grip, but the setup does not describe each position in detail, so you can click the link to your desired grip to see the additional details for how to use that specific grip. Regardless of your grip, the exercise technique will be the same, along with how you begin and finish setting up. 

1. Adjust the rack’s J-hooks to near mid-chest height, and place a barbell on top of them.
    1. The optimal starting height should place the bar slightly above the tops of the J-hooks, with your feet flat on the floor, after you stand upright with the bar on your back. 
    2. If you later need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground while walking out in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks, you initially positioned the J-hooks too high. Walking with your heels raised increases the risk of losing your balance and should be avoided when you have a barbell on your back.
    3. If you need to bend your knees and hips beyond a mild degree (e.g., more like a half squat than a quarter squat) initially to place the bar on your back, you positioned the J-hooks excessively low. This can make it more challenging to rerack the bar at the end of your set.
2. Face the center of the bar in approximately a shoulder-width stance.
3. Based on your preference, set up with a full hand clean grip, fingertip clean grip, cross-arm grip, or straps.
4. While simultaneously bending your elbows, shallowly bend at your knees and hips with an upright torso to walk under the bar and situate it on the front of your shoulders just in front of the base of your neck.
    1. The bar should be resting on your front delts (which are the muscles on the front of your shoulders that attach to the outer aspects of your collarbones) and the outer portions of your collarbones, which together form a shelf with the base of your neck to cradle the bar.
    2. While the bar should be directly in front of your neck, it should not be pressing into your neck to the extent that it feels like it is choking you.
    3. If you are not accustomed to front squats, it is normal to experience discomfort from the pressure the bar places on the front of your shoulders (which may cause some skin bruising), but it should not be painful with proper bar placement.
5. With the bar now on the front of your shoulders, lift your elbows up until your upper arms are parallel to the ground.
    1. You can perform steps 4 and 5 simultaneously with each other.
    2. The bony prominence on the back of each elbow should now be pointing straight forward.
    3. Your hands can assist in keeping the bar balanced, but the bar should first and foremost be resting on the front of your shoulders without necessarily needing your hands to hold it there.
6. Use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of front squat reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins prior to reracking the bar onto the J-hooks.
7. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends when front squatting.
8. Repeat steps 2-5 of the setup before proceeding to the “exercise technique” section.
## Exercise technique
1. While keeping your upper arms parallel to the ground, stand upright by straightening your knees and hips.
    1. Keep the bar resting on your shoulders throughout the set.
2. Take a couple of steps back, and position your feet near shoulder-width apart, with your toes pointing slightly out to the sides.
    1. While people often find that setting up reasonably close to this stance allows them to squat effectively, the ideal stance width and degree of toe flare will vary among individuals based on their hip structure and mobility.
    2. Ultimately, you want to position your feet where you feel comfortable and stable when squatting, which typically requires trying different stances over time and assessing how you feel in them.
    3. Some people may feel most comfortable squatting with their feet close to hip-width apart as their toes are pointing straight ahead, while others will feel best with their feet outside shoulder width and their toes rotated out to the sides at 45-degree angles.
    4. Throughout your set, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
3. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed, or simply sucking your belly button in.
    2. Continue bracing your core from this point on to maintain a rigid back position.
4. Initiate the descent by simultaneously breaking at your knees and hips. Progressively bend at your knees and hips to squat down in a controlled manner while keeping your chest and elbows up.
    1. Throughout the set, avoid dropping your elbows or letting your chest cave down.
    2. As you descend, it is natural for your torso to lean forward mildly at your hips to maintain your balance, but maintain a mostly upright torso position with your lower back flat.
    3. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the ground as your weight is evenly distributed throughout your feet (rather than being all on your heels or toes) during your set.
    4. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet.
    5. As you bend your knees, it is normal for them to track forward over your feet (especially the deeper your squat) to help you maintain your balance.
5. Continue to squat down through the full range of motion your mobility allows without rounding your lower back, lifting your heels off the ground, or letting your knees cave inward.
    1. People can often descend at least until their thighs are fairly close to being parallel to the ground, but what constitutes a full range of motion for a squat will vary among different individuals.
    2. If your lower back starts rounding, your heels lift off the ground, or your knees cave inward at the bottom of your squat, you may be squatting too deep relative to your current level of mobility and body control.
6. Reverse the movement by driving through the ground while extending (straightening) your knees and hips to stand upright.
    1. Continue keeping your chest up, back flat, feet fully on the ground, and knees aligned with your feet as you squat up.
7. Alternate between steps 4-6 until you have completed your set.
    1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
    2. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before initiating the descent for the next rep.
8. Walk forward until the barbell contacts the back of each J-hook before mildly bending your knees and hips to rerack the bar onto the J-hooks.
## Description
While statically training your spinal extensors, this exercise can effectively develop your quads, gluteus maximus, and adductor magnus.
