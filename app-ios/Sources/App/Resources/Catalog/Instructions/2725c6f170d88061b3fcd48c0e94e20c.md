## Setting up

1. Adjust the height of the Roman chair’s pad to a level that positions its top slightly below the top of your hip bones when you set up with your feet on the foot plate.
   1. The appropriate position should allow you to move your lower back freely while supporting your hips.
   2. While it can effectively stabilize your hips, positioning the top of the pad higher than the top of your hip bones can restrict your lower back’s motion, which is suboptimal for training lower back muscles.
   3. If the top of the pad is positioned much lower than the top of the hip bones (rather than only slightly lower), the hips will not be stabilized as well, which can make it more challenging to emphasize training your back muscles.
   4. Since a sweet spot exists, some trial and error may be needed to find your ideal pad position.
2. While placing your hands onto the handles for balance support, step onto the foot plate one leg at a time. Place the back of your heels against the back of the foot plate, as your soles fully contact the foot plate.
   1. Based on your preference, you can point your toes straight forward, or you can rotate them to point inward. Pointing the toes inward may help some people limit the hip muscle engagement while focusing on the back muscles.
   2. Situating your feet approximately hip width to shoulder width apart from each other will result in a viable stance width, but how wide of a stance you can use will be influenced by the width of your Roman chair’s foot plate.
   3. Your knees should be straight after you have finished setting your feet on the foot plate.
3. Check to make sure that the pad is positioned as described by step 1, and readjust the pad height if warranted before resuming your setup.
4. Place the fingertips of each hand lightly in contact with the sides of the back of your head as your upper arms and elbows are directed out away from your sides.
   1. Maintain this arm position throughout your set.
   2. Your back and neck should be near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.

## Exercise technique

1. While keeping your back mostly flat, hinge at your hips through the range of motion that is permitted by the pad and your flexibility.
   1. Only during this step are you intentionally moving about your hips to assume the starting position.
   2. Once you have reached the end range of your hip motion, it will be easier to emphasize back motion.
   3. Depending on specifically where you have the pad positioned, the pad may limit how far you can hinge, or your hamstring flexibility may limit it.
2. Round your entire back in a slow and controlled curling manner through the full range of motion that is comfortable for you.
   1. The degree to which you can round your back will be affected by your spinal mobility.
   2. It is normal to feel a stretching sensation in your back muscles in the bottom position, but you should not feel any discomfort at the end range. Only round your back as far as is comfortable.
   3. Rather than moving only one part of your back, strive to round your lower, middle, and upper back simultaneously.
   4. As a cue, you can think about bringing the bottom of your ribs toward the top of your hip bones.
3. Reverse the movement by extending (straightening) your back in a controlled manner without hinging at your hips.
   1. At the end of the lifting phase, your back should be near a neutral position, which involves your lower back being mildly arched.
   2. For increasing the size and strength of your back muscles, there is no need to arch your back maximally like you would during a cobra yoga pose.
   3. Keep your glutes relaxed to the best of your ability as you focus on moving your spine rather than your hips.
   4. You should feel your spinal extensor muscles, which run along each side of the spine, contract to extend your back.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set.

## Description

This exercise can dynamically train the erector spinae and deep intrinsic spinal extensor muscles that run along the length of your entire back.
