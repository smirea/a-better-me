## Setting up

1. Anchor a long looped elastic band around a pull-up bar (or a similar elevated object with a smooth surface, such as the beam of a power rack).
   1. To anchor the band, you can initially drape the band over the bar, loop one end of the band into the middle of the other end, and pull that end until it is taught.
   2. Inspect the band to confirm it does not have any small tears, since a small tear can swiftly become a full tear when stretching a band.
2. Grasp the band with both hands and walk backward until you begin feeling tension from the band with your elbows straightened and arms aligned with the band.
3. Let go of the band, and place a cushioned object that is comfortable for kneeling, like a Pilates mat or foam pillow, onto the ground at the location where you started feeling tension from the band.
4. Grasp the band with both hands, and kneel on the cushioned object with your knees approximately hip width apart from each other and your shoelaces (or the top surface of your feet) in contact with the ground as your heels are facing backward.
   1. Your ankles will be in the same position that they would be at the top of a calf raise.
   2. If you have limited ankle mobility and/or find this ankle position uncomfortable, you can place a half foam roller underneath the front of your ankles, which will allow you to adopt a closer to neutral ankle position.
5. Assume an upright torso position while kneeling and align your arms with the band so that you can draw a straight line from your arms to the band with straight elbows.
   1. You will keep your arms aligned with the band throughout the exercise.
   2. If there is any slack in the band in this position, you need to situate yourself further away from the band’s anchor point until you feel tension in the starting position.

## Exercise technique

1. Squeeze your glutes and brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Keep your glutes squeezed and your core braced throughout the set to help you maintain a rigid trunk position.
2. While holding the band with outstretched arms, lean your torso backward in a controlled manner by bending at your knees through the range of motion that your strength and mobility permit.
   1. The motion should exclusively come from your knees, while your hips and lower back remain near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright, which is facilitated by step 1.
   2. Throughout the exercise, you should be able to draw a straight line from your thigh through your hip to your torso, indicating that you are maintaining an appropriately rigid trunk position without moving your hips.
   3. In particular, avoid letting your lower back excessively arch, which can occur if you do not keep your glutes squeezed and core braced.
   4. Depending on how much assistance you receive from the band, the intensity of this exercise may increase as you lean back further, so you can adjust how far you descend based on your ability.
   5. The greatest possible range of motion involves leaning back until your upper back lightly contacts the ground with your knees fully bent.
   6. As you progressively lean back, it is normal to feel a stretching sensation in one of the quad muscles on the front of your upper thigh, and the flexibility of this muscle can limit how far backward certain individuals can move.
3. Reverse the movement by lifting your torso up through motion at your knees until you have returned to the starting position, where your torso is fully upright.
   1. Continue holding the band and keeping your thighs and torso aligned with each other as you resist any back or hip motion, as you did not during the descent.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop all four of your quad muscles, including your rectus femoris, which may not be trained as effectively by most squat, leg press, and lunge variations.
