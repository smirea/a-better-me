## Setting up
1. Adjust the Smith machine’s bar to a few finger widths below the height of your collarbone.
    1. The ideal starting height should let you position the bar on your upper back and unrack it in a stable position with the soles of your feet fully on the ground.
    2. If you later need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground in order to unrack the bar, you initially set it excessively high.
2. Position the safety stops (that are attached to the sliding rails) so that they are slightly below the height of the bar when your rear knee taps the ground at the end of the lowering phase of your split squat.
    1. To find this point for your body, you can perform a rep or two of the split squat with the unloaded bar as described in the “exercise technique” section, but you can initially put the safety stops a little below the top of your hip bones when standing upright as an approximation.
3. Add your desired weight to the bar and face the center of the bar with approximately a hip-width stance.
    1. If your Smith machine has angled sliding rails, position yourself to face the side in which the bar will track slightly forward as it descends (which results in the bottom of the rails being farther in front of you than the top of the rails).
    2. If your Smith machine has purely vertical sliding rails, you can perform the same movement regardless of which direction you face.
4. Grasp each side of the bar with a somewhat outside than shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
    1. The proper grip width will vary from person to person, and what feels best to you will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
    2. If you are not accustomed to exercises that have a bar on your back, you may have to try out different hand placements to find one that personally suits you well.
5. While holding onto the bar, shallowly bend at your hips and knees to then walk under the bar and situate it on your upper back in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
    1. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back.
    2. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
    3. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have the bar placed too low on your back.
    4. While positioning the bar on your upper back, you may have to readjust where you are grasping the bar if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
    5. If you are not accustomed to having a bar on your back, you may not feel particularly comfortable doing so, especially when it is loaded with additional weight, but whichever position you use should not be painful.
## Exercise technique
1. Pull down on the bar to tighten your upper back muscles, and unrack it by starting to stand up as you straighten your knees.
2. After the bar rests fully on your upper back rather than the safety bars, rotate the safety hooks out away from the safety bars with a slight wrist flick before standing fully upright with the bar.
3. While keeping the entire sole of one foot firmly planted on the ground, step backward with the other leg before contacting the ground with your forefoot (ball of your foot and bottom of your toes).
    1. Step far enough backward that you will be able to descend until your rear knee is just above the ground with your front thigh close to parallel to the ground. This may initially require trying out different step lengths if you are new to backward lunges.
    2. Strive to step backward in a straight line rather than stepping closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
    3. Keep your torso in a mostly upright position and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
    4. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
4. After contacting the ground with your rear foot, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until your rear leg’s kneecap is just barely above the ground.
    1. Continue keeping your torso mostly upright and the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the ground.
    2. Keep your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
    3. While the forefoot of your rear leg will be in contact with the ground, the rest of the foot’s sole will be off the ground as it is directed backward.
    4. Perform steps 3 and 4 as a smooth and continuous movement.
5. Hold the lunge position for your target duration as you remain completely stationary with your rear knee hovering above the ground.
    1. Continue keeping your knees aligned with your feet, the sole of your front foot fully on the ground, and your torso upright.
    2. Since this is a static exercise, you will track how many seconds you hold the lunge rather than reps.
6. Step forward with your rear leg while extending (straightening) your knees and hips to lift your body up into a standing position with your feet symmetrically hip-width apart.
7. Rerack the bar by bending at your hips and knees as you rotate the safety hooks onto the safety bars and return the bar to resting on the safety bars.
8. After you have sufficiently rested, repeat the exercise while switching which leg you place in the lead position.
    1. Since your rear leg can be meaningfully engaged during a lunge hold, it is recommended to rest before performing your next set with your other leg forward
    2. If you wish to do so, you can alternatively perform your sets continuously without reracking the bar and resting, but fatigue may interfere with the performance of the next set if you do not rest at all between them.
## Description
This exercise primarily trains your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.
