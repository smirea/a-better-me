## Setting up

1. Follow the set-up instructions for the dual kettlebell clean, and perform the lifting phase of the dual kettlebell clean described in steps 1-5 of its exercise technique instructions.
   1. After doing so, you should now be holding the kettlebell in the front rack position. This involves situating them in front of your shoulders with your elbows bent slightly in front of your ribcage, palms facing in toward each other, and the weights resting on your forearms, which are mostly vertically with your wrists slightly closer to the midline of your body than your elbows. The handle should also be diagonally contacting your palms, such that the sides of the handles by your thumbs are angled mildly higher than the sides of the handles by your little fingers.
   2. As an alternative to cleaning the kettlebells, you can curl them up into the same front rack position described above if you are using loads that are light enough to do so. With this technique, initially grasp the handles with your little fingers directly next to one of the horns (i.e., side handles) on each kettlebell to help the handles rest diagonally across your palms in the front rack position. Feel free to perform a cheat curl-like motion where you initially generate some momentum by swinging the weights mildly backward with straight elbows before swinging your elbows forward while curling them. This is a less technically complicated means of moving the kettlebells into the front rack, but it is not as efficient when using heavy weights for lifters who are proficient with cleaning kettlebells.
2. Assume a “proud posture” with your chest up, as you avoid letting the weights pull you into a hunched position with your upper back and shoulders excessively rounded.
   1. Maintain the natural curvature of your lower back and neck, like when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.
3. Position your feet near shoulder-width apart from each other, and point your toes at least slightly out to your sides.
   1. While people may commonly find that setting up reasonably close to this stance allows them to squat effectively, the specific stance width and degree of toe flare that is ideal will vary among different individuals based on their hip structure and mobility.
   2. Ultimately, you want to position your feet in a manner that results in you feeling comfortable and stable when squatting, which typically requires trying out different stances over time and assessing how you feel with them.
   3. Some people may feel most comfortable squatting with their feet close to hip width apart as their toes are pointing straight ahead, while others will feel best with their feet outside of shoulder width with their toes rotated out to the sides at 45-degree angles.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed, or simply sucking your belly button in.
   2. Continue bracing your core from this point on to maintain a rigid back position.
2. Initiate the descent by simultaneously breaking at your knees and hips. Progressively bend at your knees and hips to squat down in a controlled manner while keeping your chest and elbows up.
   1. Throughout the set, avoid dropping your elbows or letting your chest cave down.
   2. As you descend, it is natural for your torso to lean forward mildly at your hips to maintain your balance, but maintain a mostly upright torso position with your back flat.
   3. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the ground as your weight is evenly distributed throughout your feet (rather than being all on your heels or toes) during your set.
   4. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet.
   5. As you bend your knees, it is normal for them to track forward over your feet (especially the deeper your squat) to help you maintain your balance.
3. Continue to squat down through the full range of motion your mobility allows without rounding your back, lifting your heels off the ground, or letting your knees cave inward.
   1. People can often descend at least until their thighs are fairly close to being parallel to the ground, but what constitutes a full range of motion for a squat will vary among different individuals.
   2. If your back starts rounding, your heels lift off the ground, or your knees cave inward at the bottom of your squat, you may be squatting too deep relative to your current level of mobility and body control.
4. Reverse the movement by driving through the ground while extending (straightening) your knees and hips to stand upright.
   1. Continue keeping your chest up, back flat, feet fully on the ground, and knees aligned with your feet as you squat up.
5. Alternate between steps 2-4 until you have completed your set.
   1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
   2. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before initiating the descent for the next rep.
6. Following your last rep, set the kettlebells down in the opposite manner to how you initially lifted them into the front rack position.
   1. If you initially cleaned the kettlebells, you can let the kettlebells fall forward as you swing your arms down while straightening your elbows and hinging at your hips to absorb the force of the lowering phase.
   2. If you initially curled the kettlebells up, you can similarly guide them down by letting your elbows straighten and swinging your elbows mildly back to help control the weights.

## Description

While statically training your spinal extensors, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.
