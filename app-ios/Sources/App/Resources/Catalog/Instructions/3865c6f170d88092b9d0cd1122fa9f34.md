## Setting up

1. Adjust the height of the Roman chair’s thigh pad to a level that positions the top of your hip bones somewhat above the top of the pads when you set up with your foot on the foot plate.
   1. This position will allow you to move freely about your hips in contrast to if the pad is contacting the top of your hip bones, which would restrict hip motion and prompt you to perform the exercise via spinal motion.
   2. If the top of the pad is positioned much lower than the top of the hip bones (rather than only somewhat lower), some individuals may feel knee discomfort, so a sweet spot can exist for pad placement.
   3. Some trial and error may be needed to find your ideal pad position after initially completing step 2.
2. While placing your hands onto the Roman chair for balance support, step onto the foot plate with the back of your heel (of the leg you will train) in contact with the back of the foot plate. While resting your thighs against the thigh pad, position your non-working side’s foot just behind the foot plate/lower leg roller.
   1. After you have finished setting up on the foot plate, your working side’s knee should be straight, and your sole should fully contact the foot plate. If your Roman chair has a lower leg pad, the back of your lower calf should be snugly contacting it.
   2. You can maintain a slight bend in your non-working side’s knee to keep that side foot and shin just above the foot plate/lower leg roller.
   3. Aim to align your working side’s foot with your hip when viewed from the back, rather than situating your foot further out to the side of the foot plate. If you positioned your other leg in the same manner, this would result in a hip-width stance.
3. Check to make sure that the pad is positioned as described by step 1, and readjust the pad height if it is not prior to resuming your setup.
4. Let go of the Roman chair, and place your arms in your preferred position, such as crossing your arms across your chest with bent elbows to situate each hand on the front of the opposite shoulder.
   1. How you position your arms will affect the difficulty of this exercise, so you can select an arm position that promotes the degree of challenge you wish to have.
   2. Compared to placing your hands on your shoulders, the intensity will be greater if you hold your hands behind your head with bent elbows, and the difficulty will further increase if you raise your arms fully overhead with straight elbows.
   3. Maintain your preferred arm position throughout your set.
   4. All the subsequent instructions will apply to your working leg’s side.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forceful slap.
   1. Continue bracing your core throughout your set.
2. Squeeze your glute to keep your torso up such that you can draw approximately a straight line from your leg through your hip and to your torso as opposed to having your torso bent over.
   1. Throughout your set, keep your lower back and neck near a neutral position, like you have when you are normally standing upright on one foot and looking straight ahead.
   2. This is the starting position of the exercise, where your body should look like it does when normally standing upright except at an incline.
3. Hinge at your hip to lower your torso in a controlled manner until you reach the end range to which your hamstrings (on the back of your thigh) can comfortably stretch.
   1. Rather than just bending over by rounding your lower back, aim to have nearly all of the motion occur from you moving at your hip as you keep your back in a rigid position.
   2. Maintain a straight knee position without intentionally locking your knee out by squeezing your quads.
   3. Keep your shoulders level rather than letting one side drop lower than the other through trunk rotation.
   4. People commonly have the flexibility to descend until their hip is bent by reasonably near 90 degrees (when measuring the angle between the leg and torso from a side view), but how deeply you are able to hinge without meaningfully rounding your lower back will be dependent on your hamstring flexibility.
   5. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch, you have finished descending through your full range of motion. If you have abnormally high hamstring flexibility and do not feel a meaningful stretch at any point in the motion, you can stop descending once you feel that gravity is no longer pulling you down into a deeper hip hinge.
4. Reverse the movement by lifting your torso up through your hip until you have returned to the starting position, where your torso is aligned with your leg.
   1. To help cue hip motion instead of spinal motion, it can help to think about squeezing your glute, particularly at the top of the movement.
   2. Avoid arching your lower back, since the lifting phase should simply be a reversal of the hip motion you just performed during the lowering phase.
   3. At the end of the rep, you should be able to draw a straight line from your shoulder through your hip to your knee when viewed from the side.
5. Alternate between steps 3 and 4 until you have completed your set and are ready to repeat the exercise with your other side.

## Description

While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise dynamically trains your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
