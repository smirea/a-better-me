## Setting up
The key goal of your setup is to adjust the machine and position yourself in a manner that results in your knee joint being aligned with the machine’s pivot point, which is the center about which the machine’s lever arm rotates, that is denoted by a small dot or circle in some machines. After setting up appropriately, you should be in a position that results in you feeling comfortable and stable during the exercise. The features you can adjust will vary based on the specific machine model you use, so certain setup steps described in the instructions may not be applicable to a particular machine model. As you set up, some trial and error may be needed while you adjust the machine and position yourself to find what works best for your individual body proportions.

1. Adjust the seat’s back pad to a position that aligns your knees with the machine’s pivot point while sitting up against the back pad with your thighs resting on the seat.
2. Adjust the machine’s lever arm (that will align with your shins) to its furthest back position that allows you to begin each rep with the greatest knee bend you can achieve.
    1. Certain machines can be used for leg extensions or hamstring curls based on how the lever arm is positioned.
3. Adjust the shin pad to a position that places it slightly above the front of your ankles on the lowest aspect of your shins when seated with your legs behind the shin pad with your knees bent.
    1. Adjusting this setting exclusively moves the shin pad without moving the lever arm in contrast to step 2, where the machine lever arm and shin pad move together.
4. Add weight plates to the machine, and sit down on its seat with your lower shins behind the shin pad, knees aligned with the machine’s pivot point, and back resting against the back pad.
    1. With the appropriate positioning, it should be comfortable for you to have your knees bent.
    2. Your knees are aligned with the machine’s pivot point if you can draw a straight line through the center of both knees to the pivot point.
5. Adjust the thigh pad to press down snugly against your lower thighs.
## Exercise technique
1. For additional stability, firmly grasp the handles and continue holding them throughout your set.
2. Through a controlled kicking motion, progressively extend (straighten) your knees to lift up the shin pad until you have fully straightened your knees.
    1. While you do not lift the weight in an intentionally slow manner, avoid kicking up in an uncontrolled, jerky motion.
    2. As your lower legs move through motion at your knees, you should keep the rest of your body stationary throughout your set as your thighs remain in contact with the seat.
3. Reverse the movement as you allow your knees to bend progressively in a controlled manner to lower the shin pad until you have returned to the starting position with your knees bent as much as the machine permits.
    1. While there is no need to use an extremely slow tempo for this movement, you should be actively controlling the weight the entire time, rather than letting the weight abruptly swing your lower legs down.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set.
## Description
This exercise can effectively develop all four of your quad muscles, including your rectus femoris, which may not be trained as effectively by most squat, leg press, and lunge variations.

### **Training the entirety of your quad**

Exercises that exclusively resist knee extension like machine leg extensions or exercises that chiefly load knee extension while imparting minimal hip extensor loading (like reverse Nordic curls and sissy squats) can serve a distinct role in a training program. A wide array of exercises will train knee extension and hip extension simultaneously such as squats, lunges, step ups, and leg presses. These multi-joint exercises can certainly stimulate meaningful increases in the size and strength of the quads, but they are not ideal for training the rectus femoris, which is the one quad muscle that crosses the hip and functions as a hip flexor along with functioning as a knee extensor. Notably, the rectus femoris does not just weakly assist in flexing the hip, but rather functions as a primary hip flexor that has greater leverage for producing hip flexion torque than any other muscle in the human body at certain joint angles. Because contraction of the rectus femoris can produce a substantial amount of hip flexion torque, its recruitment during a movement that requires both hip and knee extension is less biomechanically efficient than recruitment of the other muscle bellies of the quadriceps. As the rectus femoris contracts more forcefully during a movement like the squat, the hip extensors must work harder to resist the hip flexion torque it produces. Striving to maximize efficiency, the nervous system preferentially recruits the three monoarticular (crossing only the knee joint) muscles of the quad to a greater degree than the rectus femoris during compound exercises where extension simultaneously occurs at the hip and knee joints.

Although some research indicates the rectus femoris does not grow significantly from multi-joint exercises like squats, other studies have reported statistically significant growth in this muscle following such training. When a fairly small magnitude of growth occurs (which is the case even in studies reaching the level of statistical significance), it is unsurprising that the change reaches statistical significance in certain studies but not in others. While the rectus femoris will be trained to a degree during these types of multi-joint exercises, the available body of evidence indicates that the monoarticular quad muscles are much more likely to experience a robust hypertrophy response. Correspondingly, your rectus femoris will almost certainly lag behind your other quad muscles if all of your quad training is performed through multi-joint exercises like squats.

However, during a single joint knee extension exercise where no movement occurs at the hip joint, the rectus femoris is just as active as the monoarticular muscles of the quad, if not more so. This is logical since the hip flexion torque that rectus femoris contraction generates is essentially inconsequential during an exercise that does not simultaneously resist the hip extensor muscles. Congruent with what would be expected, machine seated leg extensions have been demonstrated to produce a considerable degree of hypertrophy of the rectus femoris that is similar to or even more pronounced than that of the three monoarticular quad muscles. While much less studied than machine leg extensions, reverse Nordic curls have been found to yield significant increases in rectus femoris size and the biomechanically similar sissy squat would likely yield a similar result, although it has not been directly studied.

### **Modifying the leg extension for greater muscle growth**

When performed with a sufficiently high relative intensity and appropriate load, machine leg extensions can grow the rectus femoris regardless of how you position the seat, but the seat position can influence the magnitude to which the muscle may grow. Larsen et al. (2025) assessed changes in the thickness of the rectus femoris and vastus lateralis after untrained participants trained single leg machine leg extensions for 10 weeks. For each participant, one leg was trained with an upright torso positioning the hip into 90 degrees of flexion, while the other leg trained with a recline torso positioning the hip into only 40 degrees of flexion. The two interventions resulted in similar vastus lateralis growth, which is unsurprising since that muscle does not cross the hip joint. However, the rectus femoris experienced significantly greater growth from the leg extensions using a reclined torso position compared to the upright torso position. Because the rectus femoris functions as a hip flexor, it shortens in progressively greater angles of hip flexion, while it lengthens as the hip extends. When you are able to lean your torso back when seated, your hip is in a lower angle of flexion compared to when you are seated upright or leaned forward, so your rectus femoris can operate at a longer muscle length when you lean back. The available body of evidence indicates that biarticular muscles (which cross two joints) are likely to experience greater hypertrophy when trained at long lengths compared to shorter lengths, and the rectus femoris fits this trend along with the hamstrings, calves, and triceps.

If you wish to train your rectus femoris at longer lengths during machine leg extensions, you can intentionally position the back pad as far back as you can to allow you to lean further back at your hips when performing the exercise. With this approach, you would still want to align your knee joint with the machine’s pivot point, so you would position your thighs at the same part of the seat as you would if you had the back pad further forward to maintain an upright torso. When using the reclined technique, your upper back would still rest against the back pad, but your lower back would no longer contact the back pad in contrast to if you positioned the seat further forward. While Larsen et al. (2025) specifically utilized a machine that allowed participants to be meaningfully reclined, many leg extensions are not constructed in a manner that would allow users to set up appropriately with only 40 degrees of hip flexion. Consequently, the degree to which you practically can modify your hip position during a machine leg extension will likely be smaller than what was studied by Larsen et al. (2025). This may potentially result in using a reclined torso position on your leg extension machine having a smaller effect on rectus femoris hypertrophy compared to what was reported by Larsen et al. (2025).
