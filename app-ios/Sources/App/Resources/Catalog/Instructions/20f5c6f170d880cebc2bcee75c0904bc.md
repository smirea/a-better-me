## Setting up

1. Position a sturdy object (such as a thick bumper plate, aerobic step, or plyometric box) that you will use as a step onto the floor.
   1. You can use a range of different heights for your step, and the difficulty of the exercise will increase as the height increases.
   2. If you are new to forward step-downs, selecting an object that is near four inches (10cm) high (which is a typical height of many aerobic steps without additional risers) can be an appropriate starting point, and you can subsequently adjust the height based on how challenging this height is for you.
   3. Alternatively, you can perform this exercise on the lowest step of a staircase, which is normally near seven inches (18cm) in height.
   4. When feasible, setting up where you can face a mirror is recommended to be able to receive visual feedback about your technique.
2. Pick up a kettlebell or dumbbell and hold it right next to your chest with bent elbows, as your upper arms are slightly in front of your sides (i.e., in the goblet position).
   1. You can use either a kettlebell or a dumbbell, but some people find holding a kettlebell in the goblet position to feel more stable and comfortable, especially with a fairly heavy weight.
   2. Based on your preference and the load you use, you can transfer your kettlebell or dumbbell into the goblet position through a couple of different approaches.
   3. An effective approach is initially to pick up the weight by the handle through a deadlift motion and set it on an elevated surface (like a bench or plyometric box) with the handle facing up if you use a kettlebell or the handle directed vertically if using a dumbbell (as only one end of the dumbbell is on the surface). Then squat down until the weight is right in front of your chest, firmly grasp it in the goblet position, and squat up with the weight. If you use a kettlebell, grasp it by its horns (i.e., side handles). If you use a dumbbell, cup your hands around the top end of the weight with the base of your palms by the handle, and your fingers wrapped around the rest of that end of the weight.
   4. With a lighter kettlebell, you can tilt it onto its side with the weight base directed toward you, squat down to grasp its horns, stand upright with it, and curl it into the goblet position.
   5. With a lighter dumbbell, you can also pick it up with one hand and curl it up across your body to be in front of your chest. Then cup your free hand around the top end of the dumbbell with the base of its palm by the handle and fingers wrapped around the top end of the weight before repositioning your other hand to cup the other side of the weight in the same manner.
3. While holding the weight to your chest, step up onto the step with both feet.
   1. You will continue holding the weight close to your chest in the goblet position throughout your set.
4. Position your feet approximately hip-width apart from each other, with your toes pointing straight ahead, as the soles of your feet are fully on the step, with your toes right by the edge of the step.

## Exercise technique

1. Shift your weight into one foot and lift your other foot up and forward so that it is hanging over the edge of the step.
2. On the side hovering off the step, pull your toes and the ball of your foot upward through an ankle motion that results in your heel being closer to the ground than the ball of your foot.
   1. This is the opposite of the ankle motion that occurs when lifting your heels up during a calf raise.
   2. You will keep your foot in this position and that side’s knee straight throughout your set.
   3. Now you are in the starting position for your first rep.
3. With your leg that is on the step, bend your knee as you drive your knee forward over your foot while keeping your torso upright to lower yourself in a controlled manner until you can gently tap the ground with the heel of your other foot.
   1. Keep the entire sole (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) of your elevated foot firmly planted on the step and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your free leg drop down.
   2. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
   3. Keep your working leg’s knee aligned with your foot rather than letting your knee cave inward relative to your foot (which you can check in a mirror, along with how level your hip bones are).
   4. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your free leg’s heel, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
4. Reverse the movement by driving through your elevated foot as you straighten your knee to lift yourself up until you have returned to the starting position with your knee fully straightened.
   1. During the ascent, continue keeping your working leg’s knee stacked over your foot, the sole of your foot fully on the step, hip bones level, and torso upright.
   2. While you lift yourself up through your elevated leg, avoid pushing off the ground with your foot.
5. Repeat steps 3 and 4 until you have finished your set and are ready to repeat the exercise by switching which foot is elevated on top of the step.

## Description

While biasing your quads to a greater degree than your gluteus maximus, this exercise can help train your dynamic single leg stability.
