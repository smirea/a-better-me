## Setting up

1. Add weight plates of your desired load to a barbell situated on the ground.
   1. The size of the weight plates you add to the bar will determine how high the bar lies above the ground, which consequently affects your range of motion. Adding a pair of full diameter plates (like 45lb (20kg) plates or any bumper plates) will elevate the bar to the standard height (about 9in (23cm)) when deadlifting from the floor. If you only use smaller diameter plates, the exercise will function as a deficit deadlift with a greater range of motion that may exceed the range of motion in which some people wish to train if they lack the mobility to perform a deficit deadlift with appropriate technique. If you do not have access to bumper plates and are using loads that are not heavy enough to utilize 45lb (20kg) plates, you can perform a block pull with the bar elevated to where it would be when deadlifting from the floor with full diameter plates.
2. Stand with the middle of your feet under the bar, and spread your legs apart into a wide stance that is clearly outside shoulder width with your toes moderately rotated out away from your sides.
   1. Provided that your feet are wider apart than your shoulders but closer together than the plates, you can use a range of stance widths for your sumo deadlift.
   2. The ideal stance width can meaningfully vary among different individuals based on someone’s body structure and hip mobility.
   3. Trying out different foot positions will normally be required to determine which stance specifically feels best to you.
   4. As a starting point, you can widen your feet as far apart as you feel comfortable and stable while being able to push your knees out approximately over your heels, resulting in close to vertical shins. You will push your knees out when descending to reach the bar, so subsequent aspects of the setup can help you gauge if you should adjust your stance.
   5. The ideal amount of toe flare also varies among different lifters, but you can start with your toes pointing about 30-45 degrees out to the side before adjusting from there based on what feels best to you. Zero degrees of toe flare would mean your toes are pointing straight forward, while 90 degrees would mean your toes are pointing directly out to your side.
3. Direct your arms straight down toward the bar with your shoulders packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up).
   1. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and help engage your back muscles.
4. Sit your buttocks back to hinge at your hips while bending your knees and pushing your knees out until your hands are level with the bar. Rather than rounding your back, keep your lower back flat and chest up in a “proud chest” posture.
   1. Strive to keep your shins vertical as your knees essentially do not track forward (although a very slight degree of forward knee tracking can be used if that feels best to you).
   2. The proper balance of bending at your hips versus your knees should allow your shoulders to be stacked vertically above the bar and your hips to be higher than your knees at the bottom.
   3. Try to push your knees far enough out to the sides that they are vertically stacked over your heels.
   4. Throughout this exercise, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
5. With your elbows fully straightened and the bar directly in front of your shins, firmly grasp the bar with an approximately shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
   1. As an alternative to the double overhand grip, you can naturally use a mixed grip (with one palm facing forward and the other facing backward) or lifting straps with the double overhand grip if you wish to do so.
   2. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   3. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
   2. Continue bracing your core from this point onward to help you maintain a rigid back position while using your lower body muscles to lift the weight.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
   1. This can help you wedge your hips into an advantageous starting position through a maneuver known as pulling the slack out of the bar.
   2. When creating “pre-tension,” pressing through the ground should pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
   3. Keep your soles fully in contact with the ground throughout your set.
3. Drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar as you “push the floor away” by extending (straightening) your hips and knees while pushing your knees out. Prioritize keeping your chest up, lower back flat, the bar close to your legs, and your core braced throughout the entire exercise.
   1. As your hips and chest rise at the same rate, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the pull. Avoid letting your torso incline further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
4. After the bar reaches near your knees, focus on thrusting your hips forward until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
   1. Lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
   2. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your shoulders when viewed from the side.
5. Reverse the movement by hinging at your hips and bending your knees to lower the bar until the plates have returned to resting on the ground.
   1. Continue keeping your lower back flat, chest up, and the bar close to your legs while driving your knees out with vertical shins.
   2. You can initially focus on sitting your hips back while allowing your knees to bend naturally. After the bar has cleared your knees, you can further bend your knees and hips to finish lowering the bar.
6. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the ground for a moment rather than simply tapping the ground and immediately deadlifting the bar up again. Then continue to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
   1. While you do not need to pause with the bar on the ground for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferred between reps.
   2. You will be able to brace most effectively when you breathe in between reps while the weight is on the ground rather than during a rep. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before the next rep.

## Description

While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise can meaningfully train your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and quads.
