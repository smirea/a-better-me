## Setting up

1. For the guillotine press, you will set up just like you would with a wide grip bench press.
   1. While not inherently injurious, the guillotine press is not suitable for everyone’s shoulders, and it is strongly encouraged to use a spotter, particularly if you perform it without safety pins/spotter arms that are able to protect your neck in case you fail a rep.
2. Determine whether you will set up within a power rack, which could be modified to perform a variety of barbell lifts and would require you to position a mobile flat bench within the rack, or within a specific bench press station in which the bench is fused to the rack.
   1. Most power racks allow you to position J-hooks (on which the barbell rests before being unracked) and safety pins at incrementally different heights, respectively, to help you situate the bar close to your ideal starting position and to receive the bar safely if you fail a rep.
   2. Bench press stations may also have spotter arms (that function just like safety pins by receiving the bar if you fail a rep) and J-hooks that can be adjusted in height, but a variety of models lack spotter arms and have multiple pairs of fixed height J-hooks. While most people can find a J-hook height that allows them to bench press effectively, some people will notice that their ideal starting position of the bar cannot be achieved with the available J-hook heights, which can make it difficult to unrack the bar independently.
3. With a power rack, position the J-hooks to a height that initially allows you to grasp the bar with slightly bent elbows once you are set up on the bench, which will allow you to unrack the bar efficiently. With a bench press station, you will find which J-hook height places you closest to this position.
   1. After fully straightening your elbows to lift the bar off the J-hooks, the bar should ideally be close to 2in (5cm) above the top of the J-hooks while keeping your shoulder blades tucked back together.
   2. The proper height easily enables you to avoid bumping the J-hooks when unracking the bar while simultaneously not requiring much effort to lift the bar initially.
   3. If the J-hooks are too high, you may have to spread your shoulder blades apart in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks when unracking, which can place you in a less stable position to start your set, since it can be challenging to reposition your shoulder blades after unracking the bar.
   4. If the J-hooks are too low, the unrack will involve a greater-than-ideal pressing range of motion, which will require greater effort and generate more fatigue before your set begins.
   5. If neither of the two closest J-hook heights is ideal, it is generally better to use the height that is a little too low, since being able to start the set with optimal positioning is typically more advantageous than the alternative.
   6. Some trial and error is often needed to find the best J-hook height for you.
4. After situating a bar on top of the appropriate J-hooks, use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of guillotine press reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins/spotter arms prior to re-racking the bar onto the J-hooks.
5. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins/spotter arms to be slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of the lowering phase of your guillotine press.
   1. If you have the shoulder mobility to touch the bar to your collarbone, it can be quite difficult to position the safety pins/spotter arms at a height that allows you to train through your full range of motion while simultaneously protecting your neck, since your neck is right next to your collarbone. If you are in this situation, you can decide whether you will accept the risk of performing this exercise through a full range of motion without safety pins/spotter arms while using a competent spotter. Alternatively, you can position the safety pins/spotter arms to the lowest point that is above your neck and lower the bar until it lightly contacts the safety mechanisms, which will slightly reduce the range of motion.
   2. If you happen to fail a rep without a spotter, you could relax your upper back by allowing your shoulder blades to spread apart, which will lower your chest and ideally allow the bar to rest on the safety pins/spotter arms.
   3. You can avoid adding a pair of collars to the end of the bar to allow you to dump the weight to one side and have the weights slide off the bar if you become pinned underneath the bar after failing a rep. The con of not using collars is that the plates can accidentally slide to one side when pressing if you happen to tilt the bar asymmetrically, but it can be an acceptable tradeoff to make sure that you can avoid being pinned if you fail a rep.
6. Lie down with your back on the bench as your mouth is close to being directly underneath the barbell, and your knees are bent with your feet on the floor approximately shoulder width apart from each other.
   1. Based on what feels most stable, you can position the soles of your feet flat on the ground with your shins close to being vertical, or you can bend your knees beyond this point to dig the balls of your feet and toes into the ground with your heels off the ground.
   2. You can similarly decide whether you point your toes moderately out to the sides or straight ahead based on your preference.
7. Firmly grasp the bar with your palms facing forward (resulting in an overhand grip) as your hands are well outside shoulder width apart from each other, with the bar on top of the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) with reasonably straight wrists.
   1. Like with a wide grip bench press, you can aim to situate your hands about twice the distance of your shoulder width apart from each other.
   2. For stability, your thumbs should be wrapped around the bar from the opposite side of your other fingers, resulting in the bar being fully enclosed by your hands in contrast to if you positioned your thumbs on the same side of your other fingers (which is known as a thumbless grip that is also called a “suicide grip”).
   3. While your wrists do not need to be completely straight, you should avoid grasping the bar with your wrists meaningfully cocked back into an excessively extended position that may contribute to an increased risk of experiencing wrist discomfort.
   4. After grasping the bar in a manner that allows you to feel comfortable and stable, you will maintain this hand and wrist position throughout your set.
8. While allowing your lower back to arch naturally, assume a “proud chest” posture that puffs your chest slightly up toward the ceiling by tightly pulling your shoulder blades back together, which is a position you will maintain throughout your set.
   1. As a cue for pulling your shoulder blades back, you can think about trying to squeeze a pencil between your shoulder blades, which should prompt you to feel your upper back muscles contracting, like at the end range of a rowing exercise.
   2. Focusing on adopting a “proud chest” position can naturally place your lower back into a mildly arched position, which is sufficient for optimizing muscle growth and general strength development.

## Exercise technique

1. Squeeze your glutes and brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forceful slap.
   1. You will continue doing so throughout your set, which will help create full-body tension and allow your body to function as a stable platform for pressing.
   2. From this starting position, your feet should be firmly dug into the ground, while your buttocks, upper back, and head are firmly planted on the bench. You will maintain these five points of contact throughout your whole set to maximize your stability and performance.
2. Press the bar off the J-hooks by straightening your elbows, and move the bar forward away from the J-hooks until it is approximately above your shoulders, where you will hold it for a moment before starting to lower the bar.
   1. Focus on keeping your shoulder blades pulled back together as you avoid losing your upper back tightness.
3. Lower the bar toward your collarbone in a slow and controlled manner by progressively bending your elbows and allowing them naturally to flare out away from each other while descending.
   1. While motion occurs simultaneously at your shoulders and elbows, strive to keep your elbows stacked under your mostly straight wrists, rather than letting them track in front of your wrists (closer to your hips) or behind them (closer to your head). Maintaining this alignment will promote the appropriate degree of elbow flare.
   2. The bar should move straight down without any forward movement toward your lower chest, unlike a typical bench press.
   3. An intentionally slow descent is especially beneficial for the guillotine press. Prioritize fully controlling every moment of the lowering phase.
4. Continue lowering the bar slowly until you can tap your collarbone lightly with it, or until you reach the end of the comfortable range of motion permitted by your shoulder mobility.
   1. Not everyone has the shoulder mobility to touch the bar to the collarbone, and you should not force your shoulders into a position exceeding what your mobility safely permits.
   2. It is normal to feel a stretching sensation in the chest and the front of the shoulders at the bottom of the rep, but you should not experience pain or joint discomfort.
   3. Near the bottom of the rep, your upper arms should be close to forming a “T-shape” (i.e., 90-degree angles) with your torso due to your elbows being flared out further away from your sides compared to a standard bench press.
5. Reverse the movement by pressing the bar up as you progressively drive your elbows upward and in toward each other while straightening them.
   1. Finish with your elbows fully straightened.
   2. Like during the descent, the bar should move in a vertical path as you press it up until it has returned to being situated above your shoulders.
   3. Continue keeping your buttocks firmly in contact with the bench, feet planted on the ground, and shoulder blades pulled back together.
6. Alternate between steps 3-5 until you have completed your set and are ready to rerack the bar.
7. After completing the lifting phase of your last rep, keep the bar motionless over your shoulders for about one second before moving the bar back until it contacts the back of both J-hooks.
8. Bend your elbows mildly to lower the bar onto the J-hooks to finish reracking the weight.
   1. Only begin lowering the bar after you have already contacted the back of the J-hooks.

## Description

This exercise primarily trains your entire pec major, which is assisted by your front delt and triceps.
