## Setting up

1. Approach the pair of bars/handles that you will use for your dip.
   1. Ideally, select a pair of bars that enables you to use a mildly outside shoulder-width grip for this variation.
   2. A variety of pieces of equipment can be used to perform this exercise.
   3. You can use bars that are completely parallel to each other or angled “V-grip” bars that converge to be closer together near the base and wider apart at the free ends. In contrast to stationary parallel bars, angled bars allow you to adjust your grip width based on specifically where you grasp them. Gymnastics rings can also be used to train dips, but the ring dip is presented as a distinct variation in this database.
   4. Gyms commonly have a power tower (which has dip bars protruding from its vertical frame and often has a pull-up bar and/or forearm pads to use for leg raises) or a dip stand/station (which is specifically designed for dips).
   5. You can alternatively use a dip attachment that is connected to a power rack.
   6. Some assisted dip/pull-up machines also permit you to flip the knee pad or foot bar up, therefore enabling you to train dips without machine assistance. Certain machine models allow you to adjust the distance between the bars and position them mildly outside shoulder width apart.
   7. Other options include portable parallettes (which can be positioned closer or wider apart to adjust your grip width) or parallel bars located at a playground.
2. Firmly grasp the bars with your palms facing each other and your elbows straight. Hold the handles directly under the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) with mostly straight wrists.
   1. Before you can grasp the bars with straight elbows, you may have to step up onto a step (which may be built into the equipment you use or require you to place a mobile step near the bars), depending on the design of your equipment.
   2. Squeezing the handles tightly can help you avoid letting your wrists excessively cock back.
   3. If you are using angled bars, you can grasp the bars at the point where they are mildly outside shoulder width apart for you. The grip width you use ideally should let your elbows naturally flare out somewhat away from your sides without prompting the exercise to feel uncomfortable for your shoulders. Feel free to try out different grip widths over time to determine what feels best to you.
   4. When using dip bars attached to a power rack, some people may have to face away from the rack to train through the full range of motion. The leaned forward position from a chest dip can result in the base of the attachment restricting depth for certain lifters if they face toward the base of the dip bars.
3. While keeping your elbows straightened, lift your feet off the ground.
   1. Depending on the equipment you use, you may have to bend your knees in order for your feet to clear the ground, or you may be able to keep your knees straight.
   2. With straight knees, you can keep the inner aspect of your feet against each other. If you bend your knees, you can cross your ankles.
4. Pull your shoulder blades back and down to assume a “proud chest” posture.
   1. You chiefly want to prevent your shoulders from rolling forward or shrugging up during this exercise.
   2. It is not required to keep your shoulder blades packed down further than they normally are at rest, but the intent to pack them down will prevent your torso from sagging down, which would place you into a shrugged shoulder position.
   3. Similarly, you do not necessarily need to have your shoulder blades fully back in the starting position, but pulling them back can make it easier to avoid tilting your shoulders forward, which is undesirable.
5. Lean your torso and head moderately forward while placing your legs in a position that helps you naturally maintain this forward lean.
   1. The appropriate degree of lean should result in your chest clearly being in front of your hips (when viewed from the side), but your torso should still be closer to a vertical position than a horizontal position.
   2. Leaning forward near 30 degrees from vertical is generally appropriate, but specifically how far you lean forward is up to your preference and what feels comfortable to you during the exercise.
   3. Since there is no single correct leg position, you can also position them where you personally prefer. The key is to select a position that allows you to lean your torso forward. Some people find that tucking their legs forward helps them shift their hips back and correspondingly lean their torso forward, while others prefer to keep their thighs aligned with their torso.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing your core throughout your set.
2. While allowing them to flare out somewhat away from your sides, bend your elbows to lower your body in a controlled manner.
   1. Throughout your set, continue keeping your torso moderately leaned forward, and avoid letting your shoulders roll forward or shrug up.
   2. Rather than letting them swing, keep your legs stationary relative to your hips until you finish your set.
   3. In contrast to a triceps-focused dip, you are intentionally flaring your elbows out to a degree for this variation rather than keeping them tucked very close to your sides. However, make sure to avoid excessively flaring them.
   4. The appropriate degree of elbow flare should feel comfortable for your shoulders while also permitting you to feel meaningful tension in your chest during the exercise. Specifically how much elbow flare someone should utilize will vary among different individuals and be influenced by shoulder mobility.
   5. Aiming to flare your elbows less than 45 degrees is generally appropriate for most people. Zero degrees of flare would mean your elbows are pointing straight back, parallel to each other, while 90 degrees of flare would mean they are pointing straight out to the sides.
   6. Provided that you do not intentionally try to tuck your elbows inward, the appropriate degree of elbow flare can often naturally occur without having to think about flaring your elbows out if your bars are mildly outside shoulder width.
3. Continue descending through the full range of motion that your shoulder mobility comfortably permits.
   1. It is common for people with healthy shoulders to reach the point at which their upper arms are at least parallel to the ground, but specifically how deeply you should descend will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
   2. At your end range, it is normal to feel a stretching sensation in the front of your shoulders, but you should not feel any shoulder discomfort or pain.
   3. If you feel joint discomfort or pain at the bottom, you most likely lowered yourself excessively far relative to your current mobility. Training dips over time may improve your mobility, but you should not force yourself into an aggravating range.
   4. While you do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, using a slow and controlled tempo as you approach the bottom position is recommended.
4. Reverse the movement by pushing through the bars to lift yourself up until you have fully straightened your elbows.
   1. You can think about driving your elbows simultaneously in toward your sides and downward.
5. Alternate between steps 2-4 until you have completed your set.
   1. Once you have fully straightened your elbows after your last rep, step down from the bars.

## Description

While also training the front delt and upper pec major, this exercise will particularly develop the middle and lower regions of the pec major, along with the triceps.
