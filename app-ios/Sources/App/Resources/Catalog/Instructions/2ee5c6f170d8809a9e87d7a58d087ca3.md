## Setting up
1. After determining whether you want to use the high or low handles of a trap bar, add weight plates of your desired load to the bar.
    1. The ideal handle height should let you train through the full hip hinge range of motion that your mobility comfortably permits. If you are new to this exercise, you start with the high handles and then switch to the lower handles if the high handles limit your range of motion.
    2. If the plates touch the ground at the bottom of your Romanian deadlift before you have achieved the end range to which your hamstrings can comfortably stretch when using the high handles, the low handles are likely preferable.
    3. Note that training this exercise over time can increase your hamstring flexibility, so it may be appropriate to switch to the low handles in the future, even if the high handles are ideal for you at first.
    4. The setup for this exercise is simply performing the lifting phase of a high handle or low handle trap bar deadlift.
2. In a near hip-width stance, stand within the middle of the trap bar.
3. With your arms pointing straight down toward the handles, sit your hips backward while bending your knees until your hands are level with the handles. Keep your lower back flat and chest up rather than rounding your back.
    1. More motion should come from your hips than your knees as you descend, and your knees should not track far forward. However, you can let your knees shift mildly forward rather than maintaining completely vertical shins.
4. With your elbows fully straightened, firmly grasp the center of the handles with your palms facing in toward each other.
    1. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the handles with the handles pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
    2. If you situate the handles closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the handles can slide down your hands while holding the bar, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
    3. Keep your elbows fully straightened throughout the exercise.
5. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed, or simply sucking your belly button in.
    2. Continue bracing your core from this point on to maintain a rigid back position while using your lower body muscles to lift the weight.
6. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
    1. The initial submaximal pull allows you to check if you have grasped the handles correctly, which will be indicated by the handles not rotating forward or backward when applying the pre-tension.
    2. If you pick up a trap bar with your hands positioned slightly away from the middle of the handles, the trap bar can start rotating in a manner that makes it very difficult to hold and lift appropriately.
    3. Even for experienced lifters, it can be easy to grasp a trap bar’s handles without having their hands truly centered initially, so applying the pre-tension can make sure you are in your ideal starting position.
    4. If the bar starts rotating when you apply the pre-tension, re-center your grip, and apply the pre-tension again until the bar no longer rotates.
7. While keeping your lower back flat and chest up, drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar before thrusting your hips forward and squeezing your glutes to stand upright with the bar.
## Exercise technique
1. While allowing your knees to bend mildly, sit your buttocks backward to hinge at your hips in a controlled manner as you keep your lower back flat, chest up, and core braced.
    1. Strive to lower the bar in a straight line, rather than letting it drift forward, which may be more likely to occur if you think about “bending over” rather than “sitting back.”
    2. While you hinge at your hips, your torso will transition to an angle that is closer to horizontal as you lower the bar.
    3. Most of the motion should come from your hips, and your shins should remain vertical throughout your set, but do not keep your knees fully straight. You do not need to focus on bending your knees, since the appropriately mild degree of knee bend can naturally occur when you sit your buttocks backward without intentionally keeping your knees locked.
    4. As a cue, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks while your hands are carrying something.
    5. Throughout the set, keep your feet fully planted on the ground and your neck near a neutral position, as when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.
2. Continue hinging at your hips through the full range of motion in which you can comfortably move.
    1. Lifters with normal flexibility can typically lower the bar until their hands are at least below their knees, but how deeply you can hinge without rounding your lower back or excessively bending your knees depends on your hamstring flexibility.
    2. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch and can no longer sit back any farther, you have reached your personal end range. Descending past this point would require you to begin rounding your back or bending your knees in a squat-like manner.
    3. If you have high hamstring flexibility and can hinge until the plates contact the ground, descend until you can tap the plates very lightly on the ground, but do not let the weight rest on the ground.
    4. While you do not need to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, you should clearly be in control of the entire descent.
3. Reverse the movement by thrusting your hips forward to lift your torso upright into the starting position.
    1. The lifting phase should simply be a reversal of what you just performed, so keep your lower back flat, chest up, core braced, and feet fully on the ground.
    2. When you have finished lifting the bar, you should be able to draw a straight line from your shoulders through your hips to the middle of your feet when viewed from the side.
    3. Avoid leaning back at the top through excessive lower back arching that would pull your shoulders a little behind your hips. Squeezing your glutes at the end of the lifting phase can help you finish with your hips properly stacked under your shoulders, preventing excessive arching.
4. Alternate between steps 1-3 until you have completed your set and are ready to set the bar down onto the floor.
    1. You will be able to brace most effectively when you breathe between reps in the fully upright position, rather than during a rep. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before initiating the descent for the next rep.
    2. After finishing the last rep, set the bar down in the same manner in which you initially picked it up (i.e., bending your hips and knees while keeping your back flat).
## Description
While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
