## Setting up

1. Adjust the Smith machine’s bar to a height that allows you to position the bar on your upper back and un-rack it in a stable position.
   1. As an initial approximation, you can position the bar such that it is a few finger widths below the height of your collarbone.
2. Position a sturdy object near knee height (like a flat exercise bench, split squat roller stand, aerobic step with risers, or plyometric box) behind the Smith machine’s bar at a distance that is about twice the length of your feet.
   1. If your Smith machine has angled sliding rails, you will face the side in which the bar will track slightly forward as it descends (which results in the bottom of the rails being farther in front of you than the top of the rails) during this exercise, so you will place the object behind where you will face.
   2. If your Smith machine has purely vertical sliding rails, you can perform the same movement regardless of which direction you face, so it does not matter which side you place the elevated object.
   3. To determine an appropriate distance, you can stand with the middle of your feet directly under the bar, step backward with one foot until its big toe is just behind the heel of your other foot, and do the same with your other foot.
   4. Where the heel of your rear foot reaches after taking these two steps backward is where you can place your elevated object just behind.
   5. While this strategy can consistently situate most people in a viable starting position for the exercise, a range of foot placements can be effectively used, including having the object foot two and a half “foot lengths” behind the bar (which may be even more comfortable for some people).
   6. If it is available, a split squat roller stand will normally be the most comfortable object you can use to elevate your foot during this exercise.
   7. If you are using an angled Smith machine, a bench may potentially be too wide to fit behind you within the machine.
3. Position the safety stops (that are attached to the sliding rails) so that they are slightly below the height of the bar when your rear knee taps the ground at the end of the lowering phase of your split squat.
   1. To find this point for your body, you can perform a rep or two of the split squat with the unloaded bar as described in the “exercise technique” section, but you can initially put the safety stops a little below the top of your hip bones when standing upright as an approximation.
4. Add your desired weight to the bar and face the center of the bar with approximately a hip-width stance.
5. Grasp each side of the bar with a somewhat wider than shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
   1. The proper grip width will vary from person to person, and what feels best to you will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
   2. If you are not accustomed to exercises that have a bar on your back, you may have to try out different hand placements to find one that personally suits you well.
6. While holding onto the bar, shallowly bend at your hips and knees to then walk under the bar and situate the bar on your upper back in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
   1. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back.
   2. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back.
   3. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
   4. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have the bar placed too low on your back.
   5. While positioning the bar on your upper back, you may have to readjust where you are grasping the bar if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
   6. If you are not accustomed to having a bar on your back, you may not feel particularly comfortable doing so, especially when it is loaded with additional weight, but whichever position you use should not be painful.
   7. If you need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground in order to position the bar on your upper back, you can remove the weights from the bar, lower the height of the bar, and add the weight plates back before repositioning the bar on your upper back.

## Exercise technique

1. Pull down on the bar to tighten your upper back muscles, and unrack it by starting to stand up as you straighten your knees.
2. After the bar rests fully on your upper back rather than the safety bars, rotate the safety hooks out away from the safety bars with a slight wrist flick before standing fully upright with the bar.
3. Shift your bodyweight into your front foot before placing the top surface (on the side of your shoe laces) of your rear foot on top of the elevated surface with that side’s knee bent.
   1. To help shift your bodyweight into your front foot, you can lean your torso mildly forward by hinging at your hip as you slightly bend that side’s knee.
   2. Once you have situated your rear foot into the elevated position, you can return to keeping your torso in a fairly upright position, which should involve you maintaining the natural curvature of your lower back, like when you are normally standing upright (rather than excessively arching beyond that position).
   3. Strive to move your rear foot backward in a straight line to keep your feet approximately hip width apart rather than bringing your rear foot closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
   4. Keep the entire sole of your front foot firmly planted on the ground and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
   5. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
   6. If you are using a split squat roller to elevate your foot, you can contact the surface with the front of your ankle along with your foot, but only your foot will contact the elevated surface if it is flat, like a bench.
4. From your split stance position, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until you are able to tap the ground gently with the kneecap of your rear leg.
   1. Keep most of your weight shifted onto your front foot while you are primarily using your rear foot to assist with balance.
   2. Continue keeping your torso fairly upright and the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the ground.
   3. Keep your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
   4. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your rear knee, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
   5. At the end of the lowering phase, your front leg’s thigh should be close to parallel to the ground.
   6. If you initially situated your elevated object excessively far behind you, you may reach the end of your available hip range of motion before your rear knee reaches the ground.
   7. If you initially situated your elevated object excessively close to you, you may not be able to tap the ground with your rear knee unless you also lift the heel of your front foot up off the ground.
   8. In either case of suboptimal foot placement, you do not need to force your rear knee to tap the ground, and you can adjust how far in front your front foot is situated after finishing your rep.
5. While keeping your front foot firmly planted on the ground and your rear foot in its elevated position, reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knees to lift your body up until you have returned to an upright position in your split stance.
   1. You can finish the ascent with a slight bend in your front knee rather than fully locking it out.
   2. Like during the descent, keep your torso fairly upright and knees aligned with your feet while having most of your weight shifted onto the front foot.
6. Repeat steps 4 and 5 until you have finished your set and are ready to step forward with your rear leg prior to repeating the exercise by switching which leg is in the forward position and which leg is in the elevated position.
7. After finishing the set with your other leg forward, step forward with your rear leg before you rerack the bar by bending at your hips and knees as you rotate the safety hooks onto the safety bars and return the bar to resting on the safety bars.

## Description

While statically training your gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, this exercise primarily trains your quads, gluteus maximus, and adductor magnus.
