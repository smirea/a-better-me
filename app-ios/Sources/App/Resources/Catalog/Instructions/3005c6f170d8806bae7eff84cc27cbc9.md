## Setting up

1. Determine whether you will set up to bench press within a power rack, which could be modified to perform a variety of barbell lifts and would require you to position a mobile flat bench within the rack, or within a specific bench press station in which the bench is fused to the rack.
   1. Most power racks allow you to position J-hooks (on which the barbell rests before being unracked) and safety pins at incrementally different heights, respectively, to help you situate the bar close to your ideal starting position and to receive the bar safely if you fail a rep.
   2. Bench press stations may also have spotter arms (that function just like safety pins by receiving the bar if you fail a rep) and J-hooks that can be adjusted in height, but a variety of models lack spotter arms and have multiple pairs of fixed-height J-hooks. While most people can find a J-hook height that allows them to bench press effectively, some people will notice that their ideal starting position of the bar cannot be achieved with the available J-hook heights, which can make it difficult to unrack the bar independently.
   3. If you have access to a power rack with safety pins and a bench press station without spotter arms, bench pressing within the power rack is recommended for its additional safety mechanism. The con of using a power rack is that it typically takes longer to prepare compared to a specific bench press station.
2. With a power rack, position the J-hooks to a height that initially allows you to grasp the bar with slightly bent elbows once you are set up on the bench, which will allow you to unrack the bar efficiently. With a bench press station, find which J-hook height places you closest to this position.
   1. After fully straightening your elbows to lift the bar off the J-hooks, the bar should ideally be close to 2in (5cm) above the top of the J-hooks while keeping your shoulder blades tucked back together.
   2. The proper height easily enables you to avoid bumping the J-hooks when unracking the bar while simultaneously not requiring much effort to lift the bar initially.
   3. If the J-hooks are too high, you may have to spread your shoulder blades apart in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks when unracking, which can place you in a less stable position to start your set, since it can be challenging to reposition your shoulder blades after unracking the bar.
   4. If the J-hooks are too low, the unrack will involve a greater-than-ideal pressing range of motion, which will require greater effort and generate more fatigue before your set begins.
   5. If neither of the two closest J-hook heights is ideal, it is generally better to use the height that is a little too low, since being able to start the set with optimal positioning is typically more advantageous than the alternative.
   6. Some trial and error is often needed to find the best J-hook height for you.
3. After situating a Buffalo bar on top of the appropriate J-hooks, use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins/spotter arms prior to re-racking the bar onto the J-hooks.
   1. The appropriate height of safety pins can be lower when using a cambered bar compared to a straight bar.
4. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins/spotter arms to be slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of your rep’s lowering phase.
   1. If you are not training with safety pins/spotter arms that can be positioned to the ideal height, you can avoid adding a pair of collars to the end of the bar. This would allow you to dump the weight to one side and allow the weights to slide off the bar if you become pinned underneath the bar after failing a rep. The con of not using collars is that the plates can accidentally slide to one side when pressing if you happen to tilt the bar asymmetrically, but it can be an acceptable tradeoff to make sure that you can avoid being pinned if you fail a rep.
5. Lie down with your back on the bench. Position yourself so that your mouth is close to being directly underneath the barbell, and your knees are bent with your feet on the floor, approximately shoulder-width apart.
   1. Based on what feels most stable, you can position the soles of your feet flat on the ground with your shins close to being vertical, or you can bend your knees beyond this point to dig the balls of your feet and toes into the ground with your heels off the ground.
   2. Similarly, you can decide whether you point your toes moderately out to the sides or straight ahead based on your preference.
6. Use your hands to rotate the bar so that its cambered center is directed straight up if it is not already facing this way.
7. Firmly grasp the bar with your palms facing forward and your hands mildly to moderately outside shoulder width. Let the bar sit on top of the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) with reasonably straight wrists as you squeeze the bar to prevent it from rotating.
   1. Some people may find that their shoulders feel more comfortable when using a grip that is a bit closer than their standard grip width with a straight bar, but others find using the same grip width to work well for them.
   2. Feel free to try out different hand widths over time to determine where you feel best when using the cambered bar.
   3. For stability, wrap your thumbs around the bar, resulting in the bar being fully enclosed by your hands. If you’re just learning to bench press, avoid positioning your thumbs on the same side of the bar as your other fingers (which is known as a thumbless grip, or a “suicide grip”).
   4. While your wrists do not need to be completely straight, you should avoid grasping the bar with your wrists cocked back into an excessively extended position that may contribute to an increased risk of experiencing wrist discomfort. Simply squeezing the bar as hard as possible will usually keep your wrists from cocking back excessively.
   5. After grasping the bar in a manner that allows you to feel comfortable and stable, maintain this hand and wrist position throughout your set.
8. While allowing your lower back to arch naturally, assume a “proud chest” posture that puffs your chest slightly up toward the ceiling by tightly pulling your shoulder blades back together. Maintain this chest position throughout your set.
   1. As a cue for pulling your shoulder blades back, you can think about trying to squeeze a pencil between your shoulder blades. This should prompt you to feel your upper back muscles contracting, like at the end range of a rowing exercise.
   2. Focusing on adopting a “proud chest” position can naturally place your lower back into a mildly arched position.

## Exercise technique

1. Drive your feet into the ground and squeeze your glutes. Simultaneously brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing in this manner throughout your set to create full-body tension.
   2. From this starting position, your feet should be firmly dug into the ground, while your buttocks, upper back, and head are firmly planted on the bench. Maintain these five points of contact throughout your whole set to maximize your stability and performance.
2. Press the bar off the J-hooks by straightening your elbows, and move the bar forward away from the J-hooks until it is above your shoulders, where you will hold it for a moment before starting to lower the bar.
   1. Continue squeezing the bar throughout your set to prevent it from rotating and keep the center part of its shaft pointing straight up.
   2. Prioritize keeping your shoulder blades pulled back together to avoid losing your upper back tightness during the unrack.
3. Lower the bar toward your lower chest in a controlled manner. Progressively bend your elbows and allow them to flare out moderately away from each other while descending through the full range of motion that your shoulder mobility comfortably permits.
   1. Strive to keep your elbows stacked underneath your wrists as your forearms remain close to vertical throughout the set when viewed from the side. While some lifters can lower the bar fully to the touch the chest with quality technique, how far you can lower the bar while maintaining that alignment will be affected by your individual shoulder mobility.
   2. When moving through your full range of motion, it is normal to feel a stretching sensation in your chest and the front of the shoulders at the bottom.
   3. If you feel shoulder discomfort or pain at the bottom, you most likely descended excessively far relative to your current mobility. Training this exercise over time may improve your mobility, but you should not force yourself into a range that does not feel right for your shoulders.
   4. When lowering the bar toward your lower chest, the bar will track slightly forward toward your abdomen rather than moving exclusively in a vertical line.
   5. This bar path will require that you keep your elbows tucked to a degree instead of fully flaring them out, and the appropriate balance between elbow tuck and elbow flare can naturally occur when keeping your elbows stacked under your wrists as you bring the bar to your lower chest. Near the bottom of the rep, the proper balance should result in your upper arms forming approximately 45-75-degree angles with your torso (when 0 degrees means your upper arms are completely tucked by your sides and 90 degrees means your upper arms form a “T-shape” with your torso).
   6. While you do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, using a controlled and fairly slow tempo is recommended, particularly as you approach the bottom position.
4. Reverse the movement by pressing the bar up. It may help to focus on driving your elbows upward and inward.
   1. You should finish with your elbows fully straightened and the bar situated above your shoulders.
   2. The bar will arc slightly back toward your face as you press it, so that you finish the rep with the bar over your shoulders instead of your lower chest.
   3. Continue driving your feet into the ground while keeping your buttocks firmly planted on the bench, and your shoulder blades pulled back together.
5. Alternate between steps 3 and 4 until you have completed your set and are ready to rerack the bar.
6. After completing the lifting phase of your last rep, keep the bar motionless over your shoulders for about one second before moving the bar back until it contacts the back of both J-hooks.
7. Bend your elbows slightly to lower the bar onto the J-hooks to finish reracking the weight.
   1. Only begin lowering the bar after you have already contacted the back of the J-hooks.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop all regions of your pec major while also training your front delts and triceps.
