## Setting up
1. Pick up a kettlebell or dumbbell and hold it right next to your chest with bent elbows, as your upper arms are slightly in front of your sides (i.e., in the goblet position).
    1. You can use either a kettlebell or a dumbbell, but some people find holding a kettlebell in the goblet position to feel more stable and comfortable, especially with a fairly heavy weight.
    2. Based on your preference and the load you use, you can transfer your kettlebell or dumbbell into the goblet position through a couple of different approaches.
    3. An effective approach is initially to pick up the weight by the handle through a deadlift motion and set it on an elevated surface (like a bench or plyometric box) with the handle facing up if you use a kettlebell or the handle directed vertically if using a dumbbell (as only one end of the dumbbell is on the surface). Then squat down until the weight is right in front of your chest, firmly grasp it in the goblet position, and squat up with the weight. If you use a kettlebell, grasp it by its horns (i.e., side handles). If you use a dumbbell, cup your hands around the top end of the weight with the base of your palms by the handle, and your fingers wrapped around the rest of that end of the weight.
    4. With a lighter kettlebell, you can tilt it onto its side with the weight base directed toward you, squat down to grasp its horns, stand upright with it, and curl it into the goblet position.
    5. With a lighter dumbbell, you can also pick it up with one hand and curl it up across your body to be in front of your chest. Then cup your free hand around the top end of the dumbbell with the base of its palm by the handle and fingers wrapped around the top end of the weight before repositioning your other hand to cup the other side of the weight in the same manner.
2. While keeping your chest up, position your feet approximately hip-width apart, with your toes pointing straight ahead.
    1. Positioning yourself into a hip-width stance rather than a narrower stance can help you maintain your balance during this exercise.
    2. While holding the weight in the goblet position throughout your set, your upper back muscles will have to work to prevent the weight from rounding your upper back, which would prompt your chest to cave.
## Exercise technique
1. While keeping the entire sole of one foot firmly planted on the ground, step backward with the other leg before contacting the ground with your forefoot (ball of your foot and bottom of your toes).
    1. Step far enough backward that you will be able to contact the ground with your rear knee, which may initially require trying out different step lengths if you are new to backward lunges.
    2. Strive to step backward in a straight line rather than stepping closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
    3. Keep your torso in an upright position and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
    4. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
2. After contacting the ground with your rear foot, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until you are able to tap the ground gently with the kneecap of your rear leg.
    1. Continue keeping your torso upright and the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the ground.
    2. Keep your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
    3. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your rear knee, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
    4. While the forefoot of your rear leg will be in contact with the ground, the rest of the foot’s sole will be off the ground as it is directed backward.
    5. At the end of the lowering phase, your front leg’s thigh should be close to parallel to the ground.
    6. You should perform steps 1 and 2 as a smooth and continuous movement.
    7. If you initially took an excessively short step backward, you may not be able to tap the ground with your rear knee unless you also lift the heel of your front foot up off the ground.
    8. If you find that you have to lift your front heel up to tap the ground, simply descend as much as you can without lifting up your front heel, and take a longer step backward during your next rep.
3. Reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knees as you step forward with your rear leg to lift your body up until you have returned to the starting position with your feet symmetrically hip-width apart and your torso fully upright.
    1. Like during the descent, keep your torso upright and knees aligned with your feet while doing so.
4. Repeat steps 1-3 while lunging backward with your opposite side.
5. Alternate between steps 1-4 until you have completed your set and are ready to set the weight down.
    1. When setting the weight down, you can just perform the reverse of whichever technique you initially used to place it into the goblet position.
## Description
This exercise primarily trains your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.

### **Can you make your lunge more glute-dominant or quad-dominant?**

When lunging, you can utilize somewhat of a range of varying step lengths while being able to contact the ground with your rear knee as you keep the entirety of your front foot’s sole on the ground. At the short end of this step range, you will be able to drive your front knee farther forward relative to your foot, which will result in your shin being inclined forward. With a long step, you can keep your front shin in a vertical position as the knee stays stacked above the ankle, which requires less knee flexion (bending) range of motion. A longer step may also allow the thigh of the rear leg to be angled further backward (via greater hip extension) compared to the more vertical position of the rear thigh with a shorter step. Since the rear knee can initially be positioned higher up off the floor when the thigh is angled backward, a long step can permit someone to move through a mildly greater hip flexion range of motion during the descent until the rear knee contacts the floor.

These differences can enable a lifter to train the quads in a somewhat more lengthened position when lunging with a short step since the quads are lengthened in increasing angles of knee flexion. On the other hand, a long step may allow someone to train the gluteus maximus and adductor magnus at somewhat longer muscle lengths since these primary hip extensor muscles are lengthened in increasing angles of hip flexion. This has the potential to be relevant because the available body of evidence indicates that the peak length in which a muscle is trained during a particular exercise can most likely influence how much growth the exercise may stimulate. Plenty of muscles have yet to be studied in terms of how their growth may be affected by the lengths in which they are loaded during exercise, but several studies have investigated this topic for the quads. The findings of five of these studies align with each other in the sense that training the quads at moderately long peak lengths (involving up to 90-120 degrees of knee flexion) stimulates greater quad growth than training the quads at moderately short peak lengths (involving up to 50-65 degrees of knee flexion). With at least four separate studies (involving squat interventions, machine leg extension interventions, and a multi-exercise intervention that included lunges) reporting the same outcome, there is fairly strong justification to perform lunges until the rear knee gently taps the ground rather than training through a meaningfully more shallow range of motion. However, the quads can already be trained at moderately long lengths when lunging with a long step, and using a short step changes that by only a fairly small magnitude. This was demonstrated by Escamalla et al. (2025), who reported peak knee flexion angles to be approximately 90-100 degrees when participants lunged with a long step until their rear knee contacted the ground, while they reached about 100-110 degrees of knee flexion with a short step.

Up to a certain point training the quads at longer peak lengths than shorter peak lengths is likely to promote greater hypertrophy, but this effect may have a ceiling. Kubo et al. (2019) found similar quad growth to occur after participants performed a half squat intervention involving 90 degrees of peak knee flexion or a full squat intervention involving 140 degrees of knee flexion. Larsen et al. (2025) also found similar quad growth to occur whether leg pressing until 100 degrees of knee flexion or until the end range of knee flexion for each participant (which was 154 degrees of knee flexion on average). While new evidence may emerge in the future indicating otherwise, these findings suggest that training into very high angles of knee flexion to highly lengthen the quads is not necessarily a superior means of developing the quads than training to approximately 90-100 degrees of knee flexion. Furthermore, the previously mentioned study from Escamalla et al. (2025) found EMG activity of the quads generally to be greater when using a long step compared to a short step. This does not mean that lunging with a long step is necessarily more effective for training the quads than lunging with a short step, but the preponderance of evidence suggests that using a short step is unlikely to train the quads better than lunging with a long step.

On the other hand, it is worth noting that Kubo et al. (2019) found the aforementioned full squat group to experience about three times as much gluteus maximus growth and a little over twice the hip adductor muscle (involving the primary hip extensor adductor magnus) growth relative to the half squat group. With a compound exercise that meaningfully trains knee extension and hip extension simultaneously (like lunge and squat variations), training into high angles of hip flexion may consequently may be beneficial to amplify the hypertrophy stimulus to the hip extensor muscles compared to reaching only a moderately high degree of hip flexion. The findings from one squat study do not guarantee that the same relationship will exist when lunging, but it is certainly plausible that lunges can be similar in this respect. While there is no legitimate reason to think that lunging with a long step would train the hip extensor muscles less effectively than lunging with a short step, the difference in hip range of motion between step lengths potentially may be of a low enough magnitude to be inconsequential for many people. The directionality may be similar, but lunging with a long step as opposed to a short step increases the hip range of motion to a smaller degree than full squatting instead of half squatting. In isolation, the small effect that lunge step length has on the length of hip extensor muscles may limit the degree to which step length influences hip extensor muscle growth, but step length is not the only variable you can modify in an effort to amplify the stimulus you provide to these muscles.

Just as moderately different step lengths can be used when lunging, so too can somewhat different trunk positions be utilized when performing lunge variations. On one end of the spectrum, you can maintain a fully upright trunk as your torso faces straight ahead throughout your lunge like it would face when normally standing upright. Alternatively you can incline your trunk forward by hinging at the hip of your front leg, which results in your torso facing at an angle between forward and downward. It should be noted that this forward trunk lean occurs from hip motion as you maintain the natural curvature of your lower back rather than spinal motion that can occur from simply rounding your lower back. Relative to if you held weights in your hands, lunging with a forward trunk lean can be quite difficult when holding a weight in the goblet position, so other variations may be preferable if you opt to use a forward trunk lean. Compared to lunging with an upright trunk position, lunging with an inclined trunk can allow you to reach a greater angle hip flexion for the front leg, which will allow you to train your hip extensor muscles (e.g., gluteus maximus and adductor magnus) at longer muscle lengths. When you use this forward trunk lean simultaneously with a long step, you may be able to reach a meaningfully greater angle (such as near 40 degrees more) of hip flexion than could otherwise be achieved with a short step and an upright trunk position. The discrepancy between these two lunge approaches could correspondingly be fairly close to the difference between half squats and full squats in terms of the peak lengths in which the gluteus maximus and adductor magnus are trained. Besides affecting the length in which these muscles are trained, the trunk position used while lunging can affect the resistance imparted to the hip extensor muscles. Relative to an upright trunk, an inclined trunk shifts your upper body’s center of mass forward along with any weights you may be using. Doing so increases the lever arm through which resistance acts on your front leg’s hip joint while simultaneously decreasing the lever arm through which resistance acts on your front leg’s knee joint. Consequently, your front leg’s hip extensor muscles will be loaded to a relatively greater degree when lunging with an inclined trunk, while your front leg’s quads will receive comparatively greater loading when lunging with an upright trunk. While not directly proven, the combination of subjecting your hip extensor muscles to relatively greater resistance while they are trained at longer muscle lengths can likely contribute to lunges with a long step and forward trunk lean being more effective for developing your gluteus maximus and adductor magnus than lunging with a short step and upright trunk. On the other hand, keeping your trunk upright while using whichever step length that feels most comfortable to you potentially may be somewhat more effective if you prioritize quad development.

### **Forward versus backward lunges**

Forward and backward lunges are much more similar than different as they both involve similar movement patterns that resist knee extension to train the quads while resisting hip extension to train gluteus maximus and adductor magnus. While you can consciously strive to reach the same knee and hip positions during each variation, people may naturally tend to perform these two exercises with somewhat different knee and hip motions. Comfort et al. (2015) and Goulette et al. (2021) both compared bodyweight forward lunges to backward lunges and found participants to reach a slightly greater angle of peak knee flexion and peak ankle dorsiflexion (which results in a greater forward lean of the shin) during the forward lunge. The differences were significantly different for Goulette et al. (2021) but not significantly different for Comfort et al. (2015). While not significantly different, Comfort et al. (2015) also found reverse lunges to involve slightly greater peak hip flexion, and Goulette et al. (2021) found reverse lunges to involve significantly greater forward trunk lean despite participants being instructed to maintain an upright trunk position. When taking a step backward, people may naturally be more likely to adopt somewhat of a forward trunk lean to maintain balance during a reverse lunge, which may contribute to a slightly greater hip flexion angle during the descent even without consciously striving to do so. This difference in average trunk position may contribute to why Song and Woo (2021) found the lumbar erector spinae to exhibit significantly higher EMG activity during bodyweight backward lunges compared to bodyweight forward lunges.

The available evidence also demonstrates that loading parameters may differ between the forward and backward lunge. Comfort et al. (2015) calculated resistive torque acting on the knee and hip to be greater during forward lunges. Because resistive torque acting on the knee and hip is what your lower body muscles must oppose while lunging, this suggests that forward lunges may tend to be more challenging than backward lunges when performed for the same number of reps with the same resistance. If a similar relationship applies to weighted lunges, heavier loads may be required when reverse lunging to achieve the same level of resistive torque when forward lunging. Goulette et al. (2021) additionally reported the patellofemoral joint (of the knee) to experience significantly greater forces, stress, and loading rates during forward lunges, which also resulted in higher quad force. Along with the difference in resistive torque that Comfort et al. (2015) identified, these differences in knee loading may arise in part from greater momentum being present when the lead leg begins being loaded during the forward lunge compared to the backward lunge. With a backward lunge, you can start loading your lead leg with your entire sole flat on the ground the moment you lift your other foot up off the ground. In contrast, your center of mass has forward momentum by the time the heel of your lead leg contacts the ground during a forward lunge, which may increase the demands imposed onto your lead leg. Of any of the differences identified, the discrepancy in patellofemoral joint loading rate was the greatest given that the loading rate of the forward lunge was 2.2 times that of the backward lunge. Having a higher loading rate and magnitude of forces imparted into the knee does not necessarily mean that forward lunges are problematic to the knee, but these factors may be relevant to lifters who have pre-existing knee issues. While there is no reason to fear forward lunges, certain individuals may find forward lunges to provoke knee discomfort but backward lunges may feel better especially if utilizing a slower descent that can be more readily performed with backward lunges than forward lunges. Because they are quite similar to one another, if one lunge variation simply feels better to your body than the other, that can be a completely legitimate justification to train that variation.

### **Are lunges unilateral, bilateral, or something in between?**

Lunge variations are commonly considered to be unilateral exercises which have been defined by Fountaine (2018) to be exercises where “each limb works independently of the other to create the desired movement.” Unilateral exercises differ from bilateral exercises where “both limbs are used in unison to contract the muscles, which create forces, and subsequently move a given load.” Exercises can clearly be bilateral when both limbs are symmetrically loaded as they simultaneously move in the same manner (such as during a barbell back squat) or unilateral when one limb limb is loaded as the other limb remains suspended in space (such as during a pistol squat). However, both legs function simultaneously against resistance in an asymmetrical manner to move your body (and any weights you may add) during lunge variations.

The front leg may be loaded to a greater extent than the rear leg, but the rear leg can still be meaningfully loaded when lunging as demonstrated by Hefzy et al. (1997) finding the rear leg to receive about one quarter of the total load during a forward lunge. Hofmann et al. (2017) provided further evidence supporting the notable involvement of the rear leg when assessing forward lunges performed with a vertical trunk and vertical shin, a vertical trunk and inclined shin, or an inclined trunk and inclined shin. The researchers found that knee loading (measured as knee extensor impulse) was similar between the front and rear legs when lunging with the trunk and front shin both inclined forward, but the rear knee experienced meaningfully greater loading when using either technique with a vertical trunk position. In light of this data, Hofmann et al. (2017) considered lunges to be bilateral exercises along with split squats, which Schultz et al. (2014) also reported to impart greater loading to the rear knee than the front knee while simultaneously transferring more weight into the front leg. The specific technique in which someone performs a lunge can certainly affect how load is distributed between the two sides. Besides modifying the trunk angle, people may intentionally be able to shift almost all of their weight into the lead leg while using the rear leg lightly for balance assistance or they may be able to focus on pushing off forcibly through the rear leg to perform much of the ascent through work from the rear leg. The ability to lunge with variable techniques can further amplify the ambiguity with respect to whether lunges should be classified as unilateral or bilateral. What someone considers to be a unilateral or bilateral exercise can also be notably influenced by specifically how the terms are operationally defined, and there can be legitimate reasons to classify lunges either way. Given this lack of clarity, this database seeks a middle road by classifying lunge variations simply as asymmetrical exercises since both legs are simultaneously loaded in an asymmetrical fashion.

### **Lunges versus split squats**

Forward and backward lunges are quite similar to split squats given that they train the same muscle groups and can look identical to each other at the bottom of the lowering phase. The distinction between them is that your feet remain stationary throughout a split squat set except for the initial step you take into the split stance position and the step you take to reposition your feet near each other to conclude the set. With lunges, you start each rep with your feet in a symmetrical stance, step backward or forward (depending on the variation) with one foot during a rep, and then reverse the step to bring your feet back together at the end of each rep. This difference contributes to a lunge set involving a greater proportion of time in which your balance can be most challenged since the least stable portion of either exercise is when one foot is briefly off the ground when taking a step, which occurs much less frequently during split squats. However, a lunge set also involves a greater proportion of time in which your balance can be least challenged since you are in a highly stable position whenever you are standing in a symmetrical hip width stance, which you adopt between each lunge rep but not in between each split squat rep. Correspondingly, lunges can have a greater fluctuation in the balance demands throughout a set with both more frequent moments of maximal balance difficulty and more frequent moments of minimal balance difficulty. In comparison, split squats can involve a fairly constant level of moderate instability throughout most of the set, which contributes to split squats having a reputation of being relatively less challenging from a balance perspective than lunges. Nonetheless, certain individuals may actually find that it is more difficult to sustain balance throughout a split squat set since they do not receive the brief “balance break” after each rep that lunges allow when the feet are returned to the highly stable symmetrical stance.
