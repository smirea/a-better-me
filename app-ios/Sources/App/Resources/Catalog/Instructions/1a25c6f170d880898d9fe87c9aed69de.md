## Setting up
1. Adjust the Smith machine’s bar to a height that allows you to position the bar on your upper back and un-rack it in a stable position.
    1. As an initial approximation, you can position the bar such that it is a few finger widths below the height of your collarbone.
2. Position the safety stops (that are attached to the sliding rails) so that they are slightly below the height of the bar when your rear knee taps the ground at the end of the lowering phase of your split squat.
    1. To find this point for your body, you can perform a rep or two of the split squat with the unloaded bar as described in the “exercise technique” section, but you can initially put the safety stops a little below the top of your hip bones when standing upright as an approximation.
3. Add your desired weight to the bar and face the center of the bar with approximately a hip-width stance.
    1. If your Smith machine has angled sliding rails, position yourself to face the side in which the bar will track slightly forward as it descends (which results in the bottom of the rails being farther in front of you than the top of the rails).
    2. If your Smith machine has purely vertical sliding rails, you can perform the same movement regardless of which direction you face.
4. Grasp each side of the bar with a somewhat wider than shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
    1. The proper grip width will vary from person to person, and what feels best to you will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
    2. If you are not accustomed to exercises that have a bar on your back, you may have to try out different hand placements to find one that personally suits you well.
5. While holding onto the bar, shallowly bend at your hips and knees to then walk under the bar and situate it on your upper back in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
    1. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back.
    2. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back.
    3. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
    4. If you feel like the bar may slide down your back after standing upright with it, you most likely have the bar placed too low on your back.
    5. While positioning the bar on your upper back, you may have to readjust where you are grasping the bar if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
    6. If you are not accustomed to having a bar on your back, you may not feel particularly comfortable doing so, especially when it is loaded with additional weight, but whichever position you use should not be painful.
    7. If you need to rise up onto the balls of your feet and lift your heels off the ground in order to position the bar on your upper back, you can remove the weights from the bar, lower the height of the bar, and add the weight plates back before repositioning the bar on your upper back.
## Exercise technique
1. Pull down on the bar to tighten your upper back muscles, and unrack it by starting to stand up as you straighten your knees.
2. After the bar rests fully on your upper back rather than the safety bars, rotate the safety hooks out away from the safety bars with a slight wrist flick before standing fully upright with the bar.
3. While keeping the entire sole of one foot firmly planted on the ground, take a fairly long step backward with the other leg before contacting the ground with your forefoot (ball of your foot and bottom of your toes).
    1. Slightly bend the knee on the side of your stationary foot while doing so.
    2. Step far enough backward that you will be able to contact the ground with your rear knee, which may initially require trying out different step lengths if you are new to split squats.
    3. Strive to step backward in a straight line rather than stepping closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
    4. Keep your torso in an upright position and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
    5. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
4. From your split stance position, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until you are able to tap the ground gently with the kneecap of your rear leg.
    1. Continue keeping your torso upright and the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the ground.
    2. While the forefoot of your rear leg will stay in contact with the ground, the rest of the foot’s sole will be off the ground as it is directed backward.
    3. Keep your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
    4. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your rear knee, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
    5. At the end of the lowering phase, your front leg’s thigh should be close to parallel to the ground.
    6. If you initially took an excessively short step backward, you may not be able to tap the ground with your rear knee unless you also lift the heel of your front foot up off the ground.
    7. If you find that you have to lift your front heel up to tap the ground with your knee, simply descend as much as you can without lifting up your front heel, and step somewhat farther backward after you ascend before performing your next rep.
5. While keeping both of your feet planted on the ground, reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knees to lift your body up until you have returned to an upright position in your split stance.
    1. You can finish the ascent with a slight bend in your front knee rather than fully locking it out.
    2. Like during the descent, keep your torso upright and knees aligned with your feet while doing so.
6. Repeat steps 4 and 5 until you have finished your set and are ready to step forward with your rear leg to return to having your feet symmetrically hip-width apart before repeating the exercise by switching which leg is in the forward position.
7. After finishing the set with your other leg forward, step forward with your rear leg to return to having your feet symmetrically hip-width apart before re-racking the bar.
    1. Re-rack the bar by bending at your hips and knees in a quarter squat-like manner as you rotate the safety hooks onto the safety bars and return the bar to resting on the safety bars.
## Description
This exercise primarily trains your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.
