## Setting up

1. Adjust the height of the Roman chair’s pad to a level that positions the top of your hip bones somewhat above the top of the pads when you set up with your feet on the foot plate.
   1. This position will allow you to move freely about your hips in contrast to if the pad is contacting the top of your hip bones, which would restrict hip motion and prompt you to perform the exercise via spinal motion.
   2. If the top of the pad is positioned much lower than the top of the hip bones (rather than only somewhat lower), some individuals may feel knee discomfort, so a sweet spot can exist for pad placement.
   3. Some trial and error may be needed to find your ideal pad position after initially completing step 2.
2. While placing your hands onto the handles for balance support, step onto the foot plate one leg at a time with the back of your heels in contact with the back of the foot plate, so that the entirety of your soles are in contact with the foot plate.
   1. Position your feet symmetrically in a stance that feels comfortable to you, which can involve pointing your toes somewhat outward or pointing them fairly straight forward.
   2. Situating your feet approximately hip width to shoulder width apart from each other will result in a viable stance width, but how wide of a stance you can use will be influenced by the width of your Roman chair’s foot plate.
   3. Your knees should be straight after you have finished setting your feet on the foot plate.
3. Check to make sure that the pad is positioned as described by step 1, and readjust the pad height if it is not prior to resuming your setup.
4. Cross your arms across your chest with bent elbows to position each hand on the front of the opposite shoulder.
   1. Maintain this arm position throughout your set.

## Exercise technique

1. Squeeze your glutes to keep your torso up such that you can draw approximately a straight line from your legs through your hip and to your torso as opposed to having your torso bent over.
   1. Throughout your set, keep your lower back and neck near a neutral position, like you have when you are normally standing upright and looking straight ahead.
   2. This is the starting position of the exercise, where your body should look like it does when normally standing upright except at an incline.
2. Begin bracing your core by taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward.
   1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
   2. After the deep “belly breath,” you can continue thinking about tensing the front of your abdominals, sides of your abdominals, and lower back muscles simultaneously in a manner that allows you to form a “360 degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
   3. As a helpful cue for your “360 degree brace,” you can tense the front and sides of your abdominals outward like you would if you are bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
3. While keeping your core braced, hinge at your hips through your available range of motion in a controlled manner.
   1. Nearly all of the motion should occur from you moving at your hips as you keep your trunk in a static position that maintains the natural curvature of your lower back (as opposed to bending over by rounding your lower back).
   2. Maintain a straight knee position, but you should avoid intentionally locking your knees out by squeezing your quads.
   3. While hinging at your hips and keeping your hands on your shoulders, you should feel a stretching sensation in your hamstrings that progressively intensifies.
   4. Most people will be able to descend until their shoulders are lower than their hips, but how deeply you are able to hinge without meaningfully rounding your lower back will be dependent on your hamstring flexibility.
   5. Once you have achieved what feels like the end range to which your hamstrings can safely stretch, you have finished descending through your available hip range of motion.
4. Reverse the movement by lifting your torso up through your hips until you have returned to the starting position, where your torso is aligned with your legs.
   1. You can finish the lifting phase by fully squeezing your glutes, which helps cue hip movement at the top as opposed to spinal movement.
   2. Avoid arching your lower back excessively at the top since the lifting phase should simply be a reversal of the hip motion you just performed during the lowering phase.
5. Alternate between steps 3 and 4 until you have completed your set.
   1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the upright position.
   2. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.

## Description

While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise dynamically trains your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
