## Setting up

1. Add weight plates of your desired load to a barbell situated on the ground under the head of an inclined bench.
   1. Ideally set the bench to an incline angle that lets you just reach the bar with straight elbows and shoulders pulled forward once you are resting your torso on the bench.
   2. Which angle is best will be affected by the diameter of the weight plates you add to the bar and your arm length. Using a higher incline will be better for lifters with long arms who use full-diameter plates (like 45lb (20kg) plates or bumper plates) compared to lifters with short arms who load the bar exclusively with smaller diameter plates.
   3. While trying out different bench angles may be helpful, initially selecting an incline near 30-45 degrees is often appropriate.
2. Lie down with your chest contacting the head of the bench and your legs spread out as you position the balls of your feet on the ground.
3. Reach down and firmly grasp the bar with a slightly outside shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
   1. A slightly outside shoulder-width is often appropriate, but feel free to adjust your grip width based on your preference.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue keeping your core braced from this point on.
2. Pull the bar up toward your upper abdomen/lower chest by driving your elbows simultaneously backward and upward while pulling your shoulder blades together.
   1. Rather than lifting your chest up through excessive lower back arching, keep your chest and the rest of your torso in contact with the bench throughout your set.
   2. Row through your full range of motion, which ideally results in your elbows finishing at shoulder height or close to this point. Your setup can affect your end range, since the bottom of your bench may physically determine how high you may lift the bar.
   3. Rather than fully flaring your elbows straight out to the sides or keeping them fully tucked by your sides, let your elbows naturally flare out away from your sides to a degree that feels comfortable to you.
   4. As a cue for training your upper back muscles, you can think about squeezing your shoulder blades together at the top of the pull as if you were trying to pinch a pencil with your shoulder blades.
3. Reverse the movement by lowering the bar in a controlled manner until you have returned to the starting position.
   1. If your bench setup permits, lower the bar until your elbows have fully straightened.
   2. If the plates reach the ground before your elbows have fully straightened, you can finish lowering the bar once the plates lightly tap the ground.
   3. Allow the weight to pull your shoulder blades apart to stretch your upper back muscles.
   4. You do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, but you should actively control the entire descent rather than letting the weight pull your arms down rapidly.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set and are ready to place the bar back down onto the ground.

## Description

This exercise can effectively train much of the musculature constituting your middle and upper back, including your lat, teres major, rear delt, traps, and rhomboids.
