## Setting up

1. Determine what you will use to anchor your looped elastic bands securely. You can use a variety of approaches based on your equipment availability, and the specific details for the setup can differ depending on which option you select.
   1. Ultimately you want to make sure that whatever you use as your anchor point will not move during your set. Your anchor point should have a smooth surface that will not tear the band.
   2. A deadlift platform with band pegs can provide ideal anchor points for long bands, but it is not available at most gyms.
   3. A power rack with band pegs at the bottom is also a very stable choice that allows you to use short or long bands within the rack. A power rack with base beams elevated above the ground can additionally be used to loop the bands around the base beams as anchor points.
   4. Without any of the aforementioned equipment, you can anchor a single long band by draping it around the center of the bar and standing with the middle of your feet on top of it. This method is convenient because it only requires a band, a barbell, and weight plates. However, it may be less stable compared to if you are able to anchor the bands to band pegs.
   5. Bands can alternatively be anchored to a pair of heavy dumbbells on each end of the bar, but this approach also requires that you use other weights to anchor the four dumbbells. This can be a more time-intensive set-up, and it may be difficult to keep the dumbbells stationary with a certain level of band tension. If you use dumbbells, you can place a pair of weight plates on the sides of each dumbbell to reduce the likelihood that they will roll.
2. After adding weight plates to the barbell situated on the ground, anchor your bands to the barbell with your chosen method.
   1. Inspect the band to confirm it does not have any small tears, since a small tear can swiftly become a full tear when stretching a band.
   2. Once positioned, the bands should be flat rather than twisted.
   3. Ideally the bands should be mildly tensed while the weight is on the ground. If the bands are slack, they will not be as stable and may move during your set.
   4. The bands should provide the same amount of tension on each side of the bar.
3. Stand upright in approximately a hip-width stance close in front of the middle of the bar, as the bar is over the middle of your feet, with your toes pointing straight ahead or slightly turned out to the sides.
   1. For most lifters, this situates the bar about one inch (2.5cm) away from the shins.
   2. While using a near hip-width stance places most people into a viable position, the precise stance width that is ideal for you as an individual may be influenced by your body structure and mobility, so you can feel free to modify your stance a bit based on where you feel strong and comfortable.
   3. You can similarly decide whether to point your toes straight ahead or slightly out to the side based on what feels best to you.
   4. The size of the weight plates you add to the bar will determine how high the bar lies above the ground, which consequently affects your range of motion. Adding a pair of 45lb (20kg) plates (or 25kg plates common in gyms outside the USA) or bumper plates will elevate the bar to the standard height (about 9in (23cm)) when deadlifting from the floor. When only using smaller diameter plates, the exercise will function as a deficit deadlift with a greater range of motion that may exceed the range of motion in which some people wish to train if they lack the mobility to perform a deficit deadlift with appropriate technique. If you do not have access to bumper plates and are using loads that are not heavy enough to utilize the largest diameter plates, you can perform a block pull or rack pull with the bar elevated to where it would be when deadlifting from the floor with large diameter plates.
4. With your arms directed down toward the ground, straight elbows, and your shoulders packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up), hinge at your hips while bending your knees until your hands are level with the bar.
   1. More motion should come from your hips than your knees as you descend, and your knees should not track particularly far forward, but you can allow your knees to shift slightly forward rather than maintaining completely vertical shins.
   2. As a cue for hinging, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks as you sit back while your hands are carrying something, and after you have hinged through your available hip range motion, you can bend your knees further to reach the bar.
   3. Throughout this exercise, keep your lower back looking flat near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright, rather than excessively rounding your lower back while doing so.
   4. Similarly, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
   5. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and engage your back muscles.
5. Firmly grasp the bar with a shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
   1. Your arms should be directed straight down such that they form approximately 90-degree angles with the bar.
   2. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   3. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward as you tense these muscles like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
   2. Strive to tighten the entirety of your core to form a “360 degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
   3. You will continue bracing your core throughout your set, which will help you maintain a rigid trunk position while you move through your hips and knees.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
   1. Doing so can help you wedge your hips into an advantageous starting position through a maneuver known as pulling the slack out of the bar.
   2. When creating “pre-tension,” pressing through the ground should pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
3. While keeping your chest up and back flat with the bar close to your shins, drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar as you “push the floor away” while extending (straightening) your hips and knees.
   1. As you move about your knees and hips, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the pull rather than inclining further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
4. After the bar reaches near the middle of your shins, focus on thrusting your hips forward as you continue extending your hips and knees until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
   1. You should lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
   2. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your back.
   3. As you lift the bar higher up, the band tension will progressively increase.
5. While keeping your back flat, reverse the movement by hinging at your hips while bending your knees to lower the bar until the plates have returned to resting on the ground.
   1. You can initially focus on sitting your buttocks back while keeping your shins vertical and allowing your knees to bend naturally until the bar has cleared your knees, after which you can allow your knees to track mildly forward as you further bend your knees and hips to finish lowering the bar.
6. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the ground for a moment (rather than simply tapping the ground and immediately deadlifting the bar up again) before continuing to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
   1. While you do not need to pause with the bar on the ground for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferred between reps.
   2. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps with the bar on the ground.
   3. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the next rep.

## Description

While also training your spinal extensors, this exercise can train much of your lower body musculature, including your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, quads, and adductor magnus.
