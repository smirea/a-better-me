## Setting up

1. Position a power rack’s safety pins a little below the height of your knees when standing upright.
   1. Certain power racks will not allow you to position safety pins near this level, so this variation will not be feasible in every power rack. If your rack is not suitable for this variation, you can select the “conventional below-the-knee block pull” from this database to provide a similar training effect.
   2. If the closest safety pin setting of your rack situates the bar somewhat higher than your knees, you may be able to perform a below-the-knee block pull by elevating your feet like you would for a deficit deadlift. To do so, you can place a large diameter weight plate or deadlift block in the center of your rack, and you will stand on top of it to perform your rack pull from a closer to below-the-knee starting position. Depending on your preferred stance width, you can also position two plates or deadlift blocks directly next to each other if the soles of your feet do not fit fully onto one plate or deadlift block.
2. Place a barbell centered on top of the safety pins, and add your desired weight plates to it.
   1. Particularly as the load on the bar increases, rack pulls risk damaging a bar if you do not utilize a well-controlled lowering phase, which people often do not.
   2. Certain gyms have bars that were previously bent from rack pulls and are now specifically designated to be used for rack pulls to avoid damaging other bars.
   3. When using a well-controlled lowering phase during every rep, you most likely can avoid damaging the bar when performing a rack pull.
   4. If you do not feel confident in your ability to avoid damaging a bar when performing a rack pull, it is not recommended to perform rack pulls with a bar you do not own unless you receive approval from the gym owner or are using a bar designated for rack pulls.
3. Stand upright in approximately a hip-width stance close in front of the middle of the bar, as the bar is over the middle of your feet, with your toes pointing straight ahead or slightly turned out to the sides.
   1. While using a near hip-width stance places most people into a viable position, the precise stance width that is ideal for you as an individual may be influenced by your body structure and mobility, so you can feel free to modify your stance a bit based on where you feel strong and comfortable.
   2. You can similarly decide whether to point your toes straight ahead or slightly out to the side based on what feels best to you.
4. With your arms directed down toward the ground, straight elbows, and your shoulders packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up), hinge at your hips while allowing your knees to bend until your hands are level with the bar.
   1. More motion should come from your hips than your knees as you descend, and your shins should remain close to being vertical, but you can allow your knees to shift slightly forward to bring your shins right next to the bar.
   2. As a cue for hinging, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks as you sit back while your hands are carrying something, and after you have hinged through your available hip range motion, you can bend your knees a bit further to reach the bar.
   3. When hinging your hips properly, it is normal to feel a stretching sensation in your hamstrings on the back of your thighs at the bottom of your hip hinge.
   4. Throughout this exercise, keep your lower back looking flat near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright, rather than excessively rounding your lower back while doing so.
   5. Similarly, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
   6. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and engage your back muscles.
5. Firmly grasp the bar with a shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
   1. Your arms should be directed straight down, such that they form approximately 90-degree angles with the bar.
   2. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   3. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward as you tense these muscles like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
   2. Strive to tighten the entirety of your core to form a “360 degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
   3. You will continue bracing your core throughout your set, which will help you maintain a rigid trunk position while you move through your hips and knees.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force that would lift the bar if it were only half as heavy.
   1. Doing so can help you wedge your hips into an advantageous starting position through a maneuver known as pulling the slack out of the bar.
   2. When creating “pre-tension,” pressing through the ground should pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
3. While keeping your chest up and back flat with the bar close to your shins, drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar as you “push the floor away” while extending (straightening) your hips and knees.
   1. As you move about your knees and hips, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the pull rather than inclining further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
4. Immediately after the bar lifts off the pins, focus on thrusting your hips forward as you continue extending your hips and knees until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
   1. The “push the floor away” cue only applies to the very start of the rep since thrusting your hips forward is chiefly what will allow you to lift the weight during this variation.
   2. You should lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
   3. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your back.
5. While keeping your back flat, reverse the movement by hinging at your hips while bending your knees to lower the bar in a controlled manner until the bar has returned to resting on the pins.
   1. You can initially focus on sitting your buttocks back while keeping your shins vertical and allowing your knees to bend naturally until the bar has cleared your knees, after which you can allow your knees to track slightly forward as you further bend your knees and hips to finish lowering the bar.
   2. While you do not need to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, you absolutely should not slam the bar onto the pins in a jarring manner.
6. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the pins for a moment (rather than simply tapping the pins and immediately deadlifting the bar up again) before continuing to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
   1. While you do not need to pause with the bar on the pins for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferring between reps.
   2. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps with the bar on the pins.
   3. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the next rep.

## Description

While statically training your erector spinae, this exercise dynamically trains your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.
