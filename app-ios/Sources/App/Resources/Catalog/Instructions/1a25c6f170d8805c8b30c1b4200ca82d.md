## Setting up

1. Pick up a pair of kettlebells and stand upright while firmly holding the kettlebells at your sides with straight elbows as your palms face in toward your thighs and your arms are directed down toward the ground.
   1. If you are using kettlebells that you consider fairly heavy, reaching forward from a bent-over position to pick up both kettlebells simultaneously from a rack may be awkward and challenging.
   2. For an easier setup, you can pick up one kettlebell, carry it, place it on a flat bench (or sturdy box), and move the other kettlebell onto the bench before picking up both kettlebells from a mechanically advantaged position.
   3. Pick up the kettlebells with a hip hinge or squat motion, where you lift them by extending your hips and knees while keeping your lower back near a neutral position, like you have when you are normally standing upright (rather than meaningfully rounding your lower back while doing so).
2. Stand in approximately a hip-width stance with the back of your legs against the edge of a flat exercise bench or similarly sturdy elevated object that will not slide (like a split squat roller, couch, or plyometric box with a somewhat compliant surface).
   1. While not as commonly utilized as a bench, the most comfortable surface will typically be a split squat roller or a barbell that has a squat/hip thrust pad on it and is placed on top of the J-cups of a power rack set to near knee height.
   2. The split squat roller is ideal since it allows for a quicker setup than the barbell technique, but split squat rollers are not as widely available.
3. While keeping your feet approximately hip-width apart, step forward with one foot until its heel is just in front of your other foot’s big toe before doing the same with your opposite foot.
   1. This results in your front foot now being about two “foot lengths” in front of where you started when your calves were touching the object you will use to elevate your rear foot.
   2. While this strategy can consistently situate most people in a viable starting position for the exercise, somewhat of a range of foot placements can be effectively used including having your front foot two and a half “foot lengths” in front of where you started (which may be even more comfortable for some people).

## Exercise technique

1. Shift your bodyweight into your front foot before placing the top surface (on the side of your shoe laces) of your rear foot on top of the elevated surface with that side’s knee bent.
   1. To help shift your bodyweight into your front foot, you can lean your torso mildly forward by hinging at your hip as you slightly bend that side’s knee.
   2. Once you have situated your rear foot into the elevated position, you can return to keeping your torso in a fairly upright position, which should involve you maintaining the natural curvature of your lower back like when you are normally standing upright (rather than excessively arching beyond that position).
   3. Strive to move your rear foot backward in a straight line to keep your feet approximately hip-width apart rather than bringing your rear foot closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
   4. Keep the entire sole of your front foot firmly planted on the ground and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
   5. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
   6. If you are using a cylindrical object to elevate your foot, you can contact the surface with the front of your ankle along with your foot, but only your foot will contact the elevated surface if it is flat, like a bench.
   7. Keep your arms by your sides, directed down toward the ground, as you continue holding the kettlebells throughout the duration of your set.
2. From your split stance position, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until you are able to tap the ground gently with the kneecap of your rear leg.
   1. Keep most of your weight shifted onto your front foot while you are primarily using your rear foot to assist with balance.
   2. Continue keeping your torso fairly upright and the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the ground.
   3. Keep your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
   4. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your rear knee, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
   5. At the end of the lowering phase, your front leg’s thigh should be close to parallel to the ground.
   6. If you initially positioned your front foot excessively far in front of your elevated object, you may reach the end of your available hip range of motion before your rear knee reaches the ground.
   7. If you initially positioned your front foot too close to the elevated object, you may not be able to tap the ground with your rear knee unless you also lift the heel of your front foot up off the ground.
   8. In either case of suboptimal foot placement, you do not need to force your rear knee to tap the ground, and you can adjust how far in front your front foot is situated after finishing your rep.
3. While keeping your front foot firmly planted on the ground and your rear foot in its elevated position, reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knees to lift your body up until you have returned to an upright position in your split stance.
   1. You can finish the ascent with a slight bend in your front knee rather than fully locking it out.
   2. Like during the descent, keep your torso fairly upright and knees aligned with your feet while having most of your weight shifted onto the front foot.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until you have finished your set and are ready to repeat the exercise by switching which leg is in the forward position and which leg is in the elevated position.
   1. After finishing the set with your other leg forward, set the kettlebells down in the same manner in which you initially picked them up (e.g., keeping your lower back near a neutral position rather than simply rounding over at your lower back).

## Description

While statically training your gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, this exercise primarily trains your quads, gluteus maximus, and adductor magnus.
