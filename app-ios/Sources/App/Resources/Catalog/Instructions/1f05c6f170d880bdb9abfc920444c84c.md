## Setting up
1. Add weight plates of your desired load to a barbell situated on the ground.
    1. The size of the weight plates you add to the bar will determine how high the bar lies above the ground, which consequently affects your range of motion. Adding a pair of full diameter plates (like 45lb (20kg) plates or any bumper plates) will elevate the bar to the standard height (about 9in (23cm)) when deadlifting from the floor. If you only use smaller diameter plates, the exercise will function as a deficit stiff-leg deadlift with an extended range of motion that requires greater than average mobility to perform with quality technique. If you do not have access to bumper plates and are using loads that are not heavy enough to utilize 45lb (20kg) plates, you can elevate the bar like you would for a block pull or rack pull and otherwise use the same stiff-leg deadlift technique described by this page.
2. While facing the center of the bar, stand in an approximately hip-width stance with the middle of your feet under the bar and your toes pointing straight ahead or slightly out to the sides.
    1. For most lifters, this situates the bar about one inch (2.5cm) away from the shins.
    2. While using a near hip-width stance places most people into a viable position, the precise stance width that is ideal for you as an individual may be influenced by your body structure and mobility, so you can feel free to modify your stance a bit based on where you feel strong and comfortable.
    3. You can similarly decide whether to point your toes straight ahead or slightly out to the side based on what feels best to you.
3. While only allowing your knees to bend minimally and keeping your hips up, sit your hips backward to incline your torso until your hands are level with the bar.
    1. As you keep your shins vertical, the vast majority of motion should occur from you hinging at your hips, which should result in you feeling a notable stretching sensation in your hamstrings at the bottom. However, you should not keep your knees locked in a completely straight position. A mild degree of knee bend will naturally occur as you focus on sitting your hips backward without intentionally locking your knees.
    2. As a cue for hip hinging, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks while your hands are carrying something.
    3. Without lowering their hips and bending their knees further to turn this exercise into a conventional deadlift, most individuals will have to experience a mild degree of back rounding to reach the bar. Strive to have this modest rounding distributed throughout the entirety of your lower, middle, and upper back rather than only rounding your lower back.
    4. Throughout this exercise, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
4. With your elbows fully straightened, firmly grasp the bar with an approximately shoulder-width overhand grip such that your palms are facing backward.
    1. As an alternative to the double overhand grip, you can naturally use a mixed grip (with one palm facing forward and the other facing backward) or lifting straps with the double overhand grip if you wish to do so.
    2. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
    3. If you situate the bar closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the bar can slide down your hand while holding it, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. Continue bracing your core from this point onward to help you maintain a stable back position.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” into the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
    1. When creating “pre-tension,” pressing through the ground should pull yourself further into position with your back locked into a stable position as the muscles running up the length of your back are fully tensed.
3. While keeping your back tight, drive your hips forward to lift your torso up until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
    1. Avoid letting your back dynamically round when starting to lift the bar. Rather than amplifying the mildly rounded back position you adopted when setting up, your back should straighten toward a neutral position (like when you are normally upright) during this step. Recognizing that your lower back may not be fully flat in the bottom position, it can help to think about keeping your lower back flat and chest up to resist rounding your lower back or collapsing your chest when lifting the bar through your hips.
    2. Rather than letting the bar drift forward, keep it over the middle of your feet throughout the set to move the bar in a straight line.
    3. Lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
    4. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your shoulders when viewed from the side.
4. Reverse the movement by hinging at your hips via sitting your buttocks backward to lower the bar in a controlled manner. Keep your hips up and your shins vertical as you use essentially the same technique during step 3 of the setup.
    1. While you can let your back mildly round while staying tight and chiefly bending your hips, avoid letting your back excessively round in an uncontrolled manner.
    2. There is no need to lower the bar extremely slowly, but you should avoid letting the bar drop into a free fall.
    3. Your knees should only bend by a mild degree, resulting in you feeling a hamstring stretch.
5. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the ground for a moment (rather than simply tapping the ground and immediately deadlifting the bar up again) before continuing to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
    1. While you do not need to pause with the bar on the ground for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferred between reps.
    2. You will be able to brace most effectively when you breathe in between reps while the weight is on the ground rather than during a rep. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.
## Description
While also training your erector spinae, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus.

### **Straight-leg deadlift versus Romanian deadlift versus stiff-leg deadlift**

Just as the terms “straight-leg deadlift” and “stiff-leg deadlift” are commonly used as synonyms of each other, so too are the terms “Romanian deadlift” and “stiff-leg deadlift” often used interchangeably to describe the same exercise by scholarly articles and lifters in general. However, some people consider these names to refer to different hip variations, and this database includes three different exercises to allow you to select whichever specific variation you wish to utilize. Under this usage of the terms, the straight-leg deadlift utilizes a literally straight knee position in contrast to the mild knee bend that is involved with the Romanian or still-leg deadlift from the floor. This conception of the straight-leg deadlift, which has been demonstrated by sources such as Hegyi et al. (2018) along with Piper and Waller (2001), may be utilized less commonly than the other two variations.

Both the straight-leg deadlift and the Romanian deadlift start from the upright position and use a range of motion individualized to someone’s flexibility as the plates often do not contact the floor. If someone has the hamstring flexibility for the plates to contact the floor during either of these variations (which is much rarer with the straight-leg deadlift), a light tap to the ground is utilized. In contrast, the stiff-leg deadlift from the floor involves each rep starting with the plates fully on the floor like a conventional deadlift and results in the full weight of the bar being transferred into the ground at the end of each rep. While the stiff-leg deadlift from the floor does not necessarily require moderate rounding (flexion) of the back (at the lumbar and thoracic spine), people commonly lack the hamstring flexibility to hinge with a flat (near neutral) back until the plates contact the floor. Consequently, somewhat of a rounded back position is often utilized for the stiff-leg deadlift from the floor, while anybody can use flat back position for the Romanian deadlift or straight-leg deadlift because these variations do not require the plates reaching the ground. Relative to the other two variations, the straight-leg deadlift will result in a lower range of motion for most people since the hamstrings are lengthened to a greater with completely straight knees compared to mildly bent knees. Correspondingly, someone can reach maximal hamstring elongation sooner during the descent of a straight-leg deadlift, which limits the degree to which the hips can flex before someone starts the lifting phase of the exercise. Compared to using a mild knee bend, a straight-leg deadlift also constrains how far you can sit your buttock backward and results in the bar drifting farther out in front of your legs during the descent.

All three of these variations are hip hinge exercises that train the same muscle groups, but how different muscles are emphasized may mildly differ depending on the variation.

### **Learning how to hip hinge**

If you have minimal experience training hip hinge exercises such as Romanian deadlift and good morning variations, it is normal to have difficulty performing this movement pattern. Like riding a bicycle or shooting a basketball, hip hinging is a motor skill that may require repeated practice before you feel comfortable consistently using it. Broadly speaking, there are two types of movement substitution patterns that individuals who are in the process of learning how to hip hinge commonly perform when attempting to execute a hip hinge exercise.

One of these is using a stoop lift, which involves dynamically rounding the back (through flexion of the lumbar and thoracic spine) to bend over prior to uncoiling the spine into an upright position during the ascent. This often is accompanied by the buttock not moving particularly far backward and the arms drifting out in front of the legs in contrast to a strict hip hinge, which utilizes a visibly flat back position (with close to a neutral lumbar spine) and has the buttock noticeably moving backward during the descent. In terms of similarity, a stoop lift and a hip hinge both involve only a mild magnitude of knee bend during the descent while keeping the hips high. A clue that you may be performing more of a stoop lift than a hip hinge is feeling a stretching sensation in your back rather than feeling your back muscles remain statically tight throughout your set as these muscles function to maintain a rigid trunk position while you move about your hips. With a properly performed hip hinge, you should certainly feel a stretching sensation in your hamstrings at the bottom of the lift, but you may also feel this stretching sensation during a stoop lift. Nonetheless, a hip hinge can allow you to feel the hamstring stretch earlier during the descent compared to a stoop lift since the hamstrings are shortened by the posterior pelvic tilt (tucking the tailbone under) that is coupled with lumbar spine flexion.

In addition to performing a stoop lift unintentionally when attempting to hip hinge, people can commonly utilize more of a squatting motion that is characterized by greater knee bend and forward tracking of the knees during the descent. Compared to a hip hinge, using more of a squatting movement can result in the buttock moving downward during the descent as opposed to backward, and the shins can also incline forward as opposed to remaining vertical. Because the hamstrings shorten as the knees bend, someone moving in a squat pattern when attempting to perform a hip hinge exercise is unlikely to feel much of a stretching sensation in the hamstrings at the bottom of the lift. Not feeling a hamstring stretch is the most straightforward indicator that you may be squatting your hip hinge exercise, but an additional clue would be if you perceive your quads to be working (e.g., feeling a “burning” sensation or lots of tension) more intensely than your hamstrings during the exercise.

When thinking about moving through hip hinge, stoop lift, and squat patterns, it is important to recognize that these movements can occur along a spectrum. You can perform a hip hinge exercise with a technique that is mostly a hip hinge while still utilizing elements of a stoop lift or a squat that may not be ideal for what you wish to accomplish from the exercise. Similarly, your technique can incrementally progress from being stoop lift-dominant or squat-dominant to becoming progressively more of a hip hinge as your body learns how to move in a different way through repeated practice. If your hip hinge technique currently has much room for improvement, it can be beneficial to start with lighter loads that may not be especially challenging to your muscles until you developed a reasonable level of competency with the movement itself. There is no need to let perfect be the enemy of good, so you do not need to wait to load a movement meaningfully until you have immaculate technique, but it would also be inadvisable to push your body to the limit without initially learning the basic fundamentals of the movement.

Depending on which element of hip hinging in which you currently are challenged, certain external cues can expedite how quickly you can learn how to hinge. When unintentionally moving in a manner that involves a greater degree of squatting or stoop lifting than is intended, a wall may help provide tactile feedback to cue sitting your buttock backward. To do so, you can perform your hip hinge with your back facing a nearby wall, and you will be able to receive confirmation as to whether you are actually sitting your hips backward by feeling the wall contact your buttock at the end of the descent. As an initial starting point, you can set up with a heel-to-toe tandem stance as the back of one heel contacts the wall. Then you can bring your rear foot forward to be aligned with your front foot in the symmetrical stance you will utilize for your hip hinge. This can provide a reasonable approximation as to how far people may commonly be able to sit their hips back when hinging, but you can adjust from there how far away you position yourself from the wall to individualize it to you.

Specifically if you have a tendency to perform a “squatty” hip hinge, practicing your hip hinge with your calves up against something like a flat exercise bench (or even a couch or chair at home) can be a rather productive means of honing your hinge. As you descend, you will receive immediate tactile feedback if your knees track forward from no longer feeling the contact with your calves, so it can be a great way to reinforce keeping your shins vertical throughout the movement. When using this method, make sure that you maintain full contact between the soles of your feet and the ground while you avoid leaning your bodyweight into the bench to the extent that you would fall backward if the bench was removed. If you successfully keep your calves in contact with the bench throughout the movement and feel a pronounced stretching sensation in your hamstrings at the end of the descent, you are right on track to achieving a high quality hip hinge.

As an additional learning tool, you can situate a long object like a dowel rod, PVC pipe, or yardstick on your back while hip hinging if you have a tendency to utilize more of a stoop lift technique with excessive back rounding. Either the calf-to-bench technique or the buttock-to-wall technique can be incorporated into an actual Romanian deadlift where you use weights, but using a rod as a cue without a partner will require you to hold the rod in place, so you would not be able to load this drill independently. To perform this movement, you would hold the rod behind against your back with one hand higher up near your head and your other hand lower down near your buttock. While standing fully upright and looking straight ahead, you should feel the rod contacting your sacrum (the bone that lies between your upper buttock cheeks and right above your tailbone), upper back between your shoulder blades, and the back of your head. You will then hip hinge while maintaining these three points of contact throughout the entirety of the movement. If you start rounding your back or moving your neck beyond the neutral position you adopt when standing fully upright, you will feel the rod lose contact with one of those three spots. This can be a rather effective form of instant feedback to help develop your ability to move predominantly through your lower body while keeping your trunk in a rigid position throughout the movement, which can transfer to improved technique for an array of different exercises. When maintaining those three contact points, you potentially can still perform a squatting motion rather than a hip hinge since moving through your lower body while minimizing back motion is also a component of squatting technique. However, if you maintain those three contact points and additionally feel your hamstrings notably stretched at the bottom of the descent, those two factors indicate that you have successfully hip hinged in all likelihood. If you find that the rod cuing shifts your movement from a stoop lift to more of a squat, you can use the rod cuing simultaneously with the calf-to-bench cuing, which can address multiple aspects of the hip hinge concurrently.

In the context of performing a hip hinge exercise, using a form that resembles more of a squat or stoop lift rather than a hip hinge can be considered technique errors, but squatting and stoop lifting are by no means faulty movement patterns inherently. They simply differ from a hip hinge, so they can be suboptimal means of performing an exercise that you intend to train specifically for the benefits that hip hinging can provide as opposed to other movements. The hip hinge can take some practice to learn, but investing the time and effort into doing so can be a prudent investment given that having the motor skill of hip hinging can meaningfully expand your freedom in how you can move both inside and outside of the gym.

### **Straps**

If you find that your grip strength functions as the limiting factor as opposed to your lower body or lower back strength when performing this exercise, you can utilize chalk or straps in an effort to amplify the stimulus each set provides your lower body and back muscles. Among trained lifters, grip strength can very commonly limit performance of Romanian deadlifts if they do not utilize straps since the posterior chain has the potential to move rather heavy loads for this exercise. As a simple approach you can use if you are unsure whether grip strength is limiting your performance, you can perform a set of as many quality reps as you can with a particular weight without using straps and compare your rep total to a set using straps. To perform an appropriate comparison, you would want to ensure that you are using the same technique for each condition, which will mean using the same range of motion and tempo. Additionally, you would want to perform each condition when you are similarly recovered. Practically, resting at least a few minutes between the two sets should allow most fatigue to dissipate by the time you perform the second set, therefore enabling a useful comparison in terms of how performance of this exercise may be affected by your grip strength. However, if you really wish to optimize the validity of your self-assessment, you can compare your rep performance between these conditions on a second day where you reverse the order of the conditions. If you performed the set without straps prior to the set using straps on the first day, you perform the set using straps before the set without straps on a different day to control for how any fatigue resulting from the first set may influence the second set. When comparing how many reps you complete during each condition, you will be well informed as to how grip strength may be affecting performance of this exercise for you as an individual.
