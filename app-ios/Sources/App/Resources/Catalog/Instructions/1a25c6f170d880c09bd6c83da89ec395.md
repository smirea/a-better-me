## Setting up

To perform this exercise, you naturally have to position a bar on your back and have sufficient space available for you without any objects or people getting in your way. With a heavy load, it will be easiest to initially set up an Olympic barbell at mid-chest height in a squat stand or the outside of a power rack. However, many gyms will not have the necessary space to perform walking lunges starting from a squat stand/power rack. Alternatively, you can reverse curl or power clean a bar (whether that be an Olympic bar with weight plates or a fixed-weight straight bar) to your shoulders, press it up overhead, and lower it onto your upper back. With a fixed-weight straight bar, you will not require as much space to perform this exercise, but you will be limited in terms of your load potential. Regardless of how you set up, the exercise technique will be the same, but the different set-up methods are described in separate sections based on which one you wish to select.

### **Setting up with a fixed-weight straight bar**

1. Stand upright with a fixed-weight straight bar while holding it with straight elbows and a mildly outside shoulder-width overhand grip, such that your palms are directed toward you.
2. Walk over with the bar to the spot where you could begin your walking lunges.
3. While keeping your elbows straight, sit your buttocks back while allowing your knees to bend mildly and your torso to incline slightly forward.
4. As you simultaneously drive your hips forcefully forward to generate momentum from your lower body, curl the bar upward in an arcing manner by bending at your elbows while allowing your elbows to swing a bit forward.
   1. The hip drive motion, which is similar to the lockout of a deadlift, will bring your torso into a vertical position with your knees straightened.
5. Tuck your chin back to move your head behind the bar.
6. Press the bar upward with as close to a straight bar path as you can perform.
   1. If you wish to do so, you can press the bar overhead with a push press technique, where you use leg drive.
7. Immediately after the bar clears the top of your head, mildly push your head forward until your ears are close to being under the bar.
8. Finish the press with your elbows straightened as the bar is overhead.
9. Lower the bar behind your head onto your upper back muscles slightly below the base of your neck in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
   1. Aim for the bar to rest on the muscular shelf formed by your traps above your rear delts on the back of your shoulders and above the bony ridge running across your shoulder blades (known as the scapular spine). This is the high bar position, which is generally recommended over the low bar position for walking lunges.
   2. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable on your traps will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back as you squeeze your shoulder blades back together.
   3. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back and should situate the bar lower on your traps.
   4. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.

### **Setting up an Olympic barbell from a rack**

1. Adjust the J-hooks of a power rack or squat stand to a few finger widths below the height of your collarbone.
   1. If you are using a power rack, make sure that the J-hooks are on the outside of the power rack so that you can walk out with the bar.
2. Place the barbell on top of the J-hooks, and add plates of your desired load.
3. While facing the direction in which you will walk out, stand in front of the center of the bar with a near hip-width stance.
4. Grasp each side of the bar with a somewhat outside shoulder-width grip that feels comfortable to you.
   1. The proper grip width will vary from person to person, and what feels best to you will be influenced by your shoulder mobility.
5. While holding onto the bar, shallowly bend at your hips and knees in a quarter squat-like manner to then walk under the bar.
6. Situate the bar on your upper back muscles slightly below the base of your neck in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
   1. Aim for the bar to rest on the muscular shelf formed by your traps above your rear delts on the back of your shoulders and above the bony ridge running across your shoulder blades (known as the scapular spine). This is the high bar position, which is generally recommended over the low bar position for walking lunges.
   2. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable on your traps will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back as you squeeze your shoulder blades back together.
   3. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back and should situate the bar lower on your traps.
   4. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.
   5. While positioning the bar on your upper back, you may have to readjust where you are grasping the bar if your shoulders, elbows, or wrists feel uncomfortable with your initial placement.
7. Pull down on the bar to keep it positioned on your upper back, and stand up in a quarter squat-like manner by extending (straightening) your knees and hips.
8. Walk out with the bar on your back until you have reached the space where you will perform your walking lunges.

### **Setting up an Olympic barbell from the floor**

1. Position an Olympic barbell in the spot where you could begin your walking lunges, and add weight plates of your desired load to the bar.
2. Sit your hips backward while allowing your knees to bend until your hands are level with the bar.
   1. Throughout the set-up, keep your back flat and chest up rather than letting your lower back round or chest collapse.
3. With your elbows straightened, grasp the bar with a mildly outside shoulder-width overhand grip, such that your palms are directed toward you.
4. If you know how to clean (whether that be a power clean or full clean the bar), clean it into the front rack position and skip to step 8. Otherwise, follow steps 5-7 to deadlift the bar and perform a reverse cheat curl.
5. Drive through your feet, and thrust your hips forward to stand upright with the bar in a deadlift motion.
6. While keeping your elbows straight, sit your buttocks back while allowing your knees to bend mildly and your torso to incline slightly forward.
7. As you simultaneously drive your hips forcefully forward to generate momentum from your lower body, curl the bar upward in an arcing manner by bending at your elbows while allowing your elbows to swing a bit forward.
   1. The hip drive motion, which is similar to the lockout of a deadlift, will bring your torso into a vertical position with your knees straightened.
8. Tuck your chin back to move your head behind the bar.
9. Press the bar upward with as close to a straight bar path as you can perform.
   1. If you wish to do so, you can press the bar overhead with a push press technique, where you use leg drive.
10. Immediately after the bar clears the top of your head, mildly push your head forward until your ears are close to being under the bar.
11. Finish the press with your elbows straightened as the bar is overhead.
12. Lower the bar behind your head onto your upper back muscles slightly below the base of your neck in a position that feels comfortable and stable for you.
    1. Aim for the bar to rest on the muscular shelf formed by your traps above your rear delts on the back of your shoulders and above the bony ridge running across your shoulder blades (known as the scapular spine). This is the high bar position, which is generally recommended over the low bar position for walking lunges.
    2. Where precisely the bar feels most comfortable and stable on your traps will vary among different individuals, but the bar should feel like it is securely resting on the muscles of your upper back as you squeeze your shoulder blades back together.
    3. If you feel like the bar is placing pressure on your neck, you most likely have the bar placed too high on your back and should situate the bar lower on your traps.
    4. In particular, you should avoid placing the bar excessively high to the extent that the weight is pressing on any of the bony prominences at the base of your neck.

## Exercise technique

1. While keeping one foot on the ground, take a fairly long step straight forward with your other foot far enough ahead that you will be able to contact the ground with your rear knee when lunging down.
   1. Like when you step forward while walking, you can initially contact the ground with your front foot’s heel prior to lowering the entirety of your sole onto the ground in a fluid motion, which will likely occur naturally without having to think about it.
   2. When your front foot contacts the ground, you can allow your rear leg’s heel to start lifting up off the ground, which is also likely to occur naturally.
   3. Keep your torso in an upright position and the top of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
   4. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
   5. Strive to step forward in a straight line rather than stepping closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
   6. If you are new to walking lunges, you may have to try out different step lengths to determine which step length places you in the appropriate position.
2. After contacting the ground with your front foot, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until you are able to tap the ground gently with the kneecap of your rear leg.
   1. Keep your rear leg’s forefoot (ball of your foot and bottom of your toes) and the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot’s sole in contact with the ground.
   2. Continue keeping your torso upright and your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
   3. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your rear knee, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
   4. At the end of the lowering phase, your front leg’s thigh should be close to parallel to the ground.
   5. Perform steps 1 and 2 as a smooth, continuous movement.
   6. If you initially took an excessively short step forward, you may not be able to tap the ground with your rear knee unless you also lift the heel of your front foot up off the ground.
   7. If you find that you have to lift your front heel up to tap the ground, simply descend as much as you can without lifting up your front heel, and take a longer step forward during your next rep.
3. Progressively extend (straighten) your knees to lift your body up until you can repeat step 1 to step forward with your rear leg past your stationary leg in the same manner that you initially performed with your other side.
   1. Strive to begin stepping forward as you are lunging up in a fluid manner, which will advance your body forward.
   2. Like during the descent, keep your torso upright and knees aligned with your feet while doing so.
4. Repeat step 2 with your other side now in the forward position, as you lunge down just like you previously did.
5. Continue repeating steps 1-4 as you progressively lunge forward with alternating legs until you have completed your set or have to turn around and continue lunging in the opposite direction.
   1. After finishing the last rep, set the weight down in the opposite manner to how you initially set up.
   2. You can press the weight up overhead, lower it to the front of your shoulders, and then lower it to the front of your thighs, or you can rerack the bar in a rack (if you initially set up in a rack).

## Description

This exercise primarily trains your quads, gluteus maximus, and adductor magnus.
