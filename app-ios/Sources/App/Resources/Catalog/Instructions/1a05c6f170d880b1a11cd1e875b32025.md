## Setting up

1. Determine whether you will set up to use a power rack, which could be modified to perform a variety of barbell lifts and would require you to position an incline bench within the rack, or within a specific incline press station in which the bench is fused to the rack.
   1. For this variation, you will use a bench with a moderate incline near 45 degrees. Any bench angle (which includes most incline press stations) that is greater than 30 degrees but less than 60 degrees can be considered a moderate incline.
   2. Most power racks allow you to position J-hooks (on which the barbell rests before being unracked) and safety pins at incrementally different heights, respectively, to help you situate the bar close to your ideal starting position and to receive the bar safely if you fail a rep.
   3. Incline bench press stations typically lack spotter arms (that function just like safety pins by receiving the bar if you fail a rep) and have multiple pairs of fixed height J-hooks. While most people can find a J-hook height that allows them to incline press effectively, some people will notice that their ideal starting position of the bar cannot be achieved with the available J-hook heights, which can make it more difficult to unrack the bar independently.
   4. If you have access to a power rack with safety pins and an incline press station without spotter arms, bench pressing within the power rack is recommended for its additional safety mechanism. The con of using a power rack is that it normally takes longer to prepare compared to an incline press station. It is recommended to have a spotter behind you if you will be incline pressing without safety pins.
2. If you are using a power rack, place the moderate incline bench centered within the power rack. The head of the bench should be between the posts, where you will adjust the J-hooks.
   1. You should position the bench in a spot that allows your nose to be directly under the barbell after setting up the rack and lying down on the bench.
3. With a power rack, position the J-hooks to a height that initially allows you to grasp the bar with slightly bent elbows once you are set up on the incline bench, which will allow you to unrack the bar efficiently. With an incline press station, find which J-hook height places you closest to this position.
   1. After fully straightening your elbows to lift the bar off the J-hooks, the bar should ideally be close to 2in (5cm) above the top of the J-hooks while keeping your shoulder blades tucked back together.
   2. The proper height easily enables you to avoid bumping the J-hooks when unracking the bar while simultaneously not requiring much effort to lift the bar initially.
   3. If the J-hooks are too high, you may have to spread your shoulder blades apart in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks when unracking, which can place you in a less stable position to start your set, since it can be challenging to reposition your shoulder blades after unracking the bar.
   4. If the J-hooks are too low, the unrack will involve a greater-than-ideal pressing range of motion, which will require greater effort and generate more fatigue before your set begins.
   5. If neither of the two closest J-hook heights is ideal, it is generally better to use the height that is a little too low, since being able to start the set with optimal positioning is typically more advantageous than the alternative.
   6. Some trial and error is often needed to find the best J-hook height for you.
4. Place the bar on top of the appropriate J-hooks. If you are training in a power rack with safety pins, use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of incline press reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins prior to re-racking the bar onto the J-hooks.
   1. If you are training in an incline press station without spotter arms, proceed to step 6.
5. If you are training in a power rack, position the safety pins slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of the lowering phase of your incline press.
   1. If you happen to fail a rep without a spotter, you could flatten out your back to lose your arch, which will lower your chest and ideally allow the bar to rest on the safety pins.
   2. Certain power rack models have fairly wide spaces between safety pinholes that may result in the highest setting that does not restrict your incline press range of motion being too low to receive the bar after you flatten your back. It is not ideal if this applies to your rack, but the safety pins can still enhance safety if they are high enough to receive the bar if you dump it forward in the event that you become completely pinned without anyone to assist you.
6. Load the bar with your intended weight, and lie down with your back and head on the incline bench. Position yourself so that your nose is close to being directly underneath the barbell, and your knees are bent with your feet on the floor, approximately shoulder-width apart.
   1. Firmly situate the soles of your feet flat on the ground.
   2. Based on what feels most stable, you can position your heels directly under your knees with vertical shins, or you can bend your knees a bit beyond this point to position your heels slightly behind your knees with slightly angled shins.
   3. Similarly, you can decide whether you point your toes moderately out to the sides or straight ahead based on your preference.
   4. If you are not training with safety pins that can be positioned to the ideal height, you can avoid adding a pair of collars to the end of the bar. This would allow you to dump the weight to one side and allow the weights to slide off the bar if you become pinned underneath the bar after failing a rep. The con of not using collars is that the plates can accidentally slide to one side when pressing if you happen to tilt the bar asymmetrically, but it can be an acceptable tradeoff to make sure that you can avoid being pinned if you fail a rep.
7. Firmly grasp the bar with your palms facing forward (resulting in an overhand grip) with your hands well outside shoulder width. Let the bar sit on top of the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) with reasonably straight wrists.
   1. A wide grip width for bench press variations is commonly considered to be around two times your shoulder width, with a bit of leeway both narrower and wider. Feel free to try out different hand widths over time to determine where you feel best.
   2. What is personally considered a wide grip to you can also be influenced by the specific grip width you use for your standard incline press, which you can somewhat widen to individualize your wide grip. For instance, for your wide grip, you can place your index finger on the bar where you would otherwise place your little finger when performing your standard incline press.
   3. For stability, wrap your thumbs around the bar, resulting in the bar being fully enclosed by your hands. If you’re just learning this exercise, avoid positioning your thumbs on the same side of the bar as your other fingers (which is known as a thumbless grip, or a “suicide grip”).
   4. While your wrists do not need to be completely straight, you should avoid grasping the bar with your wrists cocked back into an excessively extended position that may contribute to an increased risk of experiencing wrist discomfort. Simply squeezing the bar as hard as possible will usually keep your wrists from cocking back excessively.
   5. After grasping the bar in a manner that allows you to feel comfortable and stable, you will maintain this hand and wrist position throughout your set.
8. While allowing your lower back to arch naturally, assume a “proud chest” posture that puffs your chest slightly up toward the ceiling by tightly pulling your shoulder blades back together. Maintain this chest position throughout your set.
   1. As a cue for pulling your shoulder blades back, you can think about trying to squeeze a pencil between your shoulder blades. This should prompt you to feel your upper back muscles contracting like at the end range of a rowing exercise.
   2. Focusing on adopting a “proud chest” position can naturally place your lower back into a mildly arched position, which is sufficient for optimizing muscle growth and general strength development.

## Exercise technique

1. Drive your feet into the ground, squeeze your glutes, and brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing in this manner throughout your set. This will help create full-body tension and allow your body to function as a stable platform from which to press.
   2. From this starting position, your feet should be firmly dug into the ground, while your buttocks, upper back, and head are firmly planted on the bench. You will maintain these five points of contact throughout your whole set to maximize your stability and performance.
2. Press the bar off the J-hooks by straightening your elbows, and move the bar forward away from the J-hooks until it is approximately above your shoulders, where you will hold it for a moment before starting to lower the bar.
   1. Focus on keeping your shoulder blades pulled back together to avoid losing your upper back tightness.
3. Lower the bar toward your upper chest in a controlled manner by progressively bending your elbows and allowing them naturally to flare out away from each other. Descend until you are able to tap your upper chest lightly with the bar or until you have fully moved through your available shoulder range of motion.
   1. It is normal to feel a stretching sensation in the chest and the front of the shoulders at the bottom, but certain individuals with limited shoulder mobility may not be able to touch their upper chest with the bar without discomfort. If this applies to you, lower the bar as far as you can without discomfort.
   2. While motion simultaneously occurs at your shoulders and elbows, strive to keep your elbows stacked underneath (when viewed from the side) your mostly straight wrists. Avoid letting your elbows track meaningfully in front of your wrists (closer to your hips) or behind them.
   3. Compared to a closer grip incline press, your elbows should flare out further away from your sides during a wide grip incline press, but you should not fully flare them out.
   4. The appropriate degree of elbow flare can naturally occur when keeping your elbows stacked under your wrists. Near the bottom of the rep, the appropriate amount of elbow flare should result in your upper arms forming reasonably close to 75-degree angles with your torso (when 0 degrees means your upper arms are touching your sides and 90 degrees means your upper arms form a “T-shape” with your torso).
   5. Make sure to control the entire lowering phase before gently contacting your chest with the bar, since you will not be bouncing the bar off your chest.
4. Reverse the movement by pressing the bar up. It may help to focus on driving your elbows upward and inward.
   1. You should finish with your elbows fully straightened and the bar situated above your shoulders.
   2. Continue driving your feet into the ground while keeping your buttocks firmly planted on the bench, and your shoulder blades pulled back together.
5. Alternate between steps 3 and 4 until you have completed your set and are ready to rerack the bar.
6. After completing the lifting phase of your last rep, keep the bar motionless over your shoulders for about one second before moving the bar back until it contacts the back of both J-hooks.
7. Bend your elbows slightly to lower the bar onto the J-hooks to finish reracking the weight.
   1. Only begin lowering the bar after you have already contacted the back of the J-hooks.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop your front delts and pec major with an emphasis on the upper pec while also training the middle and lower pec major, along with the triceps.
