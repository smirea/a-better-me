## Setting up

1. Determine whether you will set up to bench press within a power rack, which could be modified to perform a variety of barbell lifts and would require you to position a mobile flat bench within the rack, or within a specific bench press station in which the bench is fused to the rack.
   1. Most power racks allow you to position J-hooks (on which the barbell rests before being unracked) and safety pins at incrementally different heights, respectively, to help you situate the bar close to your ideal starting position and to receive the bar safely if you fail a rep.
   2. Bench press stations may also have spotter arms (that function just like safety pins by receiving the bar if you fail a rep) and J-hooks that can be adjusted in height, but a variety of models lack spotter arms and have multiple pairs of fixed height J-hooks. While most people can find a J-hook height that allows them to bench press effectively, some people will notice that their ideal starting position of the bar cannot be achieved with the available J-hook heights, which can make it difficult to unrack the bar independently.
   3. If you have access to a power rack with safety pins and a bench press station without spotter arms, bench pressing within the power rack is recommended for its additional safety mechanism. The con of using a power rack is that it typically takes longer to prepare compared to a specific bench press station.
2. With a power rack, position the J-hooks to a height that initially allows you to grasp the bar with slightly bent elbows once you are set up on the bench, which will allow you to unrack the bar efficiently. With a bench press station, you will find which J-hook height places you closest to this position.
   1. After fully straightening your elbows to lift the bar off the J-hooks, the bar should ideally be close to 2in (5cm) above the top of the J-hooks while keeping your shoulder blades tucked back together.
   2. The proper height easily enables you to avoid bumping the J-hooks when unracking the bar while simultaneously not requiring much effort to lift the bar initially.
   3. If the J-hooks are too high, you may have to spread your shoulder blades apart in order for the bar to clear the J-hooks when unracking, which can place you in a less stable position to start your set, since it can be challenging to reposition your shoulder blades after unracking the bar.
   4. If the J-hooks are too low, the unrack will involve a greater-than-ideal pressing range of motion, which will require greater effort and generate more fatigue before your set begins.
   5. If neither of the two closest J-hook heights is ideal, it is generally better to use the height that is a little too low, since being able to start the set with optimal positioning is typically more advantageous than the alternative.
   6. Some trial and error is often needed to find the best J-hook height for you.
3. After situating a bar on top of the appropriate J-hooks, use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of bench press reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins/spotter arms prior to re-racking the bar onto the J-hooks.
4. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins/spotter arms to be slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of the lowering phase of your bench press.
   1. If you happen to fail a rep without a spotter, you could flatten out your back to lose your arch, which will lower your chest and ideally allow the bar to rest on the safety pins/spotter arms.
   2. Certain power rack and bench press station models have fairly wide spaces between safety pin/spotter arm holes that may result in the highest setting that does not restrict your bench press range of motion being too low to receive the bar after you flatten your back. If this applies to your rack, it is not ideal, but the safety pins/spotter arms can still enhance safety if they are high enough to stop the bar from reaching your neck.
   3. If you are not training with safety pins/spotter arms that can be positioned to the ideal height, you can avoid adding a pair of collars to the end of the bar. This would allow you to dump the weight to one side and allow the weights to slide off the bar if you become pinned underneath the bar after failing a rep. The con of not using collars is that the plates can accidentally slide to one side when pressing if you happen to tilt the bar asymmetrically, but it can be an acceptable tradeoff to make sure that you can avoid being pinned if you fail a rep.
5. Lie down with your back on the bench as your mouth is close to being directly underneath the barbell, and your knees are bent with your feet on the floor approximately shoulder width apart from each other.
   1. Based on what feels most stable, you can position the soles of your feet flat on the ground with your shins close to being vertical, or you can bend your knees beyond this point to dig the balls of your feet and toes into the ground with your heels off the ground.
   2. You can similarly decide whether you point your toes moderately out to the sides or straight ahead based on your preference.
6. Firmly grasp the bar with your palms facing forward (resulting in an overhand grip) as your hands are well outside shoulder width apart from each other, with the bar on top of the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) with reasonably straight wrists.
   1. A wide grip width for the bench press is commonly considered to be near twice your shoulder width, and this involves somewhat of a range of grip widths, so you can feel free to try out mildly different hand widths over time to determine where you feel best.
   2. What is personally considered a wide grip to you can also be influenced by the specific grip width you use for your standard bench press, which you can somewhat widen to individualize your wide grip. For instance, for your wide grip, you can place your index finger on the bar where you would otherwise place your little finger when performing your standard bench press.
   3. For stability, your thumbs should be wrapped around the bar from the opposite side of your other fingers, resulting in the bar being fully enclosed by your hands in contrast to if you positioned your thumbs on the same side of your other fingers (which is known as a thumbless grip that is also called a “suicide grip”).
   4. While your wrists do not need to be completely straight, you should avoid grasping the bar with your wrists meaningfully cocked back into an excessively extended position that may contribute to an increased risk of experiencing wrist discomfort.
   5. After grasping the bar in a manner that allows you to feel comfortable and stable, you will maintain this hand and wrist position throughout your set.
7. While allowing your lower back to arch naturally, assume a “proud chest” posture that puffs your chest slightly up toward the ceiling by tightly pulling your shoulder blades back together, which is a position you will maintain throughout your set.
   1. As a cue for pulling your shoulder blades back, you can think about trying to squeeze a pencil between your shoulder blades, which should prompt you to feel your upper back muscles contracting, like at the end range of a rowing exercise.
   2. Focusing on adopting a “proud chest” position can naturally place your lower back into a mildly arched position, which is sufficient for optimizing muscle growth and general strength development. However, if you are specifically training to bench press as much weight as possible, you can optionally exaggerate your lower back arch, which may allow you to shorten the range of motion a bit and potentially lift heavier weights.

## Exercise technique

1. Drive your feet into the ground, squeeze your glutes, and brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. You will continue doing so throughout your set, which will help create full-body tension and allow your body to function as a stable platform from which to press.
   2. From this starting position, your feet should be firmly dug into the ground, while your buttocks, upper back, and head are firmly planted on the bench. You will maintain these five points of contact throughout your whole set to maximize your stability and performance.
2. Press the bar off the J-hooks by straightening your elbows, and move the bar forward away from the J-hooks until it is approximately above your shoulders, where you will hold it for a moment before starting to lower the bar.
   1. Focus on keeping your shoulder blades pulled back together as you avoid losing your upper back tightness.
3. Lower the bar toward your lower chest in a controlled manner by progressively bending your elbows and allowing them naturally to flare out away from each other while descending until you are able to tap your lower chest lightly with the bar.
   1. While motion simultaneously occurs at your shoulders and elbows, strive to keep your elbows stacked underneath your mostly straight wrists rather than letting your elbows track in front of your wrists (closer to your hips) or behind them. Maintaining this alignment as you touch the bar to your lower chest will promote the appropriate degree of elbow flare.
   2. Near the bottom of the rep, the appropriate amount of elbow flare should result in your upper arms forming reasonably close to 75-degree angles with your torso (when 0 degrees means your upper arms are touching your sides and 90 degrees means your upper arms form a “T-shape” with your torso).
   3. Compared to a closer grip bench press, your elbows should flare out further away from your sides during a wide grip bench press.
   4. When aiming to touch the bar to your lower chest, the bar will track slightly forward toward your abdomen rather than moving exclusively in a vertical line.
   5. Make sure to control the entire lowering phase before gently contacting your chest with the bar, since you will not be bouncing the bar off your chest.
4. Once the bar contacts your chest, pause in that bottom position as you remain completely stationary for a brief period of time (e.g., 1-2 seconds) while staying tight.
   1. Avoid rushing your pause to the best of your ability.
5. Reverse the movement by pressing the bar up as you progressively drive your elbows upward and inward while straightening them until you have returned to the starting position with your elbows fully straightened as the bar is situated above your shoulders.
   1. To finish with the bar over your shoulders instead of your lower chest, the bar will now arc slightly backward as you lift it up.
   2. Continue driving your feet into the ground while keeping your buttocks firmly planted on the bench, and your shoulder blades pulled back together.
6. Alternate between steps 3-5 until you have completed your set and are ready to rerack the bar.
7. After completing the lifting phase of your last rep, keep the bar motionless over your shoulders for about one second before moving the bar back until it contacts the back of both J-hooks.
8. Bend your elbows mildly to lower the bar onto the J-hooks to finish reracking the weight.
   1. Only begin lowering the bar after you have already contacted the back of the J-hooks.

## Description

This exercise primarily trains your entire pec major, which is assisted by your front delt and triceps.
