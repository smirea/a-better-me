## Setting up
1. Place a trap bar in a location where you have sufficient space to carry it, and position its high handles up if your bar has high handles.
    1. The setup for this exercise mirrors that of a trap bar deadlift, with the lifting phase of one trap bar deadlift rep.
    2. You can certainly use a trap bar without elevated handles, but it is easier to lift into the starting position when using high handles.
2. Add weight plates to the bar, and stand within the middle of the bar with a near hip-width stance and your arms at your sides.
3. Hinge at your hips while bending your knees until your hands are level with the handles when your elbows are straight, and your arms are lifted slightly out to your sides (to position your hands directly over the handles).
    1. Throughout this exercise, keep your lower back flat and chest up, rather than meaningfully rounding your back.
4. Firmly grasp the center of the handles with your palms facing in toward each other.
    1. Keep your elbows straight throughout the exercise.
5. While keeping your elbows straight, press your legs into the ground with enough force to feel the trap bar’s sleeves push up into the weight plates, but not enough force to lift the bar off the ground.
    1. Applying this pre-tension to the bar will likely produce an audible “clinking” sound.
    2. The initial submaximal pull allows you to check if you have grasped the handles correctly, which will be indicated by the handles not rotating forward or backward when applying the pre-tension.
    3. If you pick up a trap bar with your hands positioned slightly away from the middle of the handles, the trap bar can start rotating in a manner that makes it very difficult to hold and lift appropriately.
    4. Even for experienced lifters, it can be easy to grasp a trap bar’s handles without having their hands truly centered initially, so applying the pre-tension can make sure you are in your ideal starting position.
    5. If the bar starts rotating when you apply the pre-tension, re-center your grip, and apply the pre-tension again until the bar no longer rotates.
6. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
7. Drive through the ground to lift the bar upward as you extend (straighten) your hips and knees until you are standing fully upright.
    1. Continue keeping your back flat as you lift the bar with your hip and thigh muscles.
## Exercise technique
1. Walk forward while holding the trap bar while maintaining an upright posture with your shoulders approximately level with each other.
    1. While you will not swing your arms like you would when walking normally, your torso and lower body should look similar to how they would when you walk normally.
    2. Walk at a pace where you feel stable and balanced throughout the carry.
    3. If you use a weight that is fairly heavy to you, it is natural to take shorter strides compared to how you typically walk.
    4. Keep the tops of your hip bones approximately level throughout your set rather than letting them drop down to one side when taking each step.
2. Continue walking with the trap bar until you have completed your set and are ready to set it down by reversing the movement you used to pick it up initially.
    1. Remember to lower the weight by hinging at your hips while bending your knees and keeping your lower back near a neutral position.
## Description
Rather than concentrating the workload on one body region, this exercise trains a variety of muscle groups throughout your body in an integrated manner, including the muscles in your forearms, upper back, core, and lower body.

### **Straps for Carries**

Even when using chalk, the weight you can use or distance you can complete with a particular weight will commonly be limited by grip strength while performing a farmer’s carry, so this carry can be a viable means of training static grip strength. However, if you wish to increase the stimulus provided to muscles besides the forearm and hand muscles, you have the option of performing this carry with lifting straps. With the heavier loads or longer distances that straps typically facilitate, the exercise can be more challenging to your upper back and core muscles as they support your trunk and shoulder girdle in addition to your lower body muscles that stabilize your pelvis and propel you forward.

### **Training Many Muscles Simultaneously**

McGill et al. (2009) measured EMG activity of several core and lower body muscles as competitive strongmen performed a variety of movements that appear in strongman competitions, including the farmer’s walk, which was performed for several steps with a 75kg (167.6lb) weight in each hand. During the farmer’s walk, peak EMG activity was high for the internal abdominal obliques, lats, thoracic erector spinae, lumbar erector spinae, gluteus medius, gluteus maximus, and rectus femoris. The external abdominal obliques and biceps femoris also exhibited moderate peak EMG activity, while the rectus abdominis exhibited low EMG activity. The abdominal muscles and rectus femoris demonstrated their peak activity during the walking portion of the carry, while the lats, erector spinae and biceps femoris demonstrated their peak activity during the initial lifting phase. The gluteal muscles exhibited peak activity during the initial lifting phase and during the first step of the carry. As indicated by these findings, the farmer’s carry can train a large amount of musculature simultaneously, which can result in this exercise being metabolically demanding when performed at a high relative intensity, especially for longer duration sets. Correspondingly, challenging sets of farmer’s carries may be able to train your cardiovascular system more robustly than most resistance training exercises that localize the workload to a smaller amount of total muscle mass.
