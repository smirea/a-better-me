## Setting up

1. Determine what you hold with your hands for balance assistance.
   1. So long as what you use for assistance is sturdy and will be within your reach to hold throughout the exercise, you have multiple options for what you can select.
   2. With two hands, you can hold objects like the vertical posts of a rack, a door frame, anchored suspension trainer straps, or gymnastic rings.
   3. If you feel that you only require one hand for assistance, you can also use those objects in addition to a variety of others, such as a bedpost, couch, dumbbell rack, incline exercise bench, part of an exercise machine, etc.
2. Stand upright in a hip-width stance with your toes pointing straight ahead as you are close to what you hold onto to provide assistance with your arms. Grasp that object with one or two hands.
   1. Ensure that there is sufficient room for you to lift one of your legs forward and that you will be able to continue holding the object at the bottom of your rep, along with the top of it.

## Exercise technique

1. Shift your weight into the foot on the side of your body you will train. Simultaneously pick your other foot up off the ground by lifting that thigh up until it is parallel to the ground with your knee as straight as your hamstring flexibility permits.
   1. Throughout the set, keep this leg close to parallel to the ground without letting its foot touch the ground.
   2. All the subsequent directions will apply to the leg whose foot is on the ground.
2. Bend at your hip and knee to squat down in a controlled manner while assisting with your arms as needed.
   1. Throughout the rep, you can adjust how much assistance you use from your upper body, which will likely need to be greater near the bottom of the rep (where the exercise is more challenging) compared to near the top of the rep. Some people will simply use their hands to assist with balance, while others may need to utilize greater upper body involvement to reduce the demands on their lower body muscles.
   2. Keep the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your foot firmly in contact with the ground throughout your set.
   3. Strive to have your knee track straight forward in line with your second and third toes (when counting your big toe as your first toe) as you descend, while not letting your knee cave inward.
   4. To the best of your ability, keep the tops of your hip bones level with each other rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your free leg drop down. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
   5. Aim to keep your torso mostly upright within the constraints of your mobility and balance. The vast majority of people can only move through a full range of motion during this exercise if they let their backs round during the descent.
   6. Through motion at your working leg’s hip, your torso should naturally transition to an inclined position rather than remaining completely upright, but strive to keep your back near a neutral position (where it retains its natural curvature or looks flat) as you avoid excessively rounding your lower back.
3. Continue to squat down through the full range of motion that your mobility comfortably enables without letting your heel lift up off the ground or your knee cave inward.
   1. The greatest possible depth involves descending until your knee is fully bent with your hamstrings contacting your calves. If you have ankle mobility limitations, you will not be able to reach this point, but training this exercise over time can help improve your depth.
   2. If you start experiencing knee discomfort in the deepest position, you can abbreviate the range of motion to what feels right to your knee. This exercise is not inherently dangerous to the knees, but it can take some time to adapt to very deep pistol squats if you are unaccustomed to exercises that concentrate a high degree of loading into a fully bent knee position.
   3. While an extremely slow descent is unnecessary, controlling the entirety of the lowering phase is recommended rather than dropping down rapidly in an effort to bounce out of the bottom position.
4. Reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knee and hip to squat up until you have returned to standing upright.
   1. Like during the descent, continue keeping your foot fully in contact with the ground and your hip bones level as you avoid letting your knee cave inward.
   2. Greater assistance from your hands is commonly needed when squatting up compared to squatting down.
5. Alternate between steps 2-4 until you have completed your set and are ready to repeat the exercise with your other side.

## Description

While statically training your gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, this exercise primarily trains your quads, gluteus maximus, and adductor magnus.
