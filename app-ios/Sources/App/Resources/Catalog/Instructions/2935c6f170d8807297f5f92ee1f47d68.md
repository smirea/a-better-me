## Setting up

When comparing different models, hip thrust machines vary in terms of how they are designed and which features can be adjusted. While some models permit you to adjust the backrest and seat, other machines exclusively allow you to adjust the weight. Consequently, certain machine adjustments described in the instructions may not be applicable to a particular machine model you are using. Regardless of which machine you are using, you ultimately want to ensure that you set up in a manner that positions the crease of your hips under the hip belt/pad to align the machine’s pivot point with your hip joints. The proper set-up should also result in your knees being bent by about 90 degrees at the top of your hip thrust, feet feeling stable on the foot plate, upper back feeling well-supported by the back pad, and lower back feeling comfortable throughout the set. Determining how you should set up to achieve these objectives may require some trial and error during various steps if you are using a machine that is unfamiliar to you.

1. Move the back pad forward or backward to a setting that allows you to rest your upper back comfortably on it while your hips are aligned with the hip belt/pad and your feet are on the foot plate with your knees bent by at least 90 degrees.
2. Adjust the seat to a height that can result in your thighs and torso forming reasonably close to a 90-degree angle (when viewed from the side) when you are in the starting position.
   1. Aiming to achieve near this 90-degree angle is an appropriate starting point for most people, but the ideal seat height specifically should allow you to set up with your lower back in a flat position while training through a meaningful range of motion.
   2. An excessively low seat can require you to round your lower back to move into the starting position.
   3. An excessively high seat can limit the degree to which your hips are initially bent and restrict the range of motion for this exercise.
   4. The seat height that is personally best for you can be affected by your stature and your hip mobility.
3. Select your desired weight with the machine’s loading pin.
4. Lie down with your upper back on the back pad, hips aligned with the hip belt/pad, and the soles of your feet fully on the foot plate, with your knees bent by mildly greater than 90 degrees.
   1. If your machine has a fairly short upper back pad, you can place the lower part of your shoulder blades against the front of the pad. If your machine has a long back pad, you may be able to rest your middle and upper back against it.
   2. In addition to moving the back pad forward or backward, you can move your feet further forward or backward on the foot plate to modify the degree to which your knees are bent.
   3. The degree to which your knees are bent will decrease as you thrust your hips up during a rep, so initially setting up with a little over 90 degrees of knee bend can help you achieve about 90 degrees of knee bend at the top of the rep.
5. Situate your feet near shoulder width apart with your toes pointing straight ahead or mildly out to the sides, depending on what is most comfortable.
   1. A near shoulder-width stance is appropriate for most people, but feel free to adjust your stance based on what feels best to you, since what specifically is the most comfortable stance width can vary among different individuals, just like the most comfortable degree of toe flare.
6. Depending on what your machine has, snugly fasten the hip belt around the crease of your hips, or set the hip pad snugly on top of the crease of your hips.
   1. If you feel notable discomfort from the pressure of the belt/pad during your set, you can try shifting your hips slightly further forward or backward to adjust where the pressure is concentrated. When making any adjustments during a set, only do so in between reps when the machine’s lever arms (onto which you loaded plates) are resting on the stoppers underneath them.
   2. If you continue to feel discomfort from the pressure of the belt/pad, you can try cushioning your hip bones by placing a folded exercise mat or thick foam between your hips and the belt/pad.
7. Tuck your chin mildly toward your chest, and maintain this neck position throughout the exercise.

## Exercise technique

1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Continue bracing throughout your set to help stabilize your lower back.
   2. From this starting position, your back should be flat.
2. Lift your hips up by driving your heels into the foot plate and squeezing your glutes.
   1. At the end of the lifting phase, you should be able to draw a straight line from your knee through your hip to your shoulder when viewed from the side. Your thighs and torso should naturally become aligned when you fully contract your glutes.
   2. Focus on moving your hips and avoid arching your lower back excessively. Rather than flaring up (which occurs from lower back arching), the bottom of your ribs should remain pointing down toward your hip bones.
   3. If you wish to do so, you can hold the full glute squeeze at the top for a moment, which may assist with feeling your glutes engage during this exercise.
   4. As a cue for engaging your glutes, you can think about trying to crush a walnut between them or tightly holding onto a million-dollar check with your glutes.
   5. Throughout the exercise, keep your upper back snugly in contact with the back pad and the soles of your feet fully planted on the foot plate while concentrating pressure through your heels.
3. Reverse the movement in a controlled manner by lowering your hips through the full range of motion in which you can keep your lower back flat.
   1. If you were able to position the seat to a height that allowed you to assume the starting position with a flat lower back, you can lower your hips until you are able to tap the seat gently with your buttocks.
   2. If you do not have a seat under you that you can reach while maintaining a flat lower back position, simply lower your hips as much as you can without rounding your lower back. Most lifters will be able to reach the point at which their thighs and torso form at least a 90-degree angle, but your individual end range can be affected by your hip mobility.
   3. You do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, but you should avoid simply letting gravity pull you down abruptly, as opposed to using your glutes to control the descent actively.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set.
   1. After completing the lowering phase of your last rep, allow the weights you selected to rest fully on the rest of the weight stack underneath them before unfastening the hip belt or lifting up the hip pad.

## Description

This exercise will predominantly develop your gluteus maximus while also training your quads to a lesser degree.
