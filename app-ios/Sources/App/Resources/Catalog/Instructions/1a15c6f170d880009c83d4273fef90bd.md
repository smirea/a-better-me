## Setting up

1. While standing under a pull-up bar, reach up and firmly grasp it with your palms facing backward (i.e., an underhand grip) and your hands shoulder-width apart.
   1. Specifically, how closely you position your hands is up to your preference. Still, they should be clearly closer than for a standard chin-up with a moderate grip width (i.e., approximately shoulder width).
   2. Depending on the specific pull-up bar you are using, your height, and your wingspan, the bar may be beyond your reach.
   3. If you cannot reach the bar while standing on the ground, you can initially step up onto a sturdy box, an aerobic step, or a bumper plate to place the bar within reach.
   4. While not required, elevating yourself to reach the bar may help you set up with a more stable hand position around the bar than if you jumped up from the ground to reach it.
   5. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the bar with the bar pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
   6. If you position the bar closer to the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), it can slide up your hand while hanging on it, which can pull on your hand skin in an uncomfortable way and increase the risk of tearing a callus.
2. With your elbows straight and your hands firmly grasping the bar, lift your feet up off the ground by bending your knees to begin hanging on the bar.

## Exercise technique

1. While hanging on the bar with straight elbows, simultaneously pull your shoulder blades down and mildly arch your middle and upper back to angle your chest slightly upward.
   1. Doing so will place you in a more advantageous position to start your pull than if you kept your chest facing horizontally and allowed your shoulder blades to be shrugged up by your bodyweight.
   2. Arching your middle and upper back puts you in a “proud chest” posture, which you should maintain throughout your set.
2. Pull your chest up toward the bar by driving your elbows down and toward your sides while progressively bending your elbows.
   1. Since the bar is fixed, your elbows naturally will not actually drive downward, but having the intention helps you pull yourself upward with an appropriate technique that effectively trains your back muscles.
   2. Strive to pull through your full available range of motion, which commonly results in the upper arms being close to the sides of the torso and the elbows bent enough that the forearms contact the biceps; however, different individuals may have different ranges of motion enabled by their bodies.
   3. It is up to your preference whether you would like to keep your thighs directed vertically downward (resulting in a similar hip position as when you stand) or whether you like to lift your knees moderately up (resulting in a more flexed hip position, like when you are seated, but to a lesser degree). Regardless, avoid swinging your legs during the rep.
3. Reverse the motion by lowering your body in a controlled manner until you have returned to the starting position with your arms positioned overhead and your elbows fully straightened.
   1. You do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, but you should actively control the entire descent rather than simply dropping down.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set.

## Description

This exercise can effectively develop your lat, teres major, and rear delt while also meaningfully training your biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis.

### **How Your Grip and Pull-Up Technique Influence Back Muscle Development**

Somewhat of a range of grip widths can fall within what is closer than shoulder width, but whether you opt to position your hands as close together as possible or only mildly within shoulder width is unlikely to alter to any meaningful degree which muscles are trained from this chin-up variation. Nonetheless, when repeatedly performing this exercise over time, you can feel free to try out different grip widths to determine if any particular position feels most comfortable to you and/or allows you to perceive your target muscles working particularly well. Pull-ups can be performed with a variety of hand grips including different grip widths and whether an overhand, underhand (resulting in the exercise being called a chin-up), neutral, or hybrid (i.e., a combination between neutral and underhand or overhand) grip is utilized. Additionally, the shoulder motion occurring during a pull-up can be performed predominantly in the sagittal (front to back) plane, frontal (side to side) plane, or in a combination between the sagittal and frontal planes. When symmetrically moving the shoulders strictly in the sagittal plane (like would occur when lifting your arms straight out in front of you), the upper arms remain parallel to each other and do not deviate away from the mid-line of your body. When symmetrically moving the shoulders strictly in the frontal plane (like would occur when lifting your arms straight out to your sides such as with a snow angle motion), the upper arms move away from the mid-line of your body until they are parallel to the ground, and then they move closer to the mid-line of your body with further elevation. During the lifting phase of a pull-up, the shoulders could extend when moving in the sagittal plane, adduct when moving in the frontal plane, or exhibit a combination of extension and adduction when moving at a hybrid between the sagittal and frontal planes. Rather than moving the shoulders exclusively through one plane, pull-up variations are commonly performed with a technique that allows for the shoulders to move in between these two planes, but one plane of shoulder motion often makes a greater contribution than the other.

Muscles acting on the shoulder joint have different leverage to generate extension torque in the sagittal plane versus adduction torque in the frontal plane, and the nervous system is most likely to recruit the particular muscles that are best leveraged to produce the intended joint action during any active movement. Correspondingly, the plane of motion in which the shoulders move during a pull-up can affect how well different muscles are likely to be trained. With any particular grip for a pull-up, you can intentionally rotate your shoulders in a manner that allows you to move your shoulder relatively more in the sagittal or frontal plane. However, most people will typically find that a particular path of shoulder motion feels most comfortable and stable to their shoulders, elbows, and wrists when using a specific grip. For instance, people will normally perform a wide overhand grip pull-up with shoulder motion predominantly occurring in the frontal plane, while they will normally perform a close grip chin-up (using an underhand grip) with shoulder motion predominantly occurring in the sagittal plane. To perform either variation in the alternative plane of motion would require greater than normal rotational range of motion in the shoulders and forearms, and attempting to do so could feel very awkward and uncomfortable. People will typically feel most comfortable when positioning themselves in a manner that, at the portion of the pull-up when their upper arms are situated parallel with the ground, results in their elbows and wrists being vertically aligned or their elbows lying somewhat lateral (outside) to their wrists.

In general, widening a grip facilitates relatively more frontal plane shoulder motion, while narrowing a grip facilitates relatively more sagittal plane shoulder motion. An overhand, underhand, or neutral grip may be used with any grip width, but the combination of these grip variables will influence comfort and which plane of shoulder motion feels most natural. When pulling predominantly in the sagittal plane with a rather close grip, an underhand or neutral grip will typically be more comfortable than an overhand grip. Similarly, when pulling predominantly in the frontal plane with a rather wide grip, an overhand or neutral grip will typically be more comfortable and enable more frontal plane motion than an underhand grip. With a moderate width grip, people may commonly find that pulling mostly in the sagittal plane feels natural with an underhand or neutral grip, but pulling relatively more in the frontal plane feels natural with an overhand grip. Fundamentally, the shoulder motion is what matters to the back muscles, and the grip is only relevant to the back muscles based on how it influences which shoulder motion is performed. When performing this chin-up with a close grip width, you most likely will pull in a way that predominantly trains shoulder extension in the sagittal plane as your elbows remain close to being aligned with your shoulders rather than flaring out to the sides.

The lat and teres major in the back function both as primary adductors and extensors and are consequently highly likely to be trained very effectively by pull-up variations regardless of the grip or plane of shoulder motion. However, the preponderance of available evidence indicates that the rear deltoid lacks meaningful leverage to contribute to shoulder adduction, but it clearly has favorable leverage to function as a shoulder extensor. The rear deltoid is correspondingly likely to be trained better with a closer grip emphasizing shoulder extension, which would be most comfortable when using an underhand or neutral grip. Alternatively the middle and lower regions of the pectoralis major (that constitute the sternoclavicular head) have large moment arms to function as shoulder adductors, but only the lower portion exhibits a shoulder extension moment arm, and this moment arm is quite small and only present in flexed shoulder positions. Consequently, wider grip pull-ups (that will be most comfortable with an overhand or neutral grip) involving greater frontal plane shoulder motion are likely to train the pectoralis major more effectively than closer grip pulldowns. Which regions of the lats are preferentially biased by a pull-up variation may also potentially be influenced by how much shoulder motion occurs in the frontal or sagittal planes. The middle and lower regions have relatively greater leverage to function as shoulder adductors, while the upper region of the lat has relatively greater leverage to function as a shoulder extensor. Plausibly, these biomechanical differences may facilitate the middle and lower regions of the lats being preferentially trained by wider grip pull-ups, while the upper region may be preferentially trained by closer grip pulling variations. With that said, the degree to which any preferential biasing may occur among different regions of the lats when comparing different pull-up grips is more speculative in nature and has not been tested by any longitudinal training interventions.

### **Why Overhand Grip Pull-Ups may be More Difficult than Chin-Ups or Neutral Grip Pull-Ups**

While exceptions exist, people often find overhand grip pull-ups to be more difficult on a per rep basis than chin-ups or neutral grip pull-ups. The discrepancy is most likely influenced by chin-ups and neutral grip pull-ups typically being performed with a technique that emphasizes shoulder extension in the sagittal plane, while overhand grip pull-ups are typically performed with relatively more shoulder adduction in the frontal plane. As demonstrated by studies such as those from Mayer et al. (1994) along with Shklar and Dvir (1995), on average, maximal torque for shoulder extension exceeds maximal torque for shoulder adduction. The sample of participants, velocity, and type of muscle action tested can all influence the degree to which torque differs between these two actions, but maximal shoulder adduction torque has been measured to be 63-90% of maximal shoulder extension depending on the specific test conditions. In addition to the effect from how the shoulder moves, the forearm position can also affect how much elbow flexion torque you can generate during your pull. Guenzkofer et al. (2012) found that up to 15% greater elbow flexion torque can be generated when the forearm is in supinated (used for an underhand grip) or neutral position compared to a pronated (used with an overhand grip) position. Together these relationships mean that most people should be able to generate greater torque at the shoulders and elbows while performing chin-ups or neutral grip pull-ups in comparison to overhand grip pull-ups, which are likely to be more challenging when performing the same number of reps. Consequently, it is common not to be able to complete as many reps of overhand grip pull-ups in a set as you would be able to complete when performing chin-ups or neutral grip pull-ups. Nonetheless, individual differences among factors such as training history and the relative strength of different muscle groups can result in some people not noticing a difference in difficulty between these variations or even finding overhand grip pull-ups to be somewhat easier on a per rep basis.

### **Chin Over Bar?**

The technique instructions do not specify where any particular part of your body should be situated in relation to the bar at the end of the lifting phase because your full range of motion can differ from someone else’s full range of motion. Pulling until your chin is above the bar is a common standard that is utilized for pull-up testing by the United States Marines or competitions held by the World Pull-Up Organization (which permits use of an underhand or overhand grip), and you certainly can use this standard if you wish. However, when focusing on chin clearance, some people have a tendency to extend their necks in a manner that lifts their chins upward in order to clear the bar with lower range of motion through the pull itself compared to if they retained a neutral neck position. In this instance, focusing on the proxy for a full range of motion may lead to a different movement compared to focusing on what is the full range of motion for the individual. When competing or performing a standardized test that affects professional prospects, it is only sensible to perform the minimum amount of work required to complete each rep per the rules provided, but training to build muscle and strength is a different endeavor.

### **The Most Challenging Part of the Pull-Up**

The end range of the lifting phase will typically be the most challenging portion of chin-ups in large part due to how the torque our pulling muscles can generate meaningfully varies across different joint angles. When the elbow is flexed to 120 degrees, Bober et al. (2002) found maximal elbow flexion torque to be approximately 35% of the peak torque that can be generated in a moderately flexed elbow position (i.e., near 60-75 degrees of elbow flexion). With your elbows approaching full flexion toward the end range of a chin-up, your elbow flexor muscles are correspondingly operating from a rather disadvantaged position to help you complete the rep. On average, maximal shoulder extension torque is also lower when the shoulder is mildly elevated (like at the end of a chin-up’s lifting phase) compared to when it is moderately elevated (like during the middle range of a chin-up). The difference can be of a notable magnitude as indicated by Bober et al. (2002) finding maximal shoulder extension in a neutral shoulder position to be only 60% of its peak value that could be generated in 90 degrees of shoulder flexion. Together these strength curves of the muscle groups acting on the elbow and shoulder can contribute to your chin-ups consistently feeling the most difficult during the last quarter of the lifting phase.

### **Straps**

If you find that your grip strength functions as the limiting factor as opposed to your back muscle strength when performing this exercise, you can utilize chalk or straps in an effort to amplify the stimulus each set provides your back muscles. As a simple approach you can use if you are unsure whether grip strength is limiting your performance, you can perform a set of as many quality reps as you can with a particular weight without using straps and compare your rep total to a set using straps. To perform an appropriate comparison, you would want to ensure that you are using the same technique for each condition, which will mean using the same range of motion and tempo. Additionally, you would want to perform each condition when you are similarly recovered. Practically, resting at least three minutes between the two sets should allow most fatigue to dissipate by the time you perform the second set, therefore enabling a useful comparison in terms of how performance of this exercise may be affected by your grip strength. However, if you really wish to optimize the validity of your self-assessment, you can compare your rep performance between these conditions on a second day where you reverse the order of the conditions. If you performed the set without straps prior to the set using straps on the first day, you perform the set using straps before the set without straps on a different day to control for how any fatigue resulting from the first set may influence the second set. When comparing how many reps you complete during each condition, you will be well informed as to how grip strength may be affecting performance of this exercise for you as an individual.
