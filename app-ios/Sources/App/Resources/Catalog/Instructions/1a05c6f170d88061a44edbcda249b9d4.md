## Setting up
1. Position an adjustable decline bench at a decline angle of 15-30 degrees.
    1. As the decline angle becomes more pronounced, transitioning into the starting position can become more awkward, so a relatively mild decline is typically appropriate.
    2. Utilizing an approximately 15-degree decline angle is recommended if you are new to this exercise.
    3. Some adjustable benches have a mild decline setting along with incline settings, but lack a pad to secure your feet. Using a decline bench with a foot/ankle pad is recommended for stability during this exercise.
2. Pick up one dumbbell on the side you will be training, and sit down on the higher end of the bench while lifting the dumbbell onto your upper thigh.
    1. The directions describe how to assume the starting position for this exercise independently, but you can alternatively have a spotter transfer the dumbbell to you after you lie down on the bench, which can be ideal if you are using a heavy dumbbell.
3. Snugly position the front of your ankles and the top of your feet under the foot/ankle pad.
    1. Your legs should feel securely locked in place and not permit any sliding.
4. On the side firmly holding the dumbbell, tuck your elbow by your side. Tightly wrap your free hand around the hand holding the dumbbell.
5. Lean back in a controlled manner to lie down while keeping your elbow tucked by your side and the dumbbell close to your torso.
    1. While using both arms to help control weight, move your torso and arms together as one unit to minimize any shoulder stress when lying down.
    2. Your head and upper back should now be in contact with the bench, along with your buttocks and feet (which remain hooked under the pad). Maintain this body position until you have finished your set.
    3. Strive to have the dumbbell be vertically stacked over your elbow once you are lying down, resulting in your forearm being in a vertical position.
6. Unwrap your outer hand, not directly holding the dumbbell, and move it into a comfortable position away from the weight, such as resting on your torso.
    1. All the subsequent directions will apply to the arm in which you are holding the dumbbell, since that is the side you will be training.
    2. You should be holding the dumbbell on top of the base of your palm (AKA heel of your hand) with a mostly straight wrist that allows for a comfortable and stable grip that you will maintain throughout your set.
7. Assume a “proud chest” posture that puffs your chest slightly up toward the ceiling by tightly pulling your shoulder blades back together. Maintain this chest position throughout your set.
    1. As a cue for pulling your shoulder blades back, you can think about trying to squeeze a pencil between your shoulder blades. This should prompt you to feel your upper back muscles contracting like at the end range of a rowing exercise.
8. Flare your elbow moderately out to your side such that your upper arm forms near a 45-75-degree angle with your torso.
    1. Keep your forearm vertical while doing so.
    2. Based on your preference and what feels comfortable for your shoulder, you can decide specifically how much you flare your elbow out within this range.
    3. Zero degrees of elbow flare would mean your elbow is completely tucked by your side, while 90 degrees of flare would mean your upper arm would form half of a “T-shape” with your torso.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. You will continue bracing your core throughout your set, which will help your body function as a stable platform from which to press.
2. Press the dumbbell upward until your elbow is fully straightened with your arm directed straight up toward the ceiling.
    1. It may help to focus on driving your elbow upward and inward.
    2. While motion simultaneously occurs at your shoulder and elbow, your forearm should be in a vertical position throughout the press. This will result in your wrist and dumbbell remaining stacked over your elbow.
    3. At the end of the lifting phase, the dumbbell should be situated vertically over your shoulder.
3. Reverse the movement by lowering the dumbbell in a controlled manner. Move through the full range of motion your shoulder mobility comfortably permits while keeping your wrist stacked over your elbow.
    1. Let your elbow flare out moderately away from your side so that your upper arm forms an approximately 45-75-degree angle with your torso at the bottom. Lifters often find that at least a mild degree of elbow tuck makes this exercise feel most comfortable for the shoulder compared to fully flaring the elbow straight out to the side.
    2. People commonly can lower the weight until their elbows are somewhat lower than their shoulders while keeping their forearms vertical, but your specific end range will be determined by your individual shoulder mobility. It is normal to feel a stretching sensation in your chest and the front of your shoulder at your end range.
    3. While you do not have to perform the lowering phase with an extremely slow tempo, you should be actively controlling the entire movement.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set and are ready to repeat the exercise with your other side.
    1. It can be awkward to transfer the weight to your other hand with proper positioning while lying down if you are using a weight that is at least fairly heavy to you, so you can initially return to an upright position to transfer the weight before resetting to train your other side.
5. After finishing the lowering phase of the last rep, bring your upper arm in line with your side to tuck your elbow and position the dumbbell closer to your torso.
6. Wrap your free hand around the hand holding the dumbbell for additional stability.
7. If you are able to do so, rock forward in a sit-up motion until you are sitting upright and can place the dumbbell on top of your thigh before grasping the dumbbell with your opposite side and repeating the exercise.
    1. This maneuver is only feasible if you have the core strength to perform a sit-up with the weight you selected. Compared to if you decline pressed two dumbbells simultaneously, repositioning yourself will be easier with this single arm variation due to the lighter total load.
    2. If you cannot sit up with the weight, you can have a spotter take the dumbbell from your hand while you are lying down on the bench. This option can be ideal if you are using a heavy weight, but it naturally requires another person. When using this approach, make sure to communicate what you want your spotter to do before starting the set.
    3. Alternatively, you can set the dumbbell directly onto the floor while still lying down before picking up the weight with your other side and resetting. Compared to the other options, this approach potentially may involve a higher risk of injury if you move your shoulder into a disadvantaged position with a heavy weight, but you can assist with your free hand. Relative to setting the weight down under control, dumping the weight forward may be less demanding on your shoulder, but it involves the risk of having a dumbbell ricochet and potentially injuring someone nearby or damaging equipment. For these reasons, it is recommended to use a spotter or to sit up with the dumbbell when possible.
## Description
This exercise can effectively develop your pec major with a focus on its middle and lower regions while also training your front delts and triceps.
