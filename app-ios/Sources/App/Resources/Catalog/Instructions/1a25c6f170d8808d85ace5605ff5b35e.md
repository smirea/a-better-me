## Setting up

1. Adjust the J-hooks of a rack to be slightly below the height of your elbows when standing with your arms at your sides.
2. Place a barbell on the J-hooks, and add weight plates of your desired load.
3. Optionally place a squat pad or hip thrust pad around the center of the bar to provide cushioning for your elbows.
   1. Wearing a fairly thick long-sleeved shirt (like a hoodie) or elbow sleeves can be another means of cushioning your elbows.
4. Face the center of the bar in a near shoulder-width stance.
5. Mildly bend your knees, and hook your elbows under the bar as your elbows are bent and pointing down toward the ground.
   1. Based on your preference, you can clasp your hands together or leave them unclasped.
6. Stand upright with the bar by straightening your knees and hips.
7. Use the unloaded bar to perform a couple of Zercher squat reps with the form described under the “exercise technique” section to determine the appropriate height of the safety pins prior to reracking the bar onto the J-hooks.
8. Load the bar with your intended weight, and position the safety pins to be slightly under the lowest point at which the bar descends by the end of the lowering phase of the Zercher squat.
   1. If you train without safety pins and fail a rep, you can dump the weight forward, which may require shooting your hips up and pushing your knees into a more hinged position so that you can dump the bar without hitting your legs. This maneuver increases loading on your lower back (which is not inherently problematic but may be uncomfortable for some people), and it has the potential to damage certain types of flooring, so using the safety pins is recommended.
9. Repeat steps 4-6 of the setup and proceed to the “exercise technique” section.

## Exercise technique

1. Take a couple of steps backward, and position your feet shoulder-width apart or wider with your toes pointing somewhat out to the sides.
   1. The specific stance width and degree of toe flare that is ideal will vary among different individuals based on their hip structure and mobility.
   2. Ultimately, you want to position your feet in a manner that allows you to feel comfortable and stable when squatting, which typically requires trying out different stances over time and assessing how you feel with them.
   3. In the starting position, your neck should be near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright. Keep your neck in this position throughout your set.
2. Brace your core by initially taking a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward as you tense these muscles like you would do if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
   1. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
   2. Strive to create tension throughout the entirety of your core to form a “360-degree brace” instead of only tensing the front of your core.
   3. Continue bracing your core throughout your set.
3. Initiate the descent by simultaneously breaking at your knees and hips. Progressively bend at your knees and hips to squat down in a controlled manner while keeping your knees aligned with your feet.
   1. As you descend, it is natural for your torso to lean forward mildly at your hips to maintain balance, but aim to keep your torso mostly upright, with your back flat and chest up.
   2. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the ground as your weight is evenly distributed throughout your feet (rather than being all on your heels or toes) during your set.
   3. As you bend your knees, it is normal for them to track forward over your feet (especially the deeper your squat) to help you maintain your balance.
   4. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot, as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet.
   5. Keep the bar close to your torso with your bent elbows hooked under it throughout your set.
4. Continue to squat down through the available range of motion your mobility allows without rounding your back, lifting your heels off the ground, or letting your knees cave inward.
   1. People can often descend at least until their thighs are fairly close to being parallel to the ground, but what constitutes a full range of motion for a squat will vary among different individuals.
   2. If your back starts rounding, your heels lift off the ground, or your knees cave inward at the bottom of your squat, you may be squatting too deep relative to your current level of mobility and body control.
5. Reverse the movement by progressively standing up as you drive through the ground while extending (straightening) your knees and hips until you have returned to a fully upright position.
   1. Continue keeping your chest up, back flat, feet fully on the ground, and knees stacked over your feet as you squat up.
6. Alternate between steps 3-5 until you have completed your set.
   1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
   2. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before initiating the descent for the next rep.
7. Rerack the bar by walking forward until the barbell contacts the back of each J-hook, and you are ready to rest the bar on the J-hooks by once again bending at your hips and knees in a quarter squat-like manner.

## Description

While statically training your spinal extensors, this exercise can effectively develop your gluteus maximus, adductor magnus, and three of your four quad muscles.
