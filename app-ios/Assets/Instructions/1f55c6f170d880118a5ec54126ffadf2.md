## Setting up
1. Lie down on your back with your legs straight, feet approximately hip-width apart, and the back of your head either resting on the ground or on top of your hands, which you can clasp behind your head.
    1. If you do not want to position your hands behind your head, feel free to place your arms wherever you prefer (e.g., resting on the ground by your sides).
    2. On the ground, your legs and torso should be in the same position you exhibit when normally standing upright.
## Exercise technique
1. While keeping your knees straight, lift both legs off the ground through your available range of motion, tightening your abdominal muscles to press your lower back into the ground.
    1. Strive to lift your legs as close to vertical as your flexibility allows, keeping the backside of your hip bones in contact with the ground throughout this variation (in contrast to a reverse crunch).
    2. How high you can lift your legs can be influenced by hamstring flexibility, since flexing your hip with a straight knee stretches your hamstrings.
    3. Once you feel that you cannot lift your legs any higher up without raising your hip bones up off the ground, you have reached the end of the lifting phase.
    4. Pressing your lower back into the ground will flatten it out from abdominal muscle contraction and prevent you from being able to slide a hand under your lower back.
    5. If you do not contract your abdominals, lifting both legs up would prompt your lower back to develop an exaggerated arch that would allow you to slide a hand under your lower back.
2. Reverse the motion by allowing your legs to descend in a controlled manner until the back of your heels lightly contacts the ground.
3. Alternate between steps 1 and 2 until you have completed your set.
## Description
Along with training your hip flexor muscles on the front of your thigh, this exercise can statically train your abdominal muscles including your obliques and rectus abdominis, which is the “six-pack muscle.”

### **How the abdominal and hip flexor muscles work together**

As with many other abdominal exercises, the double straight leg raise trains the abdominal muscles concurrently with the hip flexors due to the opposing forces these muscles generate at the lumbopelvic region (i.e., the pelvis and lower back). For the sake of having clearer directions when referring to “up” or “down” during this explanation, we can imagine that you are standing upright rather than lying down like you would when performing this exercise. To lift the thigh via hip flexion, the hip flexor muscles contract, creating a pulling force from the front of the pelvis toward the thigh. Muscle contraction generates a force at both ends to which a muscle attaches, and the hip flexor muscles either originate on the front of the pelvis or cross the front of the pelvis as they course toward their insertion on the femur (thigh bone) or tibia (shin bone). If left unopposed, the force from the hip flexor muscles will rotate the front of the pelvis down toward the thigh as the back of the pelvis rotates up toward the lower back via a motion known as anterior (forward) pelvic tilting. When the pelvis tilts in this manner, the lumbar spine extends correspondingly as the natural arch of the lower back is exaggerated into a sway-back posture.

Contraction of the abdominal muscles can produce the opposite motions given that they arise from the ribs and attach onto the top surface of the front aspect of the pelvis. To pull the ribs closer to the top of the pelvis, the lumbar spine flexes, initially flattening the natural curvature of the lower back and then rounding it with further flexion. Contraction of the abdominal muscles can also pull the upper surface of the front of the pelvis up toward the ribs as the back of the pelvis rotates down toward the thigh, a motion known as posterior (backward) pelvic tilting. Due to the posterior pelvic tilt function of the abdominals and the anterior pelvic tilt function of the hip flexors, these two muscle groups can operate in tandem. Whenever the legs lift off the ground via hip flexion, the abdominal muscles stabilize the lumbopelvic region by preventing the lower back from arching beyond its natural curvature through a combination of anterior pelvic tilt and lumbar spine extension that would otherwise occur with abdominal muscle contraction.
