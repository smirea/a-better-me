## Setting up
1. Stand upright with approximately a hip-width stance in a location that has sufficient space behind you for lunging backward.
    1. You can position your hands wherever you prefer, such as resting by your sides, on top of your hips, in front of your chest, etc.
    2. You will keep your arms in your preferred position throughout your set.
    3. Positioning yourself into a hip-width stance rather than a narrower stance can help you maintain your balance during this exercise.
## Exercise technique
1. While keeping the entire sole of one foot firmly planted on the ground, take a fairly long step backward with the other leg before contacting the ground with your forefoot (ball of your foot and bottom of your toes).
    1. As you do so, start hinging at your front leg’s hip to incline your torso forward while keeping your lower back near a neutral position as opposed to rounding it.
    2. Step far enough backward that you will be able to contact the ground with your rear knee, which may initially require trying out different step lengths if you are new to backward lunges.
    3. Strive to step backward in a straight line rather than stepping closer in toward the midline of your body, which would position your feet horizontally closer together and narrow your base of support.
    4. Strive to keep the tops of your hip bones level with each other rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your back leg drop down.
    5. As a cue for keeping your hip bones level, you can imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
2. After contacting the ground with your rear foot, allow your knees to bend as you lower your body in a controlled manner until you are able to tap the ground gently with the kneecap of your rear leg.
    1. Continue hinging at your front leg’s hip through your available hip range of motion, which will incline your torso further forward while keeping your lower back near a neutral position.
    2. Continue keeping the entirety (heel, inside of forefoot, and outside of forefoot) of your front foot in contact with the ground.
    3. Keep your knees aligned with your feet rather than letting either knee cave inward relative to that side’s foot (when viewed from the front or the back).
    4. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your rear knee, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram).
    5. While the forefoot of your rear leg will be in contact with the ground, the rest of the foot’s sole will be off the ground as it is directed backward.
    6. At the end of the lowering phase, your front leg’s thigh should be close to parallel to the ground, and you will likely feel a stretching sensation in your front leg’s buttocks.
    7. You should aim to perform steps 1 and 2 as a smooth and continuous movement.
    8. If you initially took an excessively short step backward, you may not be able to tap the ground with your rear knee unless you also lift the heel of your front foot up off the ground.
    9. If you find that you have to lift your front heel up to tap the ground, simply descend as much as you can without lifting up your front heel, and take a longer step backward during your next rep.
3. Reverse the movement by progressively extending (straightening) your knees as you step forward with your rear leg to lift your body up until you have returned to the starting position with your feet symmetrically hip-width apart and your torso fully upright.
    1. As you extend your knees, reverse the hip hinge motion you just performed to lift your torso up from the inclined angle so that your torso is facing straight ahead.
    2. Like during the descent, keep your knees aligned with your feet and your lower back near a neutral position.
4. Repeat steps 1-3 while lunging backward with your opposite side.
5. Alternate between steps 1-4 until you have completed your set.
## Description
While still effectively training three of your four quad muscles, this exercise particularly targets your gluteus maximus and adductor magnus.
