## Setting up
1. Place a barbell in a landmine attachment or a viable substitute.
    1. As an alternative to using a landmine attachment, landmine exercises can be performed with one end of the barbell situated in a wall corner.
    2. While the scraping can be lessened by placing a towel between the wall corner and the barbell, be aware that performing landmine exercises in a wall corner can easily damage the wall corner, so this approach is not appropriate in every environment.
2. Load the bar with one full-diameter plate (such as a 45lb (20kg) plate or any bumper plate) or whichever plate will have the largest diameter of the plates you will use for your working weight.
3. Position a sturdy object with an elevated surface directly next to the plate you loaded onto the bar. Select an object with a height that will allow the bar to start at the bottom of your squat once the bar’s plate is resting on the object.
    1. The optimal object height will allow you to squat through your full range of motion. If it is excessively high, the plate will touch the object before you reach the end range of your squat, therefore restricting your squat depth.
    2. If the object’s height is excessively low relative to how deeply you can squat, you will not be able to pick it up initially in the goblet position, and it will be more difficult to set the weight down after your set.
    3. While not ideal, it is preferable to select an object that slightly limits your squat depth rather than an object that is too low for you to assume the appropriate starting position without meaningfully rounding your back.
    4. Trying out different object heights may be needed to determine which height is ideal for you as an individual. After you find what places you in your optimal position, you can make a note of what you used to expedite your set-up for subsequent sessions.
    5. People can often descend at least until their thighs are fairly close to being parallel to the ground, but what constitutes a full range of motion for a squat will vary among different individuals.
    6. As a general starting point, you can try out a flat exercise bench, a plyometric box with a height of 18in (46cm), or an aerobic step with seven risers on each side. With an aerobic step, you can naturally adjust the height via the addition or subtraction of risers. To increase the height with a bench or plyometric box, you can stack bumper plates on top of it. While more time-consuming, you can alternatively use a stack of only bumper plates to form your elevated object.
4. Lift the bar up by the free end and rest the weight plate on top of the elevated object.
5. Perform a couple of reps with only one plate on the bar to confirm you selected the appropriate object height. If you initially selected an object that is too tall or too short, adjust the height of the object before proceeding to add more weight to the bar.
    1. Skip the next setup step and follow the subsequent setup steps before following the “exercise technique” section’s instructions to perform a couple of reps before adding plates to the bar.
    2. The proper starting height should allow you to keep your back flat, feet fully on the ground, and knees in line with your feet at the bottom of your squat. If you cannot satisfy these criteria, a taller object is likely warranted. Alternatively, a lower object may be warranted if you feel that you can squat deeper with quality technique than what the object permits.
    3. If you only have access to objects near the height of a flat bench and find that you can squat deeper than they allow, you can exclusively load the bar with smaller diameter weight plates to extend your squat’s range of motion.
6. Finish adding weight plates to the bar with your desired load.
7. Stand upright with the free end of the bar above the center point between the middle of your feet. Position your feet shoulder-width apart, with your toes pointing mildly out to the sides.
    1. While people may commonly find that setting up reasonably close to this stance allows them to squat effectively, the specific stance width and degree of toe flare that is ideal will vary among different individuals based on their hip structure and mobility.
    2. Ultimately, you want to position your feet in a manner that results in you feeling comfortable and stable when squatting, which typically requires trying out different stances over time and assessing how you feel with them.
    3. Some people may feel most comfortable squatting with their feet close to hip width apart as their toes are pointing straight ahead, while others will feel best with their feet outside of shoulder width with their toes rotated out to the sides at 45-degree angles.
    4. In the starting position, your neck should be near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright, and you will keep your neck in this position throughout your set.
8. Simultaneously bend your hips and knees to squat down until the tip of the bar is just in front of your upper chest.
    1. Keep your back flat, rather than meaningfully rounding it.
9. With your elbows bent, position the bases of your palms (AKA heels of your hands) firmly against the bottom of the end of the bar. Interlock your fingers, and place your thumbs against the tip of the bar.
    1. Your elbows should be tucked slightly in front of your sides. They should be pointing slightly forward rather than pointing straight down, like they would if your upper arms were completely by your sides.
    2. You are now in the goblet position that you will maintain throughout your set.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. You will continue bracing your core throughout your set, which will help you maintain a rigid trunk position while you move through your hips and knees.
2. Drive through the ground to stand up while keeping the end of the bar directly in front of your chest. Push through your legs until you have straightened your knees and hips.
    1. Avoid rounding your lower back or letting your chest cave down through upper back rounding.
    2. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the ground as your weight is evenly distributed throughout your feet (rather than being all on your heels or toes) during your set.
    3. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet.
    4. Allow your body to lean mildly forward at your ankles to keep the bar close to your chest at the end of the lifting phase.
3. Initiate the descent by simultaneously breaking at your knees and hips. Progressively bend at your knees and hips to squat down in a controlled manner until you can lightly tap the elevated object with the weight plates.
    1. Continue keeping your chest up, back flat, feet fully on the ground, knees aligned over your feet, and the weight held close to your chest as you squat down.
    2. While you do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, you should be actively controlling the weight the entire time.
    3. Avoid bouncing the plates off the elevated object instead of gently contacting it with the weight.
4. Alternate between steps 2 and 3 until you have completed your set.
    1. Whenever you feel like you could use a breath, you can breathe in between reps while in the fully upright position.
    2. After taking a breath, make sure to resume bracing your core prior to initiating the descent for the next rep.
5. Once you have completed the lowering phase of your last rep, allow the weight to fully rest on the elevated object before letting go of the bar.
## Description
While statically training your spinal extensor muscles, this exercise can develop your gluteus maximus, quads, and adductor magnus.
