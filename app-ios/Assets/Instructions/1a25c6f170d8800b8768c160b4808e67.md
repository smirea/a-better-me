## Setting up
1. Position your shoulders snugly underneath the shoulder pads as your back is fully in contact with the back pad.
    1. Rather than being rounded in a slouched posture, your back should be near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright.
    2. Based on your preference, you can rest your head against the pad or keep it slightly in front of the pad. Regardless, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
    3. If your machine’s back pad extends low enough, you should also be resting the back of your hip bones against the back pad.
2. Place your feet fully on the middle of the foot plate, near shoulder width apart, with your toes pointing slightly outward.
    1. Many people find that setting up with a stance reasonably close to this allows them to squat effectively. However, the specific stance width and degree of toe flare that is ideal will vary among different individuals based on their hip structure and mobility.
    2. Some people will feel that they can move their hips more freely with a wider stance, while others may prefer a closer stance. Similarly, certain lifters may feel better with their toes pointing straight up, while others may feel more comfortable with their toes pointing moderately further outward.
    3. Ultimately, you want to position your feet in a manner that results in you feeling comfortable and stable when squatting through your full range of motion. Trying out somewhat different stances over time can often help you find out which position is personally best for you.
    4. While recognizing that near the middle of the foot plate works well for many people, you can also adjust how high up on the platform you position your feet based on your preference. Wherever you select should allow your heels (along with the rest of your feet) to be firmly on the foot plate throughout the whole set. Your foot placement should also allow you to keep your whole back snugly against the back pad at the bottom of each rep. The proper position should additionally feel comfortable for your knees.
    5. Placing your feet higher up may make it easier to keep your heels in contact with the foot plate, more difficult to keep your back flat against the back pad, and potentially bias the gluteus maximus to a greater degree. Placing your feet lower down may make it more difficult to keep your heels in contact with the foot plate, easier to keep your back flat against the back pad, and potentially bias the quads to a degree. Avoid positioning your feet toward either end of the platform if doing so feels awkward and/or limits your range of motion.
    6. In the starting position, your knees should be at least slightly bent to allow you to unlock the machine and begin your set. Some machines have multiple starting pegs on which the safety hook could rest, allowing variable starting heights. If your machine’s safety hook is currently latched on a peg that is too high for you to use, you can ask a taller person to reposition the machine to a lower peg. If you are unable to adjust the machine to a lower starting height, situating your feet lower on the foot plate may help you assume the starting position with slightly bent knees.
3. Without having any weight plates added to the machine, perform a few reps with the technique described in the “exercise technique” section before finishing your setup.
    1. These initial reps will help you determine what your full range of motion entails and inform you if any adjustments (such as to your foot placement or machine settings) are warranted.
    2. If you make any adjustments to your foot placement or the machine, it is recommended to try out a couple of reps without additional weights to make sure the adjustments were appropriate before adding weights.
4. If your machine allows you to adjust it, angle the foot plate to a setting that comfortably allows you to train through a full range of motion with your feet fully on the foot plate.
    1. Angling the foot plate closer toward a horizontal position can reduce the amount of ankle mobility required to keep your heels in contact with the foot plate at the bottom of the rep. If you noticed that your ability to keep your heels fully on the foot plate limited how deeply you were able to squat, it may be appropriate to try angling the foot plate closer toward a horizontal position. This may allow you to squat with a deeper knee bend compared to if the foot plate were angled more vertically.
    2. However, your feet may feel unstable and risk sliding if the foot plate is angled excessively close to being horizontal, so you want to find an appropriate balance.
    3. Most pendulum squat machines do not allow you to adjust the foot plate.
5. If your machine has it, position the safety stopper slightly under the lowest point that you reached at the bottom of your pendulum squat rep.
    1. Depending on how deeply you squat and the machine you use, the lowest safety stoppers available potentially may not be sufficiently low to allow you to descend through your full range of motion.
6. Add weight plates of your desired load to the machine, and repeat steps 1 and 2 to assume the starting position with the foot placement you determined is appropriate for you.
    1. Note that the machine will provide resistance even without any additional weight plates, so it can provide sufficiently high resistance to some people without adding any weight plates.
    2. Avoid adding so much weight that you are no longer able to train through your full range of motion.
    3. In addition to the weight sleeve by the back pad, certain machines will have an additional weight sleeve on the opposite end that serves as a counterbalance. Adding weight to the counterbalance sleeve will reduce the difficulty of the exercise compared to if no weight is added to the machine. It can be appropriate to add weight to the counterbalance sleeve if it is too challenging to squat through a full range of motion for your desired number of reps when the machine is unloaded.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. You will continue doing so throughout your set, which will help create full-body tension to amplify your stability and performance.
2. Drive your feet into the foot plate to push yourself away from it by straightening your knees.
    1. After doing so, the safety hook should no longer be resting on the machine’s starting peg.
    2. Your knees should now be straight or just slightly bent.
3. Unlock the safety handle/bar with your hands to rotate the safety hook out of the starting peg’s way.
4. For additional stability, firmly grasp the hand grips/handlebar and continue holding them/it throughout your set.
5. Squat down in a controlled manner by simultaneously bending at your knees and hips.
    1. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the foot plate.
    2. Continue keeping your back snugly in contact with the back pad.
    3. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet. It can help to think about pushing your knees out if you notice them starting to drift inward.
    4. While you do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, you should be actively in control the entire time.
6. Continue squatting down through the full range of motion you can comfortably perform while keeping your heels fully on the foot plate, along with your back flat against the back pad.
    1. People can commonly descend until their knees are bent by at least 90 degrees, and some lifters can reach the point at which their thighs touch their calves with their knees fully bent. However, what constitutes a full range of motion will vary from person to person and be influenced by your individual hip and ankle mobility, along with your foot placement.
    2. If you reach the end range to which your ankle mobility allows your knees to track over your toes, squatting any deeper can cause your heels to start lifting off the foot plate.
    3. If you reach the end range to which your hips can bend, descending any further can cause your lower back to start rounding. This would be demonstrated by your hip bones starting to curl forward.
    4. Squatting into a deep position is not inherently problematic for the knees, but there is no need to force yourself into the lowest position possible if you experience knee discomfort at your end range.
7. Reverse the movement by driving your feet into the foot plate to squat up as you simultaneously extend (straighten) your knees and hips.
    1. Based on your preference, you can finish with your knees fully straightened or ever so slightly bent. It is not inherently dangerous to fully straighten your knees when pendulum squatting, but avoid forcibly snapping your knees into a locked-out position in an uncontrolled manner.
    2. Rather than pushing all through your heels or all through your toes, you should be pushing evenly throughout your feet.
    3. Continue keeping your feet fully on the platform, knees aligned over your feet, and your back fully against the back pad.
8. Alternate between steps 5-7 until you have completed your set.
9. After your knees are straightened following the lifting phase of your last rep, use your hand(s) to rotate the safety hook fully over the starting peg.
10. Lower the safety hook onto the starting peg by bending your knees.
## Description
While also training your gluteus maximus and adductor magnus, this exercise can effectively develop your quads.
