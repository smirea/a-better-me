## Setting up
1. If your machine allows you to adjust it, angle the foot plate to a setting that comfortably allows you to train through a full range of motion with your feet fully on the foot plate.
    1. Angling the foot plate closer toward a horizontal position can reduce the amount of ankle mobility required to keep your heels in contact with the foot plate at the bottom of the rep. However, your feet may feel unstable and risk sliding if the foot plate is angled excessively close to being horizontal, so you want to find an appropriate balance.
    2. Some trial and error may be needed to find the ideal foot plate angle for you, but you can initially select an angle that is about halfway in between the two extremes before trying out different settings over time to determine which one you prefer.
2. Add weight plates of your desired load to the machine.
    1. When learning the exercise, you can alternatively leave the machine unloaded, since it will still provide resistance without additional weight plates.
3. Stand on the foot plate, and position your shoulders snugly underneath the shoulder pads as your back is fully in contact with the back pad.
    1. Rather than being rounded in a slouched posture, your back should be near a neutral position, like when you are normally standing upright.
    2. Based on your preference, you can rest your head against the pad or keep it slightly in front of the pad. Regardless, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
    3. If your machine’s back pad extends low enough, you should also be resting the back of your hip bones against the back pad.
4. Place your feet fully on the middle of the foot plate, near shoulder width apart, with your toes pointing mildly outward and the soles of your feet fully on the foot plate.
    1. People commonly find that setting up reasonably close to this stance allows them to squat effectively, but the specific stance width and degree of toe flare that is ideal will vary among different individuals based on their hip structure and mobility.
    2. Some people will feel that they can move their hips more freely with a wider stance, while others may prefer a closer stance. Similarly, certain lifters may feel better with their toes pointing straight up, while others may feel more comfortable with their toes pointing moderately further outward.
    3. Ultimately, you want to position your feet in a manner that results in you feeling comfortable and stable while being able to train through a long range of motion, which typically requires trying out different stances over time and assessing how you feel with them.
    4. While recognizing that near the middle of the foot plate works well for many people, you can also adjust how high up on the platform you position your feet based on your preference. Wherever you select should allow your heels (along with the rest of your feet) to be firmly on the foot plate throughout the whole set. Your foot placement should also feel comfortable on your knees and allow you to keep your whole back snugly against the back pad at the bottom of each rep.
    5. Placing your feet higher up may make it easier to keep your heels in contact with the foot plate, more difficult to keep your back flat against the back pad, and potentially bias the gluteus maximus to a greater degree. Placing your feet lower down may make it more difficult to keep your heels in contact with the foot plate, easier to keep your back flat against the back pad, and potentially bias the quads to a degree. Avoid positioning your feet toward either end of the plate if doing so feels awkward and/or limits your range of motion.
    6. In the starting position, your knees should be at least slightly bent to allow you to unlock the machine and begin your set. While exceptions may exist, V-squat machines often do not allow you to adjust the starting height of the pads, so certain machines may not be suitable for rather short lifters.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. You will continue doing so throughout your set, which will help create full-body tension to amplify your stability and performance.
2. Drive your feet into the foot plate to push yourself away from it by straightening your knees.
    1. Your knees should now be straight or just slightly bent.
3. Unlock the safety handle with your hand before firmly grasping the hand grips for additional stability.
4. Squat down in a controlled manner by simultaneously bending at your knees and hips.
    1. Keep the entire sole of each foot (including the heel, outer aspect of the midfoot, and inner aspect of the midfoot) firmly planted on the foot plate.
    2. Continue keeping your back snugly in contact with the back pad.
    3. Strive to keep each knee aligned with the second toe (the toe next to your big toe) or the third toe of that side’s foot as you avoid letting your knees cave inward relative to your feet. It can help to think about pushing your knees out if you notice them starting to drift inward.
    4. While you do not have to perform an extremely slow lowering phase, you should avoid dropping down in an uncontrolled fashion.
5. Continue squatting down through the full range of motion you can comfortably perform while keeping your heels fully on the foot plate, along with your back flat against the back pad.
    1. People can commonly descend until their knees are bent by at least 90 degrees, and some lifters can reach the point at which their thighs touch their calves with their knees fully bent. However, what constitutes a full range of motion will vary from person to person and be influenced by your individual hip and ankle mobility, along with your foot placement.
    2. If you reach the end range to which your ankle mobility allows your knees to track forward over your toes, squatting any deeper can cause your heels to start lifting off the foot plate.
    3. If you reach the end range to which your hips can bend, descending any further can cause your lower back to start rounding. This would be demonstrated by your hip bones starting to curl forward off the back pad.
    4. Squatting into a deep position is not inherently problematic to the knees, but there is no need to force yourself into the lowest position possible if you experience knee discomfort at your end range.
6. Reverse the movement by driving your feet into the foot plate to squat up as you simultaneously extend (straighten) your knees and hips.
    1. Based on your preference, you can finish with your knees fully straightened or ever so slightly bent. It is not inherently dangerous to fully straighten your knees during this exercise, but avoid forcibly snapping your knees into a locked-out position in an uncontrolled manner.
    2. Rather than pushing all through your heels or all through your toes, you should be pushing evenly throughout your feet.
    3. Continue keeping your feet fully on the platform, knees aligned over your feet, and your back fully against the back pad.
7. Alternate between steps 4-6 until you have completed your set.
8. After your knees are straightened following the lifting phase of your last rep, engage the safety handle with one of your hands.
9. Bend your knees to lower the weight fully onto the safety arm before stepping off the foot plate.
## Description
While also training your gluteus maximus and adductor magnus, this exercise can effectively develop your quads.
