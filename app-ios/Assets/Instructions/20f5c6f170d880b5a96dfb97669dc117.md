## Setting up
1. Position a sturdy object (such as a thick bumper plate, aerobic step, or plyometric box) that you will use as a step onto the floor.
    1. You can use a range of different heights for your step, and the difficulty of the exercise will increase as the height increases.
    2. If you are new to reverse step-downs, selecting an object that is near four inches (10cm) high (which is a typical height of many aerobic steps without additional risers) can be an appropriate starting point, and you can subsequently adjust the height based on how challenging this height is for you.
    3. Alternatively, you can perform this exercise on the lowest step of a staircase, which is normally near seven inches (18cm) in height.
    4. When feasible, setting up where you can face a mirror is recommended to be able to receive visual feedback about your technique.
2. Pick up a kettlebell or dumbbell and hold it right next to your chest with bent elbows, as your upper arms are slightly in front of your sides (i.e., in the goblet position).
    1. You can use either a kettlebell or a dumbbell, but some people find holding a kettlebell in the goblet position to feel more stable and comfortable, especially with a fairly heavy weight.
    2. Based on your preference and the load you use, you can transfer your kettlebell or dumbbell into the goblet position through a couple of different approaches.
    3. An effective approach is initially to pick up the weight by the handle through a deadlift motion and set it on an elevated surface (like a bench or plyometric box) with the handle facing up if you use a kettlebell or the handle directed vertically if using a dumbbell (as only one end of the dumbbell is on the surface). Then squat down until the weight is right in front of your chest, firmly grasp it in the goblet position, and squat up with the weight. If you use a kettlebell, grasp it by its horns (i.e., side handles). If you use a dumbbell, cup your hands around the top end of the weight with the base of your palms by the handle, and your fingers wrapped around the rest of that end of the weight.
    4. With a lighter kettlebell, you can tilt it onto its side with the weight base directed toward you, squat down to grasp its horns, stand upright with it, and curl it into the goblet position.
    5. With a lighter dumbbell, you can also pick it up with one hand and curl it up across your body to be in front of your chest. Then cup your free hand around the top end of the dumbbell with the base of your palm by the handle and fingers wrapped around the top end of the weight before repositioning your other hand to cup the other side of the weight in the same manner.
3. Step up onto the step with your feet hip-width apart, toes pointing straight ahead, and heels right by the edge.
    1. Your soles should be fully on the step.
## Exercise technique
1. Shift your weight onto one foot, then lift your other foot up and back so it hangs over the edge of the step.
    1. Continue holding the weight close to your chest in the goblet position throughout your set.
    2. Naturally, the side of your body to which you shift your weight will be the working side during this set.
2. On the side hovering off the step, pull your toes and the ball of your foot up toward your shin through an ankle motion that brings your heel closer to the ground than the ball of your foot.
    1. This is the opposite of the ankle motion that occurs when lifting your heels up during a calf raise, and you should feel the muscles on the front of your shin contract.
    2. Throughout your set, maintain this foot position and keep the knee of your non-working side straight.
    3. You are now in the starting position for your first rep.
3. Sit your buttocks back to initiate the descent. Then progressively bend your working leg’s knee and hip in a controlled manner until you can gently tap the ground with the heel of your other foot.
    1. Let your torso naturally lean forward at your hip to maintain your balance, but keep your back flat and chest up. When starting to descend, you can think about beginning to sit on a chair, which cues the intended hip motion that loads your glute.
    2. Keep the tops of your hip bones level with each other to the best of your ability, rather than letting the hip bone on the side of your free leg drop down. Imagine that you have a basin of water filled to the brim on top of your hip bones, and you are trying not to spill any water by tipping it to either side.
    3. While letting your working leg’s knee naturally track forward, strive to keep it aligned with your middle toes, rather than letting it cave inward relative to your foot (which you can check in a mirror, along with how level your hip bones are). Think about driving your knees out to the side if it starts drifting inward.
    4. Keep your elevated heel firmly planted on the step, along with the rest of your sole.
    5. As a cue for the appropriately controlled descent, you can imagine that a weight scale is underneath your free leg’s heel, and gently tapping it will not cause the scale to read more than about two pounds (one kilogram). Lowering yourself slowly can help achieve this very light tap at the bottom.
4. Reverse the movement by driving through your elevated foot to step up into an upright position.
    1. By extending (straightening) your knee and hip, lift your body up completely through your working leg, as you avoid pushing off the ground with your other side’s foot.
    2. Continue keeping your hip bones level and your working leg’s knee aligned with your middle toes.
5. Alternate steps 3 and 4 until you have finished your set and are ready to repeat the exercise with your other side on the step.
    1. Based on your preference, you can keep your non-working side’s foot hovering in the air between reps, or briefly place it on the step before lifting it off for the next rep. The latter approach can be useful if you feel that you need to reset your balance between reps, but either option is valid.
## Description
While training your quads, glutes, and adductor magnus, this exercise can improve your single leg stability.
