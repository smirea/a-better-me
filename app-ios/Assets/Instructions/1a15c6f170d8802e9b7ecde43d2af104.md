## Setting up
1. Pick up one kettlebell, and hold it directly next to your chest with bent elbows as your upper arms are slightly in front of your sides (i.e., in the goblet position).
    1. There are multiple ways in which you transfer the kettlebell to this position based on your preference.
    2. One such way is to tilt a kettlebell onto its side with the bottom of its base facing you, firmly grasp its horns (i.e., the corner handles) with the middle handle facing away from you, stand upright, and curl the kettlebell toward your chest.
    3. With a heavier weight, you can alternatively pick it up by its middle handle in a deadlift motion before setting it on top of a flat exercise bench with its handle facing up. Then you can squat down until the weight is right in front of your chest, firmly grasp it by the horns, and squat up with it close to your chest.
2. With your back facing the head of the position, sit down on a flat exercise bench while keeping the kettlebell close to your chest.
    1. Ensure that there is enough room on the bench behind you so that you can lie down with your head on the end of the bench.
3. Lean back to lie down on the bench while marching your thighs up to help when transitioning. Continue keeping the kettlebell close to your torso with your elbows by your sides.
    1. Let your elbows lower toward the ground when leaning back, which will allow the kettlebell to move slightly further away from your head compared to when you were sitting upright. When sitting upright, this would be the same motion as moving your elbows back slightly back toward your ribcage.
    2. Once you are lying down, return your feet to the ground in a stable position with your knees bent.
4. In a bench pressing motion, press the kettlebell up until your arms are directed straight up toward the ceiling with straight elbows.
    1. In this starting position, you should be holding the kettlebell's horns with mostly straight wrists as the bottom of the kettlebell’s base is directed forward (away from your head).
## Exercise technique
1. Bend your elbows to lower the kettlebell’s handle toward your head in a controlled arcing manner while keeping your elbows up.
    1. Aim to keep your wrists straight throughout the set, which should result in the bottom of the kettlebell’s base angling upward and its handle angling downward while lowering the weight.
    2. Based on what feels most natural and comfortable, you can keep your elbows tucked in, or you can allow your elbows to flare out to your sides slightly.
    3. Movement should chiefly occur from your forearms hinging at your elbows as you strive to avoid letting your elbows drift back and down with the kettlebells through shoulder motion. When viewed from the side, your elbows should remain aligned over your shoulders rather than moving behind (closer to your head) them.
    4. You can think of your elbows as the center of an analog clock, while your forearms are the hands that rotate about these center elbows during this exercise.
2. Finish lowering the kettlebell once its handle is just above your forehead.
    1. Most lifters will be able to finish with their elbows bent by approximately 90 degrees.
    2. While you do not have to perform the lowering phase with an extremely slow tempo, you should be actively controlling the weight the entire way, particularly as it approaches your head.
3. Reverse the movement by straightening your elbows to lift the kettlebell into the starting position.
    1. You should finish with your elbows fully straightened.
    2. Continue keeping your upper arms in a vertical position as you avoid pushing your elbows forward (toward your chest) through shoulder motion.
4. Alternate between steps 1-3 until you have completed your set.
    1. After your last rep, lower your elbows toward your sides while bending them to bring the kettlebell toward your chest.
    2. March your thighs up before rocking forward to sit upright while keeping the kettlebell close to your chest.
## Description
This exercise can effectively develop the entirety of your triceps, including the long head, which is not trained particularly well by pressing exercises.
