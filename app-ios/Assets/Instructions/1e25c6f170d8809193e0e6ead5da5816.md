## Setting up
1. To elevate the bar to your desired height, position a trap bar on top of a pair of deadlift blocks or a similarly sturdy substitute capable of safely withstanding the load you will use.
    1. If your trap bar has a pair of high handles and low handles, you can decide which handles to situate facing up based on the position in which you wish to start your trap bar block pull, which will also be influenced by the height of the blocks you select. It is generally recommended to use a block height that will position the handles you will use at least below your knees once you have placed the trap bar on top of the blocks.   
    2. Aerobic steps can often be used for block pulls while having the option to adjust the height by changing the number of risers you use, but you should be aware of the recommended weight limit if you opt to use heavy loads.
    3. The weight limit for aerobic steps will vary by brand, but some of the common brands recommend not exceeding 300lb (136kg) per step, which would result in a 600lb (272kg) limit when using a pair of steps for a block pull.
    4. The height of the aerobic step will also vary by brand, but the step itself is commonly near 4in (10cm) in height, with the risers being near 2in (5cm) in height.
    5. With thickness typically ranging from about 1-3.5in (2.5-8.9cm), depending on the weight and brand, bumper plates can also function as blocks that can be stacked on top of each other to provide a range of different heights.
2. Add weight plates to the trap bar, and stand within the middle of it with approximately a hip-width to shoulder-width stance and your arms at your sides.
    1. Specifically how wide you situate your feet apart can be based on what feels most comfortable to you when picking up a weight off the floor with a deadlifting motion.
3. With your arms pointing straight down toward the bar and your shoulders packed down (opposite to if you shrugged your shoulders up), sit your hips backward while bending your knees until your hands are level with the bar. Keep your lower back flat and chest up rather than rounding your back.
    1. More motion should come from hinging at your hips than bending your knees as you descend, and your knees should not track particularly far forward, but you can allow your knees to shift moderately forward rather than maintaining completely vertical shins.
    2. As a cue for hinging at your hips, you can think about closing a car door with your buttocks as you sit back while your hands are carrying something. After you have fully sat back and feel a stretching sensation in your hamstrings, you can bend your knees further to reach the bar.
    3. Throughout this exercise, keep your neck near a neutral position, like when you are normally looking straight ahead while standing upright.
    4. As a cue to pack your shoulders down, you can think about making yourself have long arms, which can place you into an efficient start position and help engage your back muscles.
4. With your elbows fully straightened, firmly grasp the center of the handles with your palms facing in toward each other.
    1. For the most stable grip, wrap your fingers around the handles with the handles pressed into where the bases of your fingers meet the palm side of your knuckles.
    2. If you situate the handles closer toward the middle of your palms (rather than the bases of your fingers), the handles can slide down your hands while holding the bar, which pulls on your hand skin in an uncomfortable manner that increases the risk of tearing a callus.
## Exercise technique
1. Brace your core by tensing the front and sides of your abdominals outward, like you would if you were bracing for someone to strike your stomach with a punch or forcible slap.
    1. For additional stability, you can initially take a deep “belly breath” (AKA diaphragmatic breath) that expands the front and sides of your abdominals outward. Avoid taking a “chest breath” that predominantly lifts your chest upward and leaves your abdominals relaxed or simply sucking your belly button inward.
    2. Continue bracing your core from this point onward to help you maintain a rigid back position while using your lower body muscles to lift the weight.
2. Before lifting the bar, apply “pre-tension” to the bar as you develop tension throughout your whole body and start pressing the middle of your feet into the ground with enough force to hear the weight plates clink against the bar.
    1. The initial submaximal pull allows you to check if you have grasped the handles correctly, which will be indicated by the handles not rotating forward or backward when applying the pre-tension.
    2. If you pick up a trap bar with your hands positioned slightly away from the middle of the handles, the trap bar can start rotating in a manner that makes it very difficult to hold and lift appropriately.
    3. Even for experienced lifters, it can be easy to grasp a trap bar’s handles without having their hands truly centered initially, so applying the pre-tension can make sure you are in your ideal starting position.
    4. If the bar starts rotating when you apply the pre-tension, re-center your grip, and apply the pre-tension again until the bar no longer rotates.
3. Drive through your legs to begin lifting the bar as you “push the floor away” while extending (straightening) your hips and knees. Prioritize keeping your chest up, lower back flat, and your core braced throughout the entire exercise.
    1. As you move about your knees and hips, your torso should remain in a similar position when initiating the pull rather than inclining further toward horizontal, which could occur if your hips shoot up from not driving into the ground appropriately.
4. Once the bar is moving, focus on thrusting your hips forward as you continue extending your hips and knees until you are standing fully upright with your chest up and shoulders back.
    1. Lock out the rep with your glutes squeezed as you avoid leaning your torso back through excessive lower back arching, which can occur if you do not focus on squeezing your glutes.
    2. Once upright, you should be able to draw a straight line from your legs through your hips to your shoulders when viewed from the side.
5. Reverse the movement by hinging at your hips while bending your knees to lower the bar until the plates have returned to resting on the blocks.
    1. Just like when lifting the bar, keep your lower back flat and chest up as you descend while actively avoiding letting your lower back round or chest collapse.
    2. You can initially focus on sitting your buttocks back while keeping your shins vertical and allowing your knees to bend naturally until the bar has cleared your knees. Then you can allow your knees to track mildly forward as you further bend your knees and hips to finish lowering the bar.
6. Allow the weight to come to a dead stop on the blocks for a moment (rather than simply tapping the blocks and immediately lifting the bar up again) before continuing to alternate between steps 2-5 until you have completed your set.
    1. While you do not need to pause with the bar on the blocks for more than a second between reps, there should not be any momentum transferred between reps.
    2. You will be able to brace most effectively when you breathe in between reps while the weight is on the blocks rather than during a rep. After taking a breath, resume bracing your core before the next rep.
## Description
While statically training your erector spinae, this can meaningfully train your gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and adductor magnus, along with your quads to a lesser degree.

### Why Pull from Blocks Instead of the Floor?

When performing a low handle trap bar deadlift from the floor, your body proportions will determine the magnitude of hip flexion and knee flexion that is required in order to grasp the handles. Along with bumper plates, plates weighing 45lb or 20kg (which differ slightly) typically have a diameter near 45cm (17.7in) that result in the center of a bar (and a trap bar’s low handles) to which they are loaded being about 22.5cm (8.9in) above the ground. Based on differences in body proportions such as arm length relative to height, certain individuals may find that the handles at this level of elevation require deeper joint angles than they wish to train. In light of a particular person’s mobility, training goals, and injury history, the ideal starting height of any deadlift variation will vary among different individuals. For instance someone with short arms relative to his/her height and limited hip mobility may find pulling from the floor to be quite awkward, but elevating the handles mildly allows for a more comfortable starting position that feels better to that individual. Many trap models have a pair of high handles that allow for a lifter to pull from a height akin to a block pull, but some models lack such handles, so you can perform a trap bar block pull to replicate the high handle trap bar deadlift if you do not have access to a trap bar with high handles. Even when using a trap bar with high handles, a trap bar block pull can still serve a purpose because the height of the handles is fixed. The high handles of trap bars are commonly elevated 4in (10.2cm) above the low handles, but some models have slightly lower high handles, while others have high handles that are up to about 8in (20.3cm) above the low handles. Based on your specific trap bar, body dimensions, and training goals, high handle trap deadlifting from the floor may not provide quite enough handle elevation or it may provide excessive elevation. Correspondingly, you can adjust the starting position to your preference by determining the height of the blocks and which handles you use. 

### **Hip Hinging versus Squatting Your Trap Bar Deadlift**

Relative to conventional deadlifts or block pulls with a straight barbell, trap bar deadlifts can allow you greater liberty in how you perform them with respect to biasing more of a hip hinge or squat movement pattern. A conventional deadlift keeps the load in front of you and constrains forward knee travel, while a trap bar deadlift positions the load closer to your center of mass and permits pushing your knees farther forward than you would do during a conventional deadlift. Trap bar deadlifting with greater forward knee travel makes the movement more squat-like in nature compared to maintaining a closer to vertical shin position, which results in a comparatively more hip-hinge dominant movement. As discussed by Greg Nuckols in his Stronger By Science article “Trap Bar Deadlifts are Underrated”, squat and deadlift variations can be analyzed as existing along a “squat-hinge continuum” based on the demands they impart to the hip extensor muscles (e.g., gluteus maximus, hamstrings, adductor magnus) relative to the quads, which extend the knees. Compared to trap bar deadlifting with a vertical shin, the lever arm of the resistance acting on the knees is greater when deadlifting with your knees situated farther forward, resulting in a greater ratio of loading imparted to the quads relative to the hip extensor muscles. In contrast, the lever arm of the resistance acting on the hips is greater when deadlifting with vertical shins, which prompts you to sit your hips farther back and experience a lower ratio of loading imparted to the quads relative to the hip extensor muscles. While people tend to perform trap bar deadlifts with their knees situated farther forward than when they conventional deadlift, you certainly have the ability to trap bar deadlift with the same knee position you would use for conventional deadlifts. Correspondingly, you can trap bar deadlift with whichever technique you prefer in congruence with your training goals.

Based on research assessing participants using their self-selected technique (which likely is the technique in which they were most efficient for pulling), it is worth noting that low handle trap bar deadlifts have been found to be more hip-dominant than knee-dominant. Swinton et al. (2011) specifically assessed male powerlifters performing low handle trap bar deadlifts and conventional dreadlifts. Both exercises imparted meaningfully greater loading to the hip extensors and lumbar spinal extensors than the quads, but conventional deadlifts were relatively more hip dominant. When lifting 80% of the participants’ conventional deadlift one-rep maxes, the ratio of peak resistive torque acting on the hip to the peak resistive torque acting on the knee was 3.7 for conventional deadlifts and 1.8 for low handle trap bar deadlifts. This difference was mainly attributed to the low handle trap bar deadlift having greater peak resistive torque acting on the knee, which was 190% of the conventional deadlift. A smaller gap was present for hip extensor loading as the peak resistive torque acting on the hip for the low handle trap bar deadlift was 92% that of conventional deadlift. These findings indicate that the degree to which low handle trap bar deadlifts train the quads more than conventional deadlifts most likely exceeds the degree to which conventional deadlifts train the hip extensor muscles more than low handle trap bar deadlifts. In terms of which hip extensor muscles may be less involved during trap bar deadlifts, the biarticular hamstrings in particular will likely receive a lesser stimulus. Camara et al. (2016) and Andersen et al. (2018) both found the hamstring’s biceps femoris to exhibit lower EMG activity during the low handle trap bar deadlift compared to the conventional deadlift, which is congruent with what be anticipated in light of the mechanical differences between these two exercises. Since the trap bar deadlift imparts greater loading to the knee, the knee flexion torque that results from hamstring contraction will have more of a deleterious effect on performance than during the conventional deadlift. While the hip extension torque that the hamstrings produce is beneficial, their knee flexion torque will oppose the quads as they extend the knee, so the hamstrings are not the most efficient hip extensor muscles for the nervous system to recruit during an exercise that imparts meaningful resistance to the quads. To a lesser extent, this is the same principle that results in the hamstrings experiencing minimal hypertrophy after interventions of squatting, which imparts even greater loading of the quads relative to the hip extensor muscles. Accompanying the lower hamstring EMG activity,  Camara et al. (2016) found the quad’s vastus lateralis (which was not assessed by Andersen et al.) to exhibit greater activity with the trap bar deadlift compared to the conventional deadlift. In contrast to the difference in hamstring EMG activity, Andersen et al. (2018) also found the gluteus maximus (which was not assessed by Camara et al.) to exhibit similar EMG activity during the two deadlift variations, which makes sense given that the gluteus maximus is able to generate hip extension without simultaneously generating knee flexion torque.

While the previously discussed data indicates that low handle trap bar deadlifts are typically performed with a more squat-like technique than conventional deadlifts, it is natural to wonder how they may compare to back squats. Choe et al. (2021) did not assess trap bar deadlifts, but they studied conventional deadlifts and back back squats performed with 70-85% of the mixed sex participants’ one-rep maxes. The researchers found the ratio of peak resistive torque acting on the hip to the peak resistive torque acting on the knee to be 3.0 for conventional deadlifts and 1.4 for squats. The participants of this study had some squat and deadlift training experience as the males had average deadlift and squat maxes that were respectively 143.3kg and 122.7kg, while the females had average deadlift and squat maxes that were respectively 95.0kg and 88.0kg. However, they were not nearly as strong as the powerlifter participants partaking in the study from Swinton et al. (2011) who had average conventional deadlift and low handle trap bar deadlift maxes that were respectively 244.5kg and 265.0kg. The participants from Swinton et al. (2011) correspondingly most likely used more efficient techniques, which may explain why they exhibited a higher ratio (3.7 versus 3.0) of peak resistive torque acting on the hip to the peak resistive torque acting on the knee when conventional deadlifting relative to the participants from Choe et al. (2021). When comparing the low handle trap bar deadlift findings from Swinton et al. (2011) to the back squat findings from Choe et al. (2021), the back squat was more knee dominant than the low handle trap bar deadlift, but the previously mentioned hip to knee torque ratio difference (1.7 versus 1.4) was not particularly pronounced.

This data suggests that the ratio of hip to knee loading of the low handle trap bar may be more similar to back squats than conventional deadlifts, but the strengths of our conclusions are limited when comparing findings from different studies assessing meaningfully different participants. For the most valid comparison of trap bar deadlifts to back squats, we ideally would want to analyze the findings from a study that assessed the same participants performing both exercises, which is what Stahl et al. (2024) precisely investigated. The researchers specifically compared the high handle trap bar deadlift (permitting chalk use but not straps) to the back squat (performed until thighs were parallel with the floor) as moderately trained mixed-sex participants lifted loads up to 100% of their maxes. They found the ratio of peak resistive torque acting on the hip to the peak resistive torque acting on the knee to be 0.9 for high trap bar deadlifts and 1.4 for squats (which was the same value determined by Choe et al.), indicating that high handle trap bar deadlifts can be even more knee-dominant than back squats. This difference was influenced by the resistive lever arm acting on the hip during back squats exceeding that during high trap bar deadlifts, while the resistive lever arm acting on the knee was similar between the two exercises. Comparing the data from Stahl et al. (2024) to the data from Swinton et al. (2011), high handle trap bar deadlifts appear to be more knee-dominant than low handle trap bar deadlifts with self-selected techniques, while back squats fall in between these two variations along the spectrum of hip to knee loading.

With that said, it is important to reiterate that you have the ability to perform trap bar deadlifts with either handle height in a more hip-dominant fashion if you wish to do so. Furthermore, it is also worth emphasizing that the aforementioned findings do not mean that high handle trap bar deadlifts are superior to back squats for training the quads. While the knee to hip loading ratios are relevant when appraising the ability of a deadlift or squat variation to train particular muscle groups, the capacity of an exercise to induce muscle hypertrophy is multi-factorial in nature. Multiple studies involving back squat training interventions have found back squats to induce significant quad growth, but the effect that high handle or low handle trap bar deadlifts may have on quad development has yet to be studied.

### **Trap Bar Deadlift versus Conventional Deadlift Strength**

Two different studies from Lockie et al. (2018) have compared one rep maxes without straps between conventional deadlifts and trap bar deadlifts with 4-inch (10.2cm) higher handles. Together they assessed a total of 54 mixed sex participants with at least two years of lifting experience that included conventional and trap bar deadlifts in addition to hitting maxes. Pooling the data from the two studies together, the average high handle trap bar max was 12% greater than the conventional deadlift max, resulting in a 39lb (17.7kg) mean difference. When comparing 1-rep maxes of the conventional deadlift to the low handle trap bar deadlift, Camara et al. (2016) found the maxes to be nearly identical to each other for the two lifts, but Swinton et al. (2011) and Lake et al. (2017) found their participants to lift an average of 6-8% greater loads during the low handle trap bar deadlift.

### **Straps**

If you find that your grip strength functions as the limiting factor when performing this exercise, you can utilize chalk or straps in an effort to amplify the stimulus each set provides your lower body and spinal extensor muscles. As a simple approach you can use if you are unsure whether grip strength is limiting your performance, you can perform a set of as many quality reps as you can with a particular weight without using straps and compare your rep total to a set using straps. To perform an appropriate comparison, you would want to ensure that you are using the same technique for each condition, which will mean using the same range of motion and tempo. Additionally, you would want to perform each condition when you are similarly recovered. Practically, resting at least three minutes between the two sets should allow most fatigue to dissipate by the time you perform the second set, therefore enabling a useful comparison in terms of how performance of this exercise may be affected by your grip strength. However, if you really wish to optimize the validity of your self-assessment, you can compare your rep performance between these conditions on a second day where you reverse the order of the conditions. If you performed the set without straps prior to the set using straps on the first day, you perform the set using straps before the set without straps on a different day to control for how any fatigue resulting from the first set may influence the second set. When comparing how many reps you complete during each condition, you will be well informed as to how grip strength may be affecting performance of this exercise for you as an individual.
