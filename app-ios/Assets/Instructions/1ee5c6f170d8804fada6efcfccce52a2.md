## Setting up
1. Firmly grasp an asymmetrical weighted implement (or a viable alternative) by its lighter end at a position that can provide your desired level of resistance.
    1. Asymmetrical weighted implements include adjustable dumbbells with plates loaded only on one side, hammers, and end-loaded baseball bats.
    2. As an alternative to an asymmetrical weighted implement, you can grasp certain symmetrically weighted cylindrical implements closer toward one end, which can provide the same training effect. Examples include a fixed-weight straight bar, a fixed-weight EZ bar, an adjustable EZ bar, and a cable straight bar attachment (like a lat pulldown bar).
    3. The resistance your forearm pronator muscles experience during this exercise will be dictated by how closely to one end you grasp the implement.
    4. For greater resistance, you can grasp farther away from the weighted end with an asymmetrically loaded implement or closer to whichever end you are holding with a symmetrically loaded implement.
    5. When performing this exercise for the first time with a particular implement, you may have to try out different grip positions to determine specifically where you should grasp the implement to provide the magnitude of resistance you wish to have.
2. Sit down on a seat (e.g., chair, flat exercise bench, plyometric box), and position your forearm (on the side holding the weight) on top of your thigh with a bent elbow and your hand slightly in front of your knee.
    1. You can situate your wrist just above your kneecap, which should allow the weighted implement to rotate freely without contacting any part of your leg.
3. If not already in this position, direct your palm to face in toward your opposite side with a straight wrist position and the heavier end of your weighted implement directed straight up toward the ceiling.
    1. From here, your wrist and forearms are in a neutral position as they would normally be when standing upright with your palms facing in toward your sides.
## Exercise technique
1. While keeping your forearm resting on your thigh, progressively rotate your palm down toward the floor in a controlled manner through your available range of motion.
    1. Doing so will rotate the weighted end (if using an asymmetrically loaded object) or longer end (if using a symmetrically loaded object) in toward the midline of your body, such that this end moves in front of your torso rather than out to the side.
    2. Most people will have the forearm mobility to rotate until their palm is close to facing straight up, which will result in the weight’s handle being close to parallel to the floor, but some people may not be able to rotate quite as far, while others can rotate beyond that point.
    3. As you rotate your forearm, keep your wrist in a straight (neutral) position rather than letting the weight bend your wrist and pull your hand downward (which would bring the palm of your hand closer toward your knee if the wrist flexes).
2. After reaching your end range, reverse the movement until you have returned to the starting position by turning your palm to face in toward your opposite side.
    1. Doing so will lift the weighted end or longer end of the implement upward and outward in an arcing manner until the handle is vertically oriented.
3. Continue rotating your palm up toward the ceiling in a controlled manner through your available range of motion.
    1. Doing so will rotate the weighted end (if using an asymmetrically loaded object) or longer end (if using a symmetrically loaded object) out to your side, such that this end moves away from the midline of your body.
    2. Most people will have the forearm mobility to rotate until their palm is close to facing straight up, which will result in the weight’s handle being close to parallel to the floor, but how far you can move will be influenced by your individual forearm mobility.
    3. Continue keeping your wrist in a straight (neutral) position rather than letting the weight bend your wrist and pull your hand downward (which would bring the back of your hand closer toward your knee if the wrist extends).
4. After reaching your end range, reverse the movement until you have returned to the starting position by turning your palm to face in toward your opposite side.
5. Alternate between steps 1-4 until you have completed your set and are ready to repeat the exercise with your other side.
## Description
While also training your biceps, this exercise can effectively develop your forearm’s pronator teres, pronator quadratus, and supinator. 

### **How to progressively overload**

Resistive torque is what fundamentally determines the challenge any particular muscle group experiences during an exercise. To increase resistive torque, you can increase the weight of the resistance you use, or you can increase the lever arm in which the resistance acts on the joints (and the muscles acting on those joints) you are training. Either variable can be adjusted as a form of progressive overload to increase the torque that your muscles must overcome as they become stronger (i.e., are able to generate greater torque) over time. Whether you modify the load or the lever arm of the resistance is inconsequential to your muscles since the product of those two variables (torque) is what directly matters to the muscles you train. With a forearm pronation and supination exercise such as this, you correspondingly can progress by using a heavier load or by incrementally grasping the implement closer toward one end as you increase your pronation and supination strength. If you have reached the end of your implement and wish to continue increasing the resistance you provide your pronator and supinator muscles, you naturally will have to add load to your implement (which is easiest to do so when using an adjustable dumbbell) or switch to an implement that has a longer handle or a greater weight.

### Training forearm pronation and supination with one exercise

This variation combines the asymmetrical weight forearm pronation and asymmetrical weight forearm supination exercises into one, which can readily be done in part because those two exercises utilize the same starting position. As you transition from the neutral forearm position (with your palm facing in toward your opposite side) to your palm facing down, you are eccentrically training your supinators as your forearm pronates. When moving from the fully pronated position (with your palm down) to the neutral position, you subsequently are concentrically training supination. As you transition from the neutral forearm position to your palm facing up, you are eccentrically training your pronators as your forearm supinates. When moving from the fully supinated position (with your palm up) to the neutral position, you are then concentrically training pronation.

When combining two movements that train different different muscle groups together into one exercise, it is worth noting that whichever muscle group is most fatigued by the time you finish the set will likely be the muscle group that receives the greatest stimulus. Depending on which movements are paired together, you can risk one of the movements being considerably underloaded to the extent that it provides a magnitude of stimulus that is simply insufficient to promote increases in strength or muscle size. For instance, if you combine a dumbbell squat and dumbbell biceps curl into one exercise where you perform a squatting movement followed by a curling movement, you would be noticeably limited by your curling strength in all likelihood. People can normally squat markedly more weight than they curl, and the lower body muscles would not experience remotely close to the same stimulus that they could receive from a squat using loads that are not constrained by curling strength.

However, average forearm pronation and supination strength have been found to be fairly similar in the general population. When pooling the results together from nine studies that measured both pronation and supination strength, Kotte et al. (2018) found average pronation strength to be 93% of supination strength for the dominant arm and 98% of supination strength for the non-dominant arm among males. Females exhibited a mildly greater discrepancy as their average pronation strength was 90% of their supination strength for the dominant arm and 89% of supination strength for the non-dominant arm. Correspondingly, forearm pronation strength will more likely serve as the limiting factor than supination strength during an exercise training both functions, but the difference may be fairly small. Relative to the general population, the discrepancy between supination strength and pronation strength will likely be greater among lifters who commonly perform any curling, vertical pulling (e.g., lat pulldown, pull-up), or rowing variations since these exercises can strengthen the biceps. With the exception of the brachioradialis, which can contribute to both pronation and supination while being effectively trained along with the biceps by exercises resisting elbow flexion, the other muscles that function as forearm pronators or supinators are unlikely to be meaningfully trained by exercises that people most commonly perform. Compared to the general population, lifters consequently will likely have proportionally greater forearm supination strength relative to pronation strength. If your pronation strength limits performance of this combined exercise before you feel your supinators are meaningfully fatigued, you simply can finish the set by performing reps through the half of the movement that resists supination (i.e., alternating between the neutral start position and the palm down position). With that approach, you can ensure that both your supinators and your pronators are effectively trained by the time you finish the exercise.
