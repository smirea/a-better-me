Depending on the path in which your upper arm moves, you can row with different techniques that influence which back muscles are preferentially trained. On one of the spectrums, you can row predominantly through shoulder extension in the sagittal plane, which occurs when keeping your elbow tucked close to your side. When moving your shoulder exclusively through extension in the sagittal plane (which can also occur during exercises like pullovers and underhand grip lat pulldowns), your upper arm will be parallel with your torso toward the end of the row’s lifting phase. Alternatively, you can row predominantly through shoulder horizontal extension (AKA horizontal abduction) in the horizontal plane (AKA transverse plane), which occurs when flaring your elbow out to your side and away from the mid-line of your body. When moving your shoulder exclusively through horizontal extension (which can also occur during exercises like the reverse fly on a pec deck machine), your upper arm will form a right angle with your torso toward the end of the row’s lifting phase. Naturally, you can also row in a manner that involves a hybrid between sagittal extension and horizontal extension, and if your shoulder moves equally between these two planes, your upper arm will form a 45-degree angle with your torso toward the end of the row’s lifting phase.

Without consciously intending to move in a particular plane of motion, people will commonly move their shoulders through a plane that feels comfortable for their shoulders, elbows, and wrists. Which plane of motion feels most comfortable can be influenced by which modality (e.g., barbell, dumbbell, machine) is used in addition to the specific grip used. As grip width progressively increases, rowing with a technique emphasizing greater shoulder horizontal extension (resulting in greater elbow flare) tends to feel more comfortable. However, rowing with a technique that emphasizes greater shoulder sagittal extension (resulting in greater elbow tuck) tends to feel more comfortable as grip width progressively decreases. Whether you utilize an overhand, underhand, neutral, or hybrid (i.e., a combination between neutral and underhand or overhand) grip during any particular row can also affect which grip width and plane of shoulder motion feels comfortable. For instance, when performing a barbell row with an underhand grip, using a shoulder width grip and keeping your elbows tucked while rowing predominantly through shoulder sagittal extension will typically feel most comfortable and natural. However, with an overhand grip, a wider range of grip widths and shoulder planes of motion can be more comfortable. For example, a wide overhand grip barbell row can be performed predominantly through horizontal shoulder extension, resulting in the elbows flaring outward. On the other hand, a shoulder width overhand grip barbell row can be comfortable to perform in a manner that emphasizes more sagittal shoulder extension or exhibits a fairly even balance between horizontal extension and sagittal extension.

Fundamentally, the plane of shoulder motion is what matters to the back muscles, and the grip is only relevant to the back muscles based on how it influences which shoulder motion is performed. Back muscles acting on the shoulder have different leverage to generate sagittal extension torque versus horizontal extension torque, so their ability to contribute to a row will be affected by the specific plane in which shoulder motion occurs during the row. A muscle’s leverage to contribute to a particular joint action can be quantified as its moment arm, which is the perpendicular distance between a joint’s axis of rotation and the muscle’s line of pull. When multiplied by how much force its contraction produces, the moment arm determines how much torque a muscle can generate for a particular joint action. The rear delt has meaningful moment arms to function as both a primary sagittal extensor and a primary horizontal extensor, while the rotator cuff’s teres minor also has leverage to function as both a secondary sagittal extensor and a secondary horizontal extensor. While they cannot meaningfully contribute to sagittal extension, the rotator cuff’s infraspinatus and supraspinatus can also assist as secondary horizontal extensors, but like the teres minor, their horizontal extension moment arms are not particularly large. Across the joint angles used in a row, the lat and teres major have negligibly small horizontal extension moment arms and are therefore incapable of generating any meaningful magnitude of shoulder horizontal extension torque. However, both of these back muscles are well situated to function as primary shoulder extensors.

During any active movement, the nervous system is most likely to recruit the muscles best suited to produce the joint actions required for efficient performance. Consequently, the biomechanical differences among the aforementioned back muscles can contribute to different row techniques, influencing how likely certain muscles are to be effectively trained. Rowing with a technique that emphasizes sagittal extension of the shoulder will most likely be preferable if you prioritize development of your lats and teres major, but using a technique emphasizing horizontal extension will most likely be better if you prioritize development of your rotator cuff’s infraspinatus and supraspinatus. Primarily due to the lats being the most voluminous back muscle, a shoulder extension-dominant row can effectively train more overall muscle mass than a shoulder horizontal extension-dominant row. Nevertheless, the rotator cuff muscles are larger than many people may realize, so a shoulder horizontal extension row still preferentially targets a sizable amount of muscle. According to two studies, the combined volume of the teres minor, infraspinatus, and supraspinatus has been measured to be 55-67% of the volume of the lats and teres major together. The teres minor and supraspinatus are slightly smaller and larger, respectively, than the fairly small teres major, but the infraspinatus is a sizable muscle along with the rear delt. In fact, the combined volume of these two shoulder horizontal extensor muscles has been measured to be essentially equivalent (ranging from slightly lower to mildly higher) to the volume of the lat. When considering their locations, these muscles are all part of the upper back, except for the lat, which is predominantly located in the middle back. Consequently, a row that emphasizes sagittal extension of the shoulder is likely to be relatively better for developing the middle back in comparison to a row that emphasizes the shoulder horizontal extension, which is likely to be relatively better for developing the upper back.
