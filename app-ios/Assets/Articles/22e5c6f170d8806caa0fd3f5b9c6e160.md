If you wish to optimize your biceps development, curls cannot be beat, but chest fly variations will still train the biceps to a degree, so you are not alone if you feel your biceps working when performing a chest fly. While the lever arm of resistance acting on the shoulder is greater, resistance will also act on the elbow, and your biceps generate elbow flexion torque to maintain the mild degree of elbow bend and prevent your elbow from fully straightening. Through this elbow action, the biceps function statically, but the biceps also have leverage to aid the pec major and front delt in generating shoulder horizontal flexion torque to perform the chest fly motion. Biceps contraction can assist in bringing the hands inward during the lifting phase and control the reversal of this movement during a chest fly. The elbow flexion and shoulder horizontal flexion functions of the biceps explain why Solstad et al. (2020) found the biceps to exhibit high EMG activity when participants performed a flat dumbbell fly, which should engage the biceps similarly to other chest fly variations.

The perception of the biceps being involved during a chest fly can be further amplified by the contribution of another upper arm muscle, namely the coracobrachialis. The coracobrachialis is situated directly deep to the upper half of the short head of the biceps, so contraction of the coracobrachialis can easily be perceived as contraction of the adjacent biceps. This thin upper arm muscle has meaningful leverage to function as a shoulder horizontal flexor, so it should be trained effectively by any pec fly variation that resists shoulder horizontal flexion, along with pressing variations that resist this shoulder motion.
