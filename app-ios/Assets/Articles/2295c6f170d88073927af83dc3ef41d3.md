Most of the work performed during any hamstring curl exercise can be attributed to the thigh muscles discussed in the “hamstring curls develop more than just your hamstrings” passage. However, the medial and lateral gastrocnemius muscles of the calf also assist with knee flexion. These muscles have favorable knee flexion moment arms of a similar magnitude to that of the hamstring’s biceps femoris and contain substantial contractile tissue that allows them to generate high forces. A muscle’s physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA) is a key determinant of its force-producing capacity and is calculated by dividing muscle volume by fiber length. Holzbaur et al. (2007) reported that the combined PCSA of the gastrocnemii is about 90% of the hamstrings’ PCSA, indicating a substantial capacity for force production. When paired with their meaningful knee flexion moment arms, the gastrocnemii contribute notably to total knee flexion torque.

While several studies have examined changes in gastrocnemius size following resisted ankle plantarflexion exercises, no studies to date have directly assessed how hamstring curl training affects gastrocnemius size. As a result, it remains unclear how much hypertrophic stimulus the gastrocnemii receive from resisted knee flexion. However, other lines of evidence provide insight into their role during hamstring curls. Li et al. (2002) demonstrated that electrically stimulated gastrocnemius contractions generate knee flexion torque, and Cadeo et al. (2023) reported moderately high gastrocnemius EMG activity during resisted knee flexion. Li et al. (2002) also showed that both knee and ankle position influence the magnitude of knee flexion torque produced by the gastrocnemii. Torque was greatest with the knee fully extended and declined progressively as the knee flexed, reaching near zero at 115 degrees of knee flexion. These findings suggest that the gastrocnemii function primarily as knee flexors when the knee is minimally flexed, such as at the start of a leg curl rep.

Li et al. (2002) further reported that knee flexion torque was significantly greater when the ankle was dorsiflexed than when it was plantarflexed. This is likely because plantarflexion shortens the gastrocnemii, placing them at a less favorable length for force production, whereas dorsiflexion lengthens them. Knee position has a similar influence on gastrocnemius hypertrophy during calf training, with extended-knee calf raises producing markedly greater growth than bent-knee variations. More broadly, several biarticular muscles exhibit greater hypertrophy when trained at longer muscle lengths. Accordingly, it is reasonable to hypothesize that hamstring curls may train the gastrocnemii more effectively when performed with a dorsiflexed ankle.

The influence of ankle position on gastrocnemius contribution helps explain why multiple studies have found maximal knee flexion torque to be significantly greater in dorsiflexion than plantarflexion. This effect allows people to use heavier loads during hamstring curls when the ankle is dorsiflexed. Some individuals may naturally adopt dorsiflexion during hamstring curls because it is their strongest position. Cadeo et al. (2023) examined whether this strategy compromises hamstring training by assigning trained participants to perform unilateral lying hamstring curls for 10 weeks, with one leg trained in dorsiflexion and the other in plantarflexion. Both conditions produced similar increases in biceps femoris size, the only muscle measured. Both also increased knee flexion strength, but the dorsiflexed condition showed a larger average increase, about 12.2% versus 4.6%, despite the difference not reaching statistical significance. Given the trained status of the participants and the within-subject design, this difference is still noteworthy.

These findings suggest that using a dorsiflexed ankle during hamstring curls does not detract from the hamstring stimulus and may enhance training of other knee flexors, likely the gastrocnemii. Kim et al. (2015) reported similar results in untrained participants who performed hamstring curls and leg extensions for three weeks with either a dorsiflexed or plantarflexed ankle. The dorsiflexed group experienced a 45% greater increase in knee flexion strength. Given the short duration of the intervention, these gains were likely driven by neural adaptations, but the most plausible explanation is greater gastrocnemius involvement in the dorsiflexed condition.

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that performing hamstring curls with a dorsiflexed ankle likely enhances gastrocnemius involvement without reducing the stimulus to the hamstrings. While future research may clarify the hypertrophic effects on the gastrocnemii, current evidence indicates no meaningful adverse effects on dorsiflexion. That said, hamstring curls should not be considered a replacement for extended-knee calf raise variations if calf development is a priority, as the gastrocnemii remain better suited to function as ankle plantarflexors than as knee flexors.
