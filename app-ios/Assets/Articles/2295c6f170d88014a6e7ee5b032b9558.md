As a fun (or morbid) fact, upright row maximal power has been reported by Araujo et al. (2019) to correlate significantly with all-cause mortality risk in a study of 3,827 participants aged 41-85 years old. The quartile of participants with the highest upright row power had only a slightly higher risk of death than the quartile immediately below it throughout the follow-up period (median 6.5 years). However, half of the participants with the lowest upright row power had a considerably greater risk of dying, especially the least powerful quartile of participants, who had a 10-13 times greater risk of dying from any cause compared to the two quartiles with greater than average upright row power. These findings do not mean that improving your upright row performance will directly reduce your risk of mortality, but they indicate that upright row power is associated with overall health. Such a relationship is not unique to the upright row, as other studies have found bench press, leg press, hip flexor, and grip (which is especially well researched) strength to correlate significantly with mortality risk. It should not be surprising that people who have above-average strength or power in various movements tend to be physically healthier than weaker people. Upright rows may not be the fountain of youth, but including some form of resistance training (which may or may not involve upright rows) as part of your lifestyle has the potential to make a meaningful difference to your overall health.
