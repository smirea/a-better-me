*Key takeaway: Overhead pressing with your elbows flared out may potentially bias the lateral deltoid to a greater extent than pressing with your elbows tucked in, which may involve a greater contribution from your upper pec major.*

When performing an overhead pressing exercise, your shoulders can move through one of two planes of motion or a combination of the two planes, which often occurs. One of these planes of motion is the frontal plane, which divides your body into front and back halves and allows for shoulder abduction and adduction with the elbows fully flared out away from the sides. With a dumbbell overhead press using fully flared elbows that results in your upper arms forming a “T-shape” with your torso near the bottom of the rep, your shoulders can abduct during the lifting phase and adduct during the lowering phase. Shoulder flexion can also be trained with single-joint exercises such as front raise variations. Shoulder abduction can also be trained with single-joint exercises such as lateral raise variations. The other plane of motion is the sagittal plane, which divides your body into right and left halves and allows for shoulder flexion and extension with the elbows tucked close to your sides. With a neutral grip dumbbell overhead press using fully tucked elbows that result in your elbows pointing straight ahead when your upper arms are parallel to the ground near the bottom of the rep, your shoulders can flex during the lifting phase and extend during the lowering phase. Shoulder flexion can similarly be trained with single-joint exercises like front raise variations.

Depending on the variation you are training, you can intentionally move your shoulders exclusively through the frontal plane or the sagittal plane, but people commonly utilize a hybrid shoulder motion that occurs in between these two planes. If your elbows are directed at an angle equally between either of the pure planes of motion, such that they are pointing diagonally outward and forward, you can train symmetrically between the frontal and sagittal planes. As your elbows flare further out to your sides, the press involves a greater proportion of frontal plane motion, while it involves a greater proportion of sagittal plane movement as your elbows tuck in closer to pointing straight ahead.

Dumbbells allow you full freedom to move in whichever plane you wish, but the grip you use for a barbell or machine overhead press variation will constrain you to a particular type of shoulder motion while keeping the wrists stacked over the elbows for stability. For instance, using a neutral shoulder width grip for a machine overhead press will typically result in you emphasizing sagittal plane shoulder motion to a greater degree than a wider overhand grip, which facilitates a more frontal plane dominant shoulder motion. Similarly, a behind-the-neck overhead press with a barbell compels you to move your shoulders almost entirely through the frontal plane, while pressing to the front of the body during a standard barbell overhead press allows you to move your shoulders in between the two planes.

The muscles that act on the shoulder have different leverages to function as flexors in the sagittal plane or abductors in the frontal plane, so the manner in which lifters press overhead may influence how much of a contribution to the press different muscles have. Currently, no study has yet measured changes in the size of any shoulder muscles after participants completed an overhead pressing intervention. Consequently, we must draw on other forms of indirect evidence to determine how the overhead pressing technique influences hypertrophy across different muscles.

When performing a particular joint action, the nervous system is most likely to recruit the muscles that are best leveraged to produce that joint action. Nearly every muscle studied to increase in size significantly after a resistance training intervention has been found to have meaningful moment arms (which quantify leverage) for the joint actions, notably those loaded by the exercises in the training interventions. Correspondingly, overhead pressing predominantly in the frontal plane will likely develop muscles with large moment arms to function as shoulder abductors. On the other hand, overhead pressing predominantly in the sagittal plane will likely develop the muscles that have large moment arms to function as shoulder flexors.

The front deltoid and lateral deltoid can function both as shoulder flexors and abductors, but the front deltoid is relatively better suited to function as a shoulder flexor, while the lateral deltoid is relatively better suited to function as an abductor. Similarly, the rotator cuff’s supraspinatus can also contribute to both shoulder actions, but it has greater leverage to function as an abductor than a flexor. On the other hand, the clavicular head of the pectoralis major that constitutes the upper chest has notable leverage to flex the shoulder, but its leverage to function as a shoulder abductor is quite small in magnitude. In light of these differences, overhead predominantly in the frontal plane with your elbows flared out may train the lateral deltoid and supraspinatus more effectively compared to if you primarily moved your shoulders through the sagittal plane. However, overhead pressing predominantly in the sagittal plane with your elbows tucked has the potential to train your front deltoid and upper pec major to a preferentially greater degree based on the moment arms of the shoulder muscles.

In addition to assessing the extent to which the muscles of the shoulder lever arm contribute to different shoulder actions, we can draw on studies that measure EMG activity in these muscles during shoulder exercises. Coratella et al. (2022) assessed participants' EMG activity during overhead presses behind the neck (exclusively involving frontal-plane shoulder motion) and during presses to the front of the body with a barbell (involving a hybrid shoulder motion). The participants also performed machine overhead pressing with a wide overhead grip, emphasizing shoulder abduction, along with a closer neutral grip involving relatively more shoulder flexion. The researchers reported that the lateral deltoid exhibited greater EMG activity with variations involving greater frontal-plane shoulder motion. On the other hand, the upper pec major exhibited greater EMG activity during the variations involving relatively greater sagittal plane shoulder motion. In contrast, the front deltoid, upper trapezius, and triceps exhibited similar EMG activity regardless of the shoulder motion. The differences in EMG activity of the lateral deltoid and upper pec major are congruent with what could be predicted based on the aforementioned moment arms of the muscles. The absence of an effect for the front deltoid was accompanied by very high activity in every variation, so it may be close to being fully active regardless of the plane of motion in which someone overhead presses. Throughout the joint angles present when overhead pressing, the front deltoid may have a greater contribution to shoulder abduction than the lateral deltoid has for shoulder flexion, so the lateral deltoid may be more sensitive to changes in the shoulder’s plane of motion.

Muscle moment arm and EMG activity data have their limitations, so the degree to which your overhead pressing technique can influence which muscles are most likely to grow is still speculative in nature. While not definitively proven, the front deltoid is likely to be effectively trained when performing overhead pressing with the shoulders in the frontal plane, the sagittal plane, or between these two planes. However, it would be sensible for you to emphasize frontal plane movement if you wish to develop the lateral deltoid and supraspinatus through overhead pressing, while emphasizing sagittal plane motion may be preferable if you wish to develop your upper pec major to a greater degree. With that said, moderate or low incline bench press variations will likely train the upper pec major more effectively than any overhead press, and lateral raise variations may still be the gold standard for training the lateral deltoid and supraspinatus.
