Exercises like bench press, machine chest press, and fly variations can train the pec major primarily through the shoulder horizontal flexion function that each of its regions exhibits. Shoulder horizontal flexion occurs in its purest form when the arms move inward, forming 90-degree angles with the torso throughout a movement such as a flat dumbbell fly. On the other hand, dip variations performed with the “chest dip” technique, using moderately flared elbows, train a combination of shoulder flexion and adduction. In addition to occurring when raising the arm straight out in front of the body, like during a front raise, shoulder flexion occurs when moving your elbows forward if they start behind your body, like during a dip. If your arms are positioned straight out away from your sides, shoulder adduction is the motion that pulls them down toward your sides. When performing a “chest dip” with your elbows flared out, shoulder adduction pulls your elbows in toward the midline of your body.

The sternocostal portion of the pec major that constitutes your middle and lower chest functions as a shoulder adductor, while the clavicular portion that forms your upper chest functions as a shoulder flexor. These functions allow “chest dip” variations to train the pec major meaningfully even though they look very different from other types of chest exercises.

In contrast to “chest dips,” “triceps dips” are performed with the elbows tucked closely to the sides, thereby effectively training shoulder flexion (back-to-front motion) while minimizing shoulder adduction (outward-to-inward motion) demands. Currently, no research has investigated how different dip techniques may affect activity among the different pec major regions. However, the upper pec major will most likely serve a greater role during a “triceps dips” than the middle and lower pec major since the middle and lower regions do not meaningfully contribute to shoulder flexion. While “triceps dips” with fully tucked elbows may train the upper pec major, they most likely largely neglect the bulk of the chest musculature since the middle and lower regions have been measured to constitute about four fifths of total pec major muscle volume.
