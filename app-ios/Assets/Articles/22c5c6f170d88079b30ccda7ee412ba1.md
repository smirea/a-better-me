While assessing a shrug variation (that was performed unilaterally in a standing position for 3 reps with free weight resistance loaded to a 5-RM), Ekstrom et al. (2003) found EMG activity to be very high in the upper trapezius, moderate in the middle trapezius, and low in the lower trapezius. This very high upper trapezius activity during a shrug is congruent with the upper trapezius elevating the shoulder girdle by drawing the lateral aspect of the clavicle superomedially. Jakobsen et al. (2012) similarly found that the upper trap exhibited the greatest EMG activity of any trap region during standing dumbbell shrugs (performed for 15 reps with a 15-RM load), while the lower trap exhibited the least EMG activity of the three regions.

Moseley et al. (1992) also found the levator scapulae to exhibit very high peak EMG activity during a shrug variation (that was performed bilaterally for 10 reps in a seated position with “light weight” dumbbells using loads self-selected by participants), which is congruent with the ability of this muscle to elevate the shoulder girdle through scapular elevation. Moseley et al. (1992) also assessed EMG activity across all three trapezius regions during the shrug variation, along with 15 other exercises. For each muscle, they reported EMG findings from exercises they considered “optimal” based on the duration and magnitude of EMG activity, but they did not identify the shrug as “optimal” for any trapezius region. Correspondingly, Moseley et al. (1992) did not report specific EMG values of any trapezius region while performing shrugs, indicating that no trapezius region exhibited at least moderately high EMG activity for what was considered a meaningful duration of each shrug rep. The absence of high EMG activity in the upper trapezius may have been due to participants performing a set of shrugs at a relative intensity insufficient to optimize agonist muscle peak activity, in contrast to Ekstrom et al. (2003), who explicitly assessed a set of shrugs with only 2 reps in reserve. If the shrug was performed with a load and/or rep-scheme that brought the set closer to volitional failure, Moseley et al. (1992) may have measured higher upper trapezius EMG activity.

While the anatomy of the upper trapezius and levator scapulae warrants their functional classification as shoulder girdle elevators, the limited research assessing changes in the size of these muscles after a training intervention does not indicate that shrugs are effective for inducing hypertrophy of these muscles. Conley et al. (1997) used MRI to measure the cross-sectional area of the neck musculature at nine different sites (involving 13 total muscles, including the trapezius and levator scapulae) before and after recreationally active participants completed a 12-week resistance training intervention. Each week, the intervention involved four full body training sessions, with each exercise performed for 3-5 sets of 3-10 reps. The exercises included squats, push presses, bench presses, mid-thigh pulls (i.e., above the knee rack pulls), Romanian deadlifts, crunches, bent over rows, and shrugs. Following the intervention, the cross sectional area did not change at the sites corresponding to the upper trapezius and levator scapulae. Especially since the participants reportedly were not well-trained lifters (who would most likely have lower rates of muscle growth than recreationally active individuals), one would expect the upper trapezius and levator scapulae to grow if they functioned as prime movers while shrugging. While not as directly as shrugs, mid-thigh pulls, and Romanian deadlifts would also be expected to train muscles that function as shoulder girdle elevators in an isometric fashion.

When our anatomical understanding of muscles indicates that they are well suited to perform a function meaningfully resisted by an exercise, we cannot necessarily conclude from a single training study that that exercise is incapable of effectively training those muscles. This is especially the case when EMG research supports high muscle activity during that exercise. While anecdotes are not at the level of empirical evidence derived from well controlled experiments, they still can function as a form of evidence while being recognized for their clear limitations. In isolation, countless lifters use shrug variations to train their upper trapezius and report that this muscle is meaningfully working, while shrugging does not provide irrefutable evidence that it trains the upper trapezius very effectively; however, this phenomenon is not meaningless either. Ultimately, the findings from Conley et al. (1997) provide notable evidence against the claim that shrugs effectively train the upper trapezius and levator scapulae, but this single study does not preclude classifying these muscles as prime movers during shrug variations.
