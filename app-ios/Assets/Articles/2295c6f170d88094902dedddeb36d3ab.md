Based on the available evidence, ten different muscles have meaningful moment arms, which quantify leverage for generating torque, that allow them to function as hip flexors. Seven of these muscles have sufficiently large moment arms to warrant classification as primary hip flexors in at least certain positions. Three of the muscles have smaller moment arms that allow them to assist in a secondary hip flexion role, and they are likely to be trained more effectively by exercises that resist their primary functions rather than hip flexion.

Except for the psoas major, iliacus (which together constitute the iliopsoas muscle group), and the pectineus, each of the other hip flexor muscles has primary functions beyond hip flexion. Four of the hip flexor muscles (adductor longus, adductor brevis, gracilis, and pectineus) are part of the hip adductor muscle group and can all be trained with resisted hip adduction exercises, such as seated machine hip adduction; however, the pectineus contributes less to hip adduction than the other three muscles. A combination of leg curls and single-joint knee extension exercises, such as machine leg extensions, can train the primary functions of two additional hip flexor muscles, the rectus femoris and sartorius, along with one hip flexor that is also a hip adductor, the gracilis. If you wish to train two additional hip flexor muscles, you can perform hip abduction and hip external rotation exercises to train the tensor fasciae latae (TFL) and obturator externus, respectively, although these are the smallest hip flexors and contribute the least to total lower body muscle size.

Given the multiple functions of most hip flexor muscles, it is not strictly necessary to perform resisted hip flexion exercises to train the majority of the hip flexor musculature. Nonetheless, the psoas major and iliacus are relatively large muscles, and together they have been measured to constitute close to one third of the total volume of all ten hip flexor muscles combined. Whether resisted hip flexion exercises are worth including ultimately depends on your individual goals, but incorporating at least one such exercise is sensible if you wish to maximize overall muscularity. In addition to training the iliopsoas, which is largely neglected by other lower body exercises, resisted hip flexion exercises may also help more fully develop hip flexor muscles that are otherwise trained indirectly. The combined volume of the seven primary hip flexor muscles has been measured to be approximately 60% greater than the total volume of the hamstrings, and total hip flexor muscle volume, including secondary hip flexors, has been measured to be roughly twice that of the hamstrings. Consequently, resisted hip flexion exercises are capable of training a substantial proportion of the lower body musculature.
