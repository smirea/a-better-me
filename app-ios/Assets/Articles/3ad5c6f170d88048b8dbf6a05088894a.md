In addition to its padding, the handles of a safety squat bar (often abbreviated as an SSB) visibly distinguish it from a straight barbell. For lifters with pre-existing injuries or mobility limitations who may experience discomfort in their shoulders, elbows, or wrists when back squatting with a straight bar, the ability to grasp a safety squat bar’s handles can help squatting with a safety squat bar feel meaningfully more comfortable than back squatting with a straight bar. If you are one of those lifters and do not have to back squat specifically with a straight bar for a competition like a powerlifting meet, there is little reason to push through pain in your shoulders, elbows, or wrists if you can comfortably squat with a safety squat bar.

While no study has yet compared back squat and safety squat bar squat training interventions in terms of how much muscle growth they may induce, the two variations train the same muscles in similar ways, so they can most likely stimulate comparable degrees of muscle hypertrophy on average when using similar volumes and proximities to failure. With either squat variation, your quads will be heavily loaded to extend your knees while your gluteus maximus and adductor magnus will be heavily loaded to extend your hips. Simultaneously, your spinal extensors must function to keep your spine from flexing under the weight of the bar regardless of which variation you perform. Nonetheless, they do exhibit certain biomechanical differences.

The safety squat bar’s camber pitches the load further forward than a straight bar does with a back squat but to a lesser degree than a front squat does, resulting in a safety squat bar squat mechanically functioning like a hybrid between a back squat and a front squat. Situating the load further forward than a back squat particularly increases the demands on the thoracic erectors to maintain a sufficiently extended thoracic spine and prevent the chest from collapsing through thoracic spine flexion. For this reason, safety squat bar squats and, to a greater degree, front squats will preferentially train the upper back musculature compared to back squats, where the difference is more pronounced with a low bar back squat than a high bar back squat.

This difference in thoracic erector loading is accompanied by a difference in the torso angle when comparing the squat variations, as evidenced by studies reporting that, on average, people squat with a more upright torso when safety squat bar squatting or front squatting compared to back squatting.  Balance is optimized when squatting by keeping the center of total mass (accounting for the mass of the bar and the body) over the midfoot. Relative to a back squat, positioning the bar further forward means a lesser degree of forward torso lean will be required to keep the center of total mass over your midfoot when squatting down. Research has also found that the greater degree of forward torso lean when back squatting is also accompanied by a greater degree of hip flexion. As torso lean increases, the weight’s moment arm acting on the hips increases, which may slightly increase the demands on the hip extensor muscles (e.g., gluteus maximus and adductor magnus) when back squatting compared to safety squat bar squatting. While they are detectable, the differences in hip angles and hip extensor loading are of small enough magnitudes that they are unlikely to contribute to a significant difference in how much hip extensor muscle growth may be stimulated by safety squat bar squats and back squats.

When testing lifters’ 1-RM or 3-RM with the two bars, research has consistently shown that people, on average, can squat more weight when back squatting compared to safety squat bar squatting. As of writing this passage, five different studies have investigated this topic, and they all reported that their participants lifted greater max loads when back squatting. If we average their findings together, they collectively indicated that the difference in max load was about 9%.  

Ultimately, if you prefer squatting with one bar compared to the other, it is fully viable to select whichever squat variation feels best to you when training for muscle hypertrophy and/or general squatting strength. Back squats and safety squat bar squats do not have identical mechanics, but they are far more similar than different, and both can be high-quality exercises in your programs.
