You can use one of three arm positions when performing wrist roller wrist curls, each of which is described as its own variation in this database. In addition to holding your arms out in front of you with straight elbows, which places the shoulders at about 90 degrees of flexion, you can keep your upper arms by your sides with your elbows bent to about 90 degrees, or you can keep your arms slightly in front of you and directed toward the ground with straight elbows. None of the wrist flexor muscles cross the shoulder joint, so shoulder position does not directly affect them. Some wrist flexor muscles slightly cross the elbow joint and may be marginally more lengthened with straight elbows than with bent elbows, but elbow position also does not meaningfully affect wrist flexor function.

However, holding your arms outstretched parallel to the floor requires your shoulder flexor muscles, such as the front deltoid and upper pectoralis major, to act isometrically to prevent your arms from lowering. Similarly, keeping your elbows bent requires your elbow flexor muscles, such as the biceps, brachialis, and brachioradialis, to act isometrically to prevent elbow extension during the exercise. In contrast, keeping your arms directed down toward the ground places minimal demand on the shoulder or elbow flexor muscles due to the position of the weight relative to those joints.

When using one of the two variations in which the weight is held farther in front of your body, more muscle groups are recruited than when it is kept close to your body, which has potential advantages and disadvantages. If your shoulder or elbow flexor muscles must act isometrically to maintain arm position, they will receive some stimulus for strength and size adaptations alongside the wrist flexors. However, there is limited evidence on the effectiveness of mid-range isometric training for developing these muscles compared with dynamic training through a full range of motion. Rasch and Morehouse (1957) reported that six weeks of dynamic curls led to greater increases in upper arm circumference than mid-range isometric curls. That said, arm circumference is not a direct measure of muscle size, and these findings should be interpreted cautiously, especially given the study's age.

The added isometric demands placed on the shoulder or elbow flexors when holding the wrist roller farther from the body may also come at the expense of wrist flexor training. If your sets are limited by shoulder or elbow fatigue rather than by the ability of your wrist flexors to lift the weight, the wrist flexors may receive a suboptimal stimulus. Conversely, if your wrist flexors remain the limiting factor when using the bent-elbow or outstretched-arm variations, the additional isometric work for the shoulder or elbow flexors is unlikely to impair wrist flexor training.
Of the three positions, the outstretched-arm variation is the most likely to shift the limiting factor away from the wrist flexors. In this position, the weight has a much longer lever arm acting on the shoulder than on the wrist, and this shoulder lever arm is substantially longer than the elbow lever arm involved in the bent-elbow variation. As a result, the shoulder flexors must generate considerably more torque to maintain position. Although Bober et al. (2002) found average maximal shoulder flexion torque at 90 degrees to be slightly lower than maximal elbow flexion torque at 90 degrees, individual strength ratios vary widely. Consequently, the likelihood that shoulder flexion strength limits performance is generally higher with outstretched arms than the likelihood that elbow flexion strength limits performance with bent elbows, though this will differ between individuals.

A simple way to assess whether your shoulder or elbow muscles are limiting your wrist roller performance is to immediately perform additional reps with your arms directed down toward the ground after completing a set with one of the other positions. If you can perform more reps with the weight closer to your body, it suggests that your forearm muscles were not the limiting factor during the previous variation. Overall, the key takeaway is that arm position during wrist roller wrist curls influences which muscle groups are trained most effectively. If your goal is to maximize forearm development, it is best to use a variation in which the wrist flexors, rather than other muscle groups, limit the set.
