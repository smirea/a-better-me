If you have minimal experience training hip hinge exercises such as Romanian deadlift and good morning variations, it is normal to have difficulty performing this movement pattern. Like riding a bicycle or shooting a basketball, hip hinging is a motor skill that may require repeated practice before you feel comfortable consistently using it. Broadly speaking, there are two types of movement substitution patterns that people commonly perform when learning how to hip hinge.

One of these is a stoop lift, which involves dynamically rounding the back through flexion of the lumbar and thoracic spine to bend over before uncoiling the spine into an upright position during the ascent. This is often accompanied by the buttocks not moving particularly far backward and the arms drifting out in front of the legs. In contrast, a strict hip hinge uses a visibly flat back position with a near neutral lumbar spine and involves the buttocks moving noticeably backward during the descent. In terms of similarity, a stoop lift and a hip hinge both involve only a mild degree of knee bend during the descent while keeping the hips high. A clue that you may be performing more of a stoop lift than a hip hinge is feeling a stretching sensation in your back rather than feeling your back muscles remain statically tight throughout the set as they work to maintain a rigid trunk position while you move about the hips. With a properly performed hip hinge, you should feel a stretching sensation in your hamstrings at the bottom of the lift. You may also feel this stretching sensation during a stoop lift. However, a hip hinge often allows you to feel the hamstring stretch earlier during the descent because the hamstrings are shortened by the posterior pelvic tilt that accompanies lumbar spine flexion.

In addition to unintentionally performing a stoop lift, people commonly use a more squatting motion when attempting to hip hinge. This pattern is characterized by greater knee bend and forward tracking of the knees during the descent. Compared to a hip hinge, a squat pattern causes the buttocks to move downward rather than backward, and the shins to incline forward rather than remain vertical. Because the hamstrings shorten as the knees bend, someone using a squat pattern during a hip hinge exercise is unlikely to feel much of a stretching sensation in the hamstrings at the bottom of the lift. Not feeling a hamstring stretch is the most straightforward indicator that you may be squatting your hip hinge exercise. Another clue is perceiving the quads to be working more intensely than the hamstrings, such as feeling a burning sensation or substantial tension.

Hip hinge, stoop lift, and squat patterns exist along a spectrum. You can perform a hip hinge exercise that is mostly a hinge while still incorporating elements of a stoop lift or squat that may not be ideal for your goal. Likewise, your technique can gradually progress from being stoop lift dominant or squat dominant to becoming progressively more hinge dominant as your body learns the movement through repeated practice. If your hip hinge technique has significant room for improvement, it can be helpful to start with lighter loads that are not especially challenging until you develop a reasonable level of competency with the movement. You do not need to wait for immaculate technique before loading the movement meaningfully, but it would also be inadvisable to push your body to its limits without first learning the fundamentals.

Depending on which aspect of hip hinging you currently struggle with, certain external cues can help accelerate learning. If you unintentionally squat or stoop lift, using a wall can provide tactile feedback to cue sitting the hips backward. Perform the hip hinge with your back facing a nearby wall and aim to make contact with the wall using your buttocks at the end of the descent. As an initial starting point, you can set up in a heel to toe tandem stance with the back heel touching the wall, then bring your rear foot forward to align with your front foot in the stance you plan to use for the exercise. This provides a reasonable approximation of how far most people sit their hips back, and you can adjust your distance from the wall as needed.

If you tend to perform a squatty hip hinge, practicing with your calves in contact with a flat exercise bench, couch, or chair can be effective. As you descend, you will receive immediate tactile feedback if your knees track forward because your calves will lose contact. This helps reinforce keeping your shins vertical. When using this method, maintain full contact between your feet and the ground and avoid leaning your bodyweight into the bench. If you keep your calves in contact and feel a pronounced hamstring stretch at the bottom, you are likely performing a high quality hip hinge.

As an additional learning tool, you can place a long object such as a dowel rod, PVC pipe, or yardstick along your back while hip hinging if you tend to use a stoop lift technique with excessive back rounding. Either the calf-to-bench technique or the buttock-to-wall technique can be incorporated into a Romanian deadlift performed with weights, but using a rod as a cue without a partner requires you to hold the rod in place, which means you cannot load the drill independently.

To perform this drill, hold the rod against your back with one hand higher near your head and the other hand lower near your buttocks. While standing fully upright and looking straight ahead, you should feel the rod contacting your sacrum (the bone between your upper buttock cheeks and just above your tailbone), your upper back between your shoulder blades, and the back of your head. You will then hip hinge while maintaining all three points of contact throughout the movement. If you begin rounding your back or moving your neck beyond the neutral position you adopt while standing upright, the rod will lose contact with one of these points.

This provides immediate feedback and can help develop your ability to move primarily through your lower body while keeping your trunk rigid, which can transfer to improved technique across many exercises. When maintaining these three contact points, it is still possible to perform a squatting motion rather than a true hip hinge, since minimizing back motion is also a component of squatting technique. However, if you maintain all three contact points and also feel a pronounced stretch in your hamstrings at the bottom of the descent, those two factors together strongly indicate that you have successfully hip hinged. If the rod cue shifts your movement from a stoop lift toward a squat, you can combine the rod cue with the calf-to-bench cue to address multiple aspects of the hip hinge at the same time.

In the context of performing a hip hinge exercise, using a technique that more closely resembles a squat or a stoop lift can be considered a technique error. That said, squatting and stoop lifting are not inherently faulty movement patterns. They simply differ from a hip hinge and can be suboptimal ways to perform an exercise when your goal is to train specifically for the benefits that hip hinging provides. The hip hinge can take time to learn, but investing the effort to develop this motor skill can meaningfully expand how you move both inside and outside the gym.
