Pause variations can be performed with the pause at any point in the lift’s range of motion, so where the pause is utilized can be individualized to address someone’s specific weakness or technical deficiencies. While you can certainly try pausing at different points in the rep, pausing near the start of the lifting phase (when the working muscles are at their longest lengths) can generally be an effective approach. During the pause portion, muscles act isometrically (statically), without changing their length, in contrast to concentrically shortening during the lifting phase or eccentrically lengthening during the lowering phase. Compared to performing isometric exercise at shorter muscle lengths, training isometrically at longer lengths may contribute to greater muscle hypertrophy and improved strength across a wider portion of the lift. Isometrically training at long muscle lengths can transfer to better performance at short lengths, while isometrically training at short lengths is comparatively more limited in its ability to improve strength at long muscle lengths.

Along with having greater direct transfer to strength throughout the range of motion, pausing near the start of the lifting phase can often improve your positioning during subsequent parts of the lifting phase. If you begin lifting in a mechanically advantageous position, it may be easier to complete the rep than if you start with suboptimal positioning. For instance, if your hips shoot up prematurely when squatting out of the hole, you can be pitched forward excessively and have a greater difficulty with the rest of your squat. Even if you consistently fail midway through the lifting phase, starting your ascent with optimal positioning can increase the load you can handle before reaching your limit at that point.

Increasing strength near the start of the lifting phase can also make it easier to complete the initial portion of the rep, which may build greater speed and reduce difficulty in subsequent parts of the lift. If you have greater momentum entering into a later portion of the rep, you can be less likely to fail compared to if the bar was moving quite slowly into that point from having to grind to begin the lifting phase. To have improved starting strength effectively transfer to increased bar speed, you just want to make sure that you train the exercise without the pause as well, because exclusively training the pause variation can neglect to optimize the technique you use when not pausing.
