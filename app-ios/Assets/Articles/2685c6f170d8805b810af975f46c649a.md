*Key takeaway: High step-ups likely elicit greater muscle growth and strength gains than low step-ups across a wider range of motion; therefore, it is recommended to progress to high step-ups before moving on to weighted variations.*

Step-up exercises (including step-down variations) can be performed at various step heights, which can affect the joint range of motion used and the stimulus provided to the lower body muscles. Currently, no study has yet investigated how training step-ups through different ranges of motion may influence muscle hypertrophy. However, research on squatting can provide insight into how step-up range of motion most likely affects muscle growth because both types of movements primarily train hip and knee extension.

Kubo et al. (2019) assessed changes in lower body muscle size after participants completed a half squat intervention that involved reaching 90 degrees of knee flexion at end range or a full squat intervention that involved reaching 140 degrees of knee flexion at end range. The participants trained barbell back squats twice per week over the span of 10 weeks, and each session involved 3 sets of 8 reps, except for the initial two sessions. Both groups experienced significant increases in quad muscle volume of essentially the same magnitude, and neither group significantly increased the size of their hamstrings (which aligns with other studies finding that squats are ineffective for developing the hamstrings). However, the researchers found that the full-squat group experienced approximately three times the gluteus maximus growth and slightly more than twice the growth of the hip adductor muscles (which can function as hip extensors like the gluteus maximus) on the inner thigh relative to the half squat group. The hip adductor muscles that Kubo and colleagues specifically assessed were the adductor magnus, adductor longus, and adductor brevis, and the volume of these muscles added to the volume of the gluteus maximus is similar to the volume of the quads based on data from Handsfield et al. (2014). Correspondingly, full squats stimulated a meaningfully greater increase in total lower body muscle size than half squats.

Although not directly tested, the range of motion for step-up variations is likely similar to that of other muscle development tasks. Consequently, high step-ups that position you into a highly flexed hip and knee position are likely superior to low step-ups involving shallower hip and knee angles if your objective is to optimize lower body muscle growth. Furthermore, high step-ups are almost certainly preferable to low step-ups if you wish to increase strength from a position of high hip and knee flexion, since strength gains exhibit joint angle specificity to a notable degree. Kubo et al. (2019) unsurprisingly found that training full squats transferred to increases in half squat 1-RM to a greater degree than training half squats transferred to increases in full squat 1-RM. If you wish your step-ups to transfer to enhanced lower body strength when squatting, your step-ups ideally should include the deepest hip and knee angles present when you squat.

In light of the available evidence, most people who train step-up variations would benefit from progressing toward being able to perform high step-ups with quality technique. Nonetheless, training lower height step-ups with an emphasis on proper technique and controlling the descent can be appropriate for many people who are new to step-ups. This can be a more prudent approach than simply starting with a high step-up and using sloppy technique to move from point A to point B through whatever means necessary. Over time, you can incrementally increase the height of your step-ups until you can perform high step-ups with quality technique. Since training through deep ranges of motion can confer distinct benefits, progressing the range of motion with bodyweight before increasing the load via weighted variations can be a prudent strategy for many lifters. Like with half squats, adding heavier weights to lower step-ups can serve a sport-specific role among athletes (such as track athletes) who wish to focus on increasing force production in shallower joint angles. However, training to maximize performance in specific athletic endeavors is distinct from training to enhance muscularity and overall strength.

Given the benefits that training high step-ups can provide, the “muscles trained at long lengths” classification for most step-up variations specifically refers to high step-ups that begin with hip and knee flexion similar to that achieved at the bottom of a full squat. With a low step-up variation, you naturally will not be training any particular muscles at long lengths.
