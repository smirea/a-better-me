Optimizing squat efficiency involves keeping your whole center of mass with the combined load of your bodyweight and the bar weight over your midfoot to maximize your stability. As you squat down, a greater proportion of your body’s mass shifts behind your midfoot due to the mass that is concentrated in your torso, hips, and upper thighs. To keep your whole center of mass over your midfoot when a bar is on your back, the bar may have to track forward to a degree as you descend before tracking back to a degree as you ascend. When the bar’s weight is relatively light relative to your body’s weight, a visible amount of forward and backward bar motion may occur (when viewed from the side) to keep the whole center of mass aligned with your midfoot. As the bar weight progressively increases, the optimal bar path becomes correspondingly closer to fully vertical.

Recognizing that a non-zero degree of forward bar motion can be appropriate when descending, some people squat with an excessive degree of horizontal motion, which may negatively affect squat performance. Due to its ability to oscillate forward and backward, a cambered bar provides immediate feedback about whether your bar path is likely deviating beyond vertical to an excessive degree. If the bar starts swinging, the rep can become more challenging to complete, so it incentivizes you to use a closer to vertical bar path.
