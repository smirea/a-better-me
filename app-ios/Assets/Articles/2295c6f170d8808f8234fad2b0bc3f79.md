When lunging, you can use a range of step lengths while keeping your rear knee in contact with the ground, maintaining the entire sole of your front foot on the ground. At the short end of this step range, you will be able to drive your front knee farther forward relative to your foot, which will result in your shin being inclined forward. With a long step, you can keep your front shin vertical while keeping the knee stacked above the ankle, which requires less knee flexion (bending) range of motion. A longer step may also allow the thigh of the rear leg to be angled further backward (via greater hip extension) than with a shorter step, which keeps the thigh more vertical. Because the rear knee can initially be positioned higher off the floor when the thigh is angled backward, a long step allows the hip to flex slightly more during the descent until the rear knee contacts the floor.

These differences can enable a lifter to train the quads in a slightly more extended position during lunges with a short step, since the quads are lengthened at increasing angles of knee flexion. On the other hand, a longer step may allow someone to train the gluteus maximus and adductor magnus at somewhat longer muscle lengths, since these primary hip extensor muscles are lengthened as hip flexion increases. This is potentially relevant because the available evidence indicates that the peak length at which a muscle is trained during a particular exercise likely influences the extent of growth the exercise may stimulate. Plenty of muscles have yet to be studied in terms of how their growth may be affected by the lengths in which they are loaded during exercise, but several studies have investigated this topic for the quads. The findings of five of these studies align with each other in the sense that training the quads at moderately long peak lengths (involving up to 90-120 degrees of knee flexion) stimulates greater quad growth than training the quads at moderately short peak lengths (involving up to 50-65 degrees of knee flexion). With at least four separate studies (involving squat interventions, machine leg extension interventions, and a multi-exercise intervention that included lunges) reporting the same outcome, there is fairly strong justification to perform lunges until the rear knee gently taps the ground rather than training through a meaningfully more shallow range of motion. However, the quads can already be trained at moderately long lengths when lunging with a long step, and using a short step changes that only by a fairly small amount. This was demonstrated by Escamalla et al. (2025), who reported peak knee flexion angles of approximately 90-100 degrees when participants lunged with a long step until their rear knee contacted the ground, whereas they reached about 100-110 degrees with a short step.

Up to a point, training the quads with longer peak lengths than shorter ones is likely to promote greater hypertrophy, but this effect may have a ceiling effect. Kubo et al. (2019) found similar quadriceps growth after participants performed a half-squat intervention with 90 degrees of peak knee flexion or a full-squat intervention with 140 degrees of knee flexion. Larsen et al. (2025) also found similar quadriceps growth whether leg pressing was performed to 100 degrees of knee flexion or to the end range of knee flexion for each participant (154 degrees on average). While new evidence may emerge in the future indicating otherwise, these findings suggest that training to very high angles of knee flexion to lengthen the quadriceps is not necessarily a superior means of developing the quadriceps than training to approximately 90-100 degrees of knee flexion. Furthermore, the previously mentioned study by Escamalla et al. (2025) found that EMG activity in the quadriceps was generally greater when using a long step than when using a short step. This does not mean that lunging with a long step is necessarily more effective for training the quads than lunging with a short step, but the preponderance of evidence suggests that using a short step is unlikely to train the quads better than lunging with a long step.

On the other hand, Kubo et al. (2019) found that the aforementioned full-squat group experienced approximately three times as much gluteus maximus growth and slightly more than twice the growth of the hip adductor muscle (the primary hip extensor, adductor magnus) relative to the half-squat group. With a compound exercise that effectively trains knee and hip extension simultaneously (e.g., lunge and squat variations), training at high angles of hip flexion may increase the hypertrophic stimulus to the hip extensors relative to reaching only a moderately high degree of hip flexion. The findings from one squat study do not guarantee that the same relationship will hold for lunges, but it is certainly plausible that lunges will show a similar relationship. While there is no legitimate reason to think that lunging with a long step would train the hip extensor muscles less effectively than lunging with a short step, the difference in hip range of motion between step lengths may be of a magnitude low enough to be inconsequential for many people. The directionality may be similar, but lunging with a long step rather than a short step increases hip range of motion to a lesser degree than full squatting rather than half squatting. In isolation, the small effect that lunge step length has on the length of hip extensor muscles may limit the degree to which step length influences hip extensor muscle growth. Still, step length is not the only variable you can modify in an effort to amplify the stimulus you provide to these muscles.

Just as moderately different step lengths can be used when lunging, so too can somewhat different trunk positions be utilized when performing lunge variations. On one end of the spectrum, you can maintain a fully upright trunk as your torso faces straight ahead throughout your lunge, as it would when normally standing upright. Alternatively, you can incline your trunk forward by hinging at the hip of your front leg, which results in your torso facing at an angle between forward and downward. It should be noted that this forward trunk lean occurs from hip motion as you maintain the natural curvature of your lower back, rather than spinal motion that can occur from simply rounding your lower back. Relative to lunging with an upright trunk position, lunging with an inclined trunk can allow you to reach a greater angle of hip flexion for the front leg, which will allow you to train your hip extensor muscles (e.g., gluteus maximus and adductor magnus) at longer muscle lengths. When you use this forward trunk lean simultaneously with a long step, you may be able to achieve a significantly greater angle of hip flexion (e.g., approximately 40 degrees more) than would otherwise be possible with a short step and an upright trunk position. The discrepancy between these two lunge approaches could correspondingly be comparable to the difference between half squats and full squats in terms of the peak lengths at which the gluteus maximus and adductor magnus are trained. In addition to training duration, trunk position during lunging can affect the resistance imposed on the hip extensor muscles. Relative to an upright trunk, an inclined trunk shifts your upper body’s center of mass forward along with any weights you may be using. Doing so increases the lever arm through which resistance acts on your front leg’s hip joint while simultaneously decreasing the lever arm through which resistance acts on your front leg’s knee joint. Consequently, your front leg’s hip extensor muscles will be loaded to a relatively greater degree when lunging with an inclined trunk, while your front leg’s quads will receive comparatively greater loading when lunging with an upright trunk. While not directly proven, the combination of subjecting your hip extensor muscles to relatively greater resistance while they are trained at longer muscle lengths can likely contribute to lunges with a long step and forward trunk lean being more effective for developing your gluteus maximus and adductor magnus than lunging with a short step and upright trunk. On the other hand, keeping your trunk upright while using whichever step length feels most comfortable may be somewhat more effective if you prioritize quadriceps development.
